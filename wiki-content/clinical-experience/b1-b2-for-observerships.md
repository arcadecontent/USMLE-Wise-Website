A B-1/B-2 visitor visa can be used for some US observerships, but only for observing. The State Department's own guidance supports a visit by a medical doctor to observe US medical practices when no payment comes from a US source and no patient care is involved. It does not support hands-on clinical work, and it treats graduates and current students differently. This page explains the line, what hosts usually ask for, and where the risks are.

> [!CHANGED] November 2026: The Foreign Affairs Manual section on clerkships under B-1 (9 FAM 402.2-5(E)(4)) carries a revision date of December 2025 in the State Department's online text. We could not identify exactly what changed, so check the current wording yourself at the link in the sources. Visitor visa processing has also tightened since 2025 (narrower interview waivers, expanded country restrictions): see the [B-1/B-2 guide for exams and interviews](/wiki/visas/b1-b2-for-interviews-and-exams) and the [visa policy tracker](/wiki/visas/visa-policy-changes-tracker).

## The short version

| Question | What the State Department manual says (as read in November 2026) |
|---|---|
| Can a physician observe on a B-1? | Yes, a medical doctor coming to observe US medical practices and consult with colleagues on the latest techniques may be a B-1 purpose, if no remuneration is received from a US source and no patient care is involved. |
| Can a medical student do a clerkship? | Yes in a narrow case: a student at a foreign medical school taking an unpaid elective clerkship at a US medical school's hospital as an approved part of the foreign degree, under faculty supervision. |
| Can a graduate use that clerkship rule? | No. It is for people still studying for the degree, and the manual says it does not apply to graduate medical training. |
| Can you be paid? | Not for this purpose. The doctor-observer provision requires no remuneration from a US source. |
| Hands-on experience in general? | The manual says applicants seeking hands-on clerkship experience are generally not within B-1 classification, except in the cases described. |

These are summaries of a manual that guides consular officers. They are not statutes, and each application is decided on its facts.

## What "observing" means in practice

Observerships are covered in more detail in [observership vs externship vs hands-on](/wiki/clinical-experience/observership-vs-externship-vs-hands-on). In short, an observer watches care and learns. AMA's observership guidelines describe observers as not providing patient care. Host institutions often add more limits:

- No physical exams, procedures, orders or prescriptions.
- Often no access to the electronic record, or read-only access under supervision.
- Supervision by a named physician at all times with patients.
- A maximum length that the institution sets.

Because the visa category is built around observing, any placement that promises hands-on work to a graduate on a B-1 is a warning sign. See [USCE red flags and scams](/wiki/clinical-experience/usce-red-flags-and-scams).

## Students and graduates

**Students.** If you are still enrolled in a foreign medical school, a B-1 may be available for an unpaid elective clerkship that your school approves as part of your degree. The manual describes this as for students pursuing a normal third- or fourth-year internship (clinical year) at a US medical school's hospital. The host medical school usually needs to confirm this in writing. Whether patient contact is permitted is mainly a matter for the host institution.

**Graduates.** Once you have graduated, the clerkship provision does not apply. You can still be eligible to observe as a physician if you meet the manual's terms, with no pay and no patient care. If a placement offers hands-on training to a graduate, the manual points toward other categories, such as a J-1 where an exchange program exists, and notes that graduate medical training is restricted by the home-country requirement and normally requires a J visa.

> [!WARNING] The most common mistake is describing a hands-on role to the consular officer, or doing one after entry, when the visa was granted for observing. Activity that goes beyond the visa purpose can create immigration problems that last beyond the placement. Describe the activity as it will really be.

## What hosts usually ask for

Policies vary, so ask for the written requirements. Common items include:

- An application, CV, diploma and sometimes a letter from your school.
- Proof of identity and, for graduates, evidence of licensure or standing in your country.
- Immunization records and sometimes a background check or health screening.
- Proof of health insurance and sometimes malpractice or liability coverage.
- A signed agreement stating that you will not give patient care.

Hosts often issue an invitation letter that states dates, the department, the supervisor and that the experience is unpaid. Bring it to your visa interview.

## Applying for the visa

The general steps are on the State Department's visitor visa page and are covered in the [B-1/B-2 guide](/wiki/visas/b1-b2-for-interviews-and-exams): complete the online application, pay the fee, attend the interview and bring supporting documents. Points specific to observerships:

1. **Purpose and dates.** Say that you will observe, for how long, where and at whose invitation. The invitation letter and your plans should match.
2. **Funding.** You must show that you can pay your own expenses. USCIS describes eligibility for B-1 as including sufficient funds for the stay.
3. **Ties to home.** You must show a residence abroad that you do not intend to give up and other binding ties. USCIS lists this as a requirement. A long unpaid stay with no clear plan is hard to square with this.
4. **Length of stay.** USCIS says the initial period of stay for B-1 is generally limited and can be extended in some cases, and that the admission period is set by the officer at the port of entry. Host institutions often set shorter limits. Check the admission record (I-94) and do not stay past it.
5. **Waiting times.** Appointment waits vary by consulate, and the State Department has narrowed interview waivers, so apply early once the placement is confirmed.

## Risks and limits

- **Not a path to residency.** An observership on a visitor visa does not give work authorization or a route to training. A B-1 stay might also make it harder to show clear intent for a later visa if your plans are unclear. Discuss your plan with the sponsor and, if needed, an immigration attorney.
- **No pay.** You cannot receive wages or stipends from a US source for the observer activity.
- **Placement fees.** Paying a third party to arrange an observership is legal in many cases, but it does not make the placement lawful or the visa approvable. A fee is not evidence that the activity fits the visa.
- **Refusals.** A refusal or an overstay can affect later visa applications, including a J-1 for residency. If you have either in your history, talk to an immigration attorney before applying.

## Practical steps

1. Get the host's written description of duties, supervision and the policy on patient contact.
2. Ask the host's international office what visa category it expects for your status (student or graduate).
3. Read the current text of 9 FAM 402.2-5 yourself, and take the relevant lines to the interview if useful.
4. Keep your story consistent across the application, the host's letter and your interview answers.
5. For anything beyond observing, ask the host or an attorney which visa category applies. Do not improvise.
