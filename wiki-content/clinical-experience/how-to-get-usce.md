Most IMGs find US clinical experience in one of four ways: asking a hospital or physician directly, applying to an institution's formal observer program, using a personal or alumni connection, or paying a third party to arrange it. The first three are usually cheaper and easier to verify. The fourth can be legitimate but needs more checking, because this is where most problems arise.

Start with [US clinical experience explained](/wiki/clinical-experience/usce-explained) if you are unsure what you actually need.

## Before you look

Know what you are trying to get. A useful USCE placement does three things:

- It puts you next to a US physician in your target specialty.
- It lasts long enough for that physician to see how you work.
- It can plausibly lead to a specific, honest letter.

Also check your own readiness. Many hosts ask for a degree from a listed medical school, a CV, proof of immunizations and some documentation of exam status. The AMA's sample guidelines list graduation from a school in the international medical school directory and a passing score on one or more USMLE Steps as typical eligibility; individual hosts vary.

## Route 1: Direct outreach

Many IMGs contact physicians or departments directly. A short, specific, professional email works better than a mass message.

1. **Target people with a reason.** Physicians at programs that regularly train IMGs, those with a link to your school or country, and those in your specialty.
2. **Write briefly.** Who you are, your graduation year, your specialty interest, the dates you can attend and what you are hoping to learn. Attach a CV.
3. **Expect to be redirected.** Many hospitals require approval from graduate medical education, a visitor or observer office, or a department chair. That process is a good sign.
4. **Follow up once or twice** at reasonable intervals, then move on.

Do not email dozens of physicians with identical text. It is ineffective and can look careless.

## Route 2: Formal institutional programs

Some academic medical centers, community hospitals and professional associations run structured observer programs with an application, eligibility rules, onboarding and an evaluation. Look on the websites of the hospital's graduate medical education office, the department and the residency program itself. Processes, fees and requirements differ and change, so read the current page and confirm by email.

## Route 3: Networks

Alumni from your medical school who are now in the US, your own faculty's contacts and the program directors who have trained people from your country are often the easiest way in. Ask what a realistic request looks like before you send one.

## Route 4: Third-party placement providers

Some private organizations arrange placements and charge fees. This can save time, but you are paying a middleman, and the quality varies widely. We do not rate or recommend providers. If you consider one, run the checks below and compare it against the direct routes.

## How to verify that a placement is legitimate

Do these before you pay, ideally in writing.

1. **Identify the host.** Ask for the hospital or practice name and address. Check it independently through its own website, not links supplied by the seller.
2. **Identify the supervisor.** Ask for the physician's full name and specialty. Confirm they work at the host institution using the institution's directory or the state medical board's license lookup.
3. **Contact the host yourself.** Email the department or the office that handles observers and ask whether they know of the arrangement, whether it is approved and what the rules are.
4. **Get duties and dates in writing.** Including hours, who supervises you, what you may and may not do, and what documentation you receive.
5. **Ask what the fee covers.** AMA guidance says fees should reflect actual costs. Ask whether the host institution receives any of it.
6. **Ask about the letter policy.** A seller cannot honestly promise a letter, because the physician decides whether to write one.
7. **Check clearances.** Real placements usually require immunizations, a background check and orientation. A placement with no onboarding at all is unusual.

## Red flags for pay-to-play offers

> [!WARNING] These signs do not prove a scam, but each one is a reason to slow down and verify before paying anything.

- Guaranteed interviews, matches or letters of recommendation.
- Promises of hands-on patient care, procedures or "externship" duties for visitors.
- Vague host details, such as "a top US hospital" without a name.
- Pressure to pay immediately, discounts that expire, or payment only by transfer or cryptocurrency.
- The seller will not let you speak to the host or the supervising physician.
- A supervisor who cannot be found in the hospital's directory.
- Pay-for-authorship, "research included" packages or ghost-written letters. These can harm your integrity record; ECFMG treats false or misleading information as a serious matter (see its irregular behavior policy).
- Review pages that are anonymous, uniformly glowing or hosted by the seller.

## What to do if you cannot afford it

- Prioritize one well-chosen placement over several scattered ones.
- Ask about institutional programs with modest fees before private ones.
- Use other ways to show readiness, such as strong exam results, research, volunteering and clear communication.
- Remember that no placement, paid or free, guarantees an outcome.

> [!NOTE] The 2024 NRMP Program Director Survey found that the most commonly endorsed interview factors were exam results, the MSPE and specialty-specific letters. Programs differ in how they treat IMGs, so look at the specific programs on your list before deciding how much to invest.
