There is no universal answer. No national body sets a required amount of US clinical experience (USCE) for IMGs, and the NRMP data does not publish a standard "months of USCE" target. What exists is a mix of individual program policies, specialty habits and applicant folklore. This guide separates what the evidence says from what is common talk.

## What the NRMP data says, and what it does not

The NRMP runs a Program Director Survey every even year, sent to programs after they certify their rank lists. The 2026 survey was conducted in March 2026 with an overall response of 12.7% (N = 861), and the 2024 survey had 18.0% (N = 1,150). The NRMP itself says to interpret results with caution given these response rates.

What the narrative reports tell us:

- **Interview selection.** In the 2024 survey, key interview considerations included USMLE Step 1 pass (90% of respondents), the MSPE (85%) and specialty-specific letters of recommendation (84%).
- **Ranking.** Key ranking considerations were interpersonal skills (89%), interactions during interviews (87%) and feedback from current residents (76%).
- **IMG interview habits vary a lot.** In both surveys, only some specialties reported "often" interviewing and ranking IMGs. In 2024, pathology and internal medicine most often did; in 2026, pathology led at 71%, followed in some categories by pediatrics, family medicine and internal medicine.
- **Exam gates.** In 2026, 92% of programs reported requiring IMG applicants to have taken USMLE Step 1 and 84% required Step 2 CK for interview consideration, regardless of result requirement.

The factor lists printed in the 2026 survey instrument include specialty letters of recommendation, away rotations and audition electives, but, as published, they do not list US clinical experience as a standalone item. We therefore cannot cite a percentage of programs that "require" or "prefer" USCE from this source. If you read a number online that claims otherwise, ask where it comes from.

> [!NOTE] The NRMP's IMG outcomes report (Charting Outcomes, 2024) describes applicant experiences such as research, work and volunteering as self-reported counts that are not verified, with quality varying greatly. It reports matched and unmatched applicants in several specialties but does not offer a USCE benchmark. Use it for context, not for targets.

## Why expectations differ

- **Specialty culture.** Some specialties routinely interview IMGs and others rarely do. Programs in the first group are likelier to have worked out what they expect.
- **Program history.** A program that has trained IMGs from your region may care less about USCE than one with few IMGs.
- **Volume.** Programs that receive thousands of applications use filters. Some filters involve exam results, graduation year or USCE. See [how programs screen applications](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters).
- **Visa and licensure rules.** A program's sponsorship policies can matter more than USCE for who gets considered.
- **Recency.** Some programs prefer USCE from the last year or two, or accept only certain kinds.

## Common patterns (practice, not rules)

These are widely reported by IMGs and advisors, but they are not official data, so treat them as opinion:

- Many programs that accept IMGs prefer to see some USCE, particularly with a letter from a US physician.
- Programs often look for USCE in the specialty you are applying to.
- A few months of well-documented, supervised experience, ideally with letters, is a common aim.
- Applicants with a large number of unsupervised weeks sometimes find the quantity does not improve their outcomes.

## How to find a program's real policy

1. **Read the program website.** Look for sections on eligibility, applicants, requirements for IMGs or frequently asked questions.
2. **Check FREIDA and ERAS descriptions.** Programs often list visa, exam and USCE information there.
3. **Read for specifics.** Words like "preferred," "required," "within X years of graduation" and "US-based letters" mean different things.
4. **Email the coordinator.** Keep it short and specific. Ask the question once.
5. **Record answers in your program list.** See [building a program list](/wiki/application/building-a-program-list).

## What to do with this information

- Do not set your plan by an anonymous number from a forum.
- Ask whether you need more USCE or better USCE: more supervision, a specialty match or a stronger letter.
- Balance USCE against other priorities: exam scores, research, applications timing and cost.
- If a seller claims that a specific length "guarantees" consideration, ask for evidence and see our guide to [how to get USCE](/wiki/clinical-experience/how-to-get-usce) for how to check claims.

> [!IMG TIP] A one-page table with each target program, its stated USCE policy, the source page and the date you checked is more valuable than any rule of thumb. Policies change from year to year.
