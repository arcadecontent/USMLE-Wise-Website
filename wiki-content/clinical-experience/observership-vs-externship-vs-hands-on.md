An observership lets you watch US clinicians care for patients, while an externship or hands-on role lets you take part in care to some degree. The terms are used loosely, so the practical question is not what the placement is called but what you will be allowed to do, who supervises you and what the supervisor can honestly write about you afterward.

For short definitions, see the glossary entries for [[observership]] and [[usce]]. For the wider picture, see [US clinical experience explained](/wiki/clinical-experience/usce-explained).

## The three categories at a glance

| | Observership | Externship or clerkship style | Hands-on role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Patient care | None: you watch | Limited or student-like, if the host allows it | Direct, under supervision |
| Orders, notes, procedures | No | Depends on host policy and EHR access | Within the scope of your role |
| Who can usually do it | Many IMGs who have graduated | Often graduates or students of eligible schools, sometimes with extra requirements | People with a legal training status or job in that role |
| Typical length | Days to a few weeks per preceptor | A few weeks to months | Months to years |
| Main benefit | Exposure and a possible letter | Closer supervision and richer detail in a letter | Documented clinical responsibility |

Treat the table as orientation. Hospitals and states write their own rules.

## Observerships: what they allow

The AMA's published observership guidelines describe the goal as acculturation to US practice, not filling gaps in training. They list the responsibilities of observers: follow the preceptor's instructions, do not conduct physical examinations, treatments or diagnoses, accept no compensation, follow hospital rules and HIPAA, and pay actual administrative costs such as immunizations. They also describe typical rotations of two to four weeks per preceptor and say observership should not be considered a mandatory step before residency.

In practice, an observer might attend rounds, clinic and conferences; see how notes, coding and order entry are done on a screen; and discuss cases with the physician. Many hospitals bar observers from the electronic medical record entirely, so what you can see varies.

Two limits to understand:

- **No hands-on care.** If a placement promises that you will examine patients, write orders or perform procedures, be cautious. That description does not fit a standard observership and may be a red flag for legality or honesty.
- **No billing and no pay.** An observer is not a billing clinician and is not an employee.

## Externships and clerkship-style placements

"Externship" has no standard definition for IMGs. It may mean a structured clinical elective offered through a school, a hospital program with defined duties, or a marketing name for a long observership. Three questions sort them out:

1. **Who is the sponsor?** A hospital, a medical school or an accredited program carries more institutional weight than a private intermediary.
2. **What are the duties in writing?** If they are vague, assume you will be observing.
3. **What do you receive?** A supervisor evaluation or letter is more useful than a certificate.

Some of these arrangements require proof of eligibility (such as graduation from a listed school or a USMLE transcript), health clearances, malpractice coverage and background checks. Those requirements are usually a good sign that the host takes the arrangement seriously.

## Hands-on roles

Hands-on experience means real clinical responsibility under supervision. It usually requires a legal status, such as a training license or employment authorization, and a position such as a clinical research coordinator with patient contact, a medical assistant, a scribe or a trainee in a program. Many IMGs do not have access to these before residency. If you do, count what you actually did (patient contact, documentation, procedures within your role) rather than what the title implies.

## Why labels mislead

Applicants sometimes find a placement described as "hands-on" or "externship" that, on arrival, turns out to be observation only. The reverse also happens: an advertised observership in which the host expects work that goes beyond what is lawful for a visitor. Both are problems.

> [!WARNING] If a placement promises that you will see patients independently, perform procedures, or "get hands-on experience" as a visitor, ask the host institution directly (not the seller) what is permitted. Misrepresenting duties on your application can also raise integrity concerns under ECFMG's irregular behavior rules, which cover submitting inaccurate information. Describe your role exactly as it was.

## How programs may read each type

Programs differ. Some state a preference for particular kinds of experience on their websites, and some do not distinguish at all. In general, programs are looking for evidence that a US physician has seen you work, so the specifics of your role and your supervisor's letter matter more than the category name. The 2024 NRMP Program Director Survey found specialty-specific letters of recommendation among the most commonly endorsed interview factors (84% of respondents), which is why supervised time that leads to a letter is the usual goal. See [how to turn USCE into a strong letter](/wiki/clinical-experience/turning-usce-into-a-letter).

## How to choose

1. Confirm the duties and the supervisor's name in writing.
2. Ask whether the physician will evaluate you or write a letter, and under what conditions.
3. Ask what hospital or practice you will be physically in and whether you will have clearances and badge access.
4. Check that the description of your role matches what your application will say.
5. Compare total cost against what you will actually receive (see the [total cost of matching](/wiki/start-here/total-cost-of-matching) for a budget view).

> [!IMG TIP] Keep a short log as you go: dates, hours, activities and supervisor names. It makes the ERAS entry and any later letter request easier and more accurate.
