Most US clinical experience offers are honest, but the market for them is crowded and largely unregulated, and IMGs are a frequent target for fraud. This page lists warning signs, a verification routine and what to do if something goes wrong. It does not name companies. The guidance comes from consumer-protection authorities and from how observerships are meant to work. For finding real placements, see [how to get USCE](/wiki/clinical-experience/how-to-get-usce).

## Why red flags matter

USCE is valued by many programs, so applicants are willing to spend money on it. Programs say what they weigh, as in NRMP's survey of program directors, but they do not require any particular paid product. A placement that costs a great deal and teaches little may add nothing to your application, and a fraudulent one can cost you money and credibility.

> [!KEY] No honest host can promise you a letter of recommendation, a good evaluation, a residency interview or a match. The content of a letter depends on what a supervising physician actually observes.

## Payment red flags

The Federal Trade Commission lists how someone asks you to pay as a major clue to a scam.

- **Payment only by wire transfer, cryptocurrency, gift cards or a peer-to-peer app.** The FTC says that no legitimate business or government agency will insist on payment by gift card, and that anyone insisting on a payment method of this kind is probably a scammer.
- **Pressure to pay immediately,** with claims that the offer will vanish. The FTC advises resisting pressure to act immediately and says honest businesses give you time to decide.
- **A personal account.** Payment to an individual or a name that does not match the organization.
- **A fake check.** You receive a check or transfer and are asked to send some of it back. The check later turns out to be fake.
- **No refund terms in writing,** or an "administrative fee" that is never refundable even if the host cancels.
- **Charges for things that should be free,** such as a placement inside a hospital that normally takes observers without a fee. Fees can be legitimate, but ask the host directly what its own policy is.

## Promise red flags

- **Guaranteed letters of recommendation,** guaranteed strong evaluations, or letters pre-written for you to sign.
- **Guaranteed interviews, matches or program placement,** or claims of inside influence with program directors.
- **Hands-on clinical work for graduates on a visitor visa.** See [B-1/B-2 visas for observerships](/wiki/clinical-experience/b1-b2-for-observerships) for what is generally permitted. The observership guidelines used by the AMA describe observers as not providing patient care, so a promise of procedures or orders is a warning sign.
- **"Externship" labels without detail.** See [observership vs externship vs hands-on](/wiki/clinical-experience/observership-vs-externship-vs-hands-on). The term is not standardized, so ask for a written description of duties.
- **Vague claims of "approval"** by a hospital, a state board, ECFMG or ACGME. Ask for the specific person and document.
- **Visa promises.** An organizer cannot promise that a consulate will issue a visa.

## Identity and communication red flags

- Only a free email address, a messaging app or a social media profile as contact.
- A website with no named physician, department, address or institution, or with stock photos of hospitals.
- No named supervising physician, or a supervisor you cannot contact independently.
- Offer letters with errors, a logo you can't match to the hospital's real site, or a signature with no title.
- Pressure not to contact the hospital directly.
- Many "locations" for one organizer, with identical wording and no named hosts.
- Fake or paid reviews. Treat testimonials on the seller's own site as marketing, and be cautious about review sites you can't check.

## Quality red flags (not scams, but poor value)

- No defined schedule, supervisor or learning goals.
- Large numbers of observers per physician, so that no one can get to know you.
- No evaluation form, or a form you complete yourself.
- Placement in a facility that your target programs wouldn't recognize as clinical, such as an office with no patients.

See [turning USCE into a letter](/wiki/clinical-experience/turning-usce-into-a-letter) and [how much USCE programs want](/wiki/clinical-experience/how-much-usce-programs-want) for what makes experience useful.

## Verify before you pay

1. **Find the host on its own official website.** Use a search you perform yourself, not a link in the offer.
2. **Contact the named supervisor or office** using contact details from that website. Ask them to confirm in writing that you have been accepted, the dates, the department, your duties and any fee.
3. **Ask for terms in writing:** what you receive, what you pay, who gets the payment, the refund policy, how and when letters or evaluations are issued.
4. **Check the hospital's observer policy** (many publish one) and see whether it matches the offer.
5. **Pay by a traceable method** such as a card, and avoid payment methods that are hard to reverse.
6. **Talk to past participants** you can find independently, such as colleagues from your school, rather than people supplied by the seller.
7. **Check with your school or mentor,** who may know the program.
8. **If the visa matters,** ask the host's international office what category it expects and check the State Department wording yourself.

## Document integrity

ECFMG's Information Booklet treats falsified documents and misrepresentation as irregular behavior, which can lead to serious consequences for certification. Never sign a letter that you did not write, claim experience you did not have, or let someone else describe an observership you did not complete. Programs verify, and the consequence of a false statement lasts longer than the benefit. See [ECFMG certification overview](/wiki/ecfmg/ecfmg-certification-overview).

> [!WARNING] Letters of recommendation must come from the person who wrote them. If a third party offers to "arrange" or "prepare" a letter for a supervisor to sign, do not accept. Programs read letters closely and can contact the writer.

## If you have been scammed

1. Stop all payments and contact the bank, card issuer or payment service immediately. Ask about reversing the payment.
2. Save emails, messages, receipts, screenshots and the offer.
3. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov if the seller is in the US or targets people there, and to the consumer authority in your own country.
4. If a hospital's name was used without permission, tell the hospital using the contact details on its official site.
5. Warn others in your professional networks without making unverified accusations. Stick to facts you can document.

## Opinion: how we would approach it

This section is opinion. Place the most weight on a named supervising physician at a real institution who can write about you, a clear written description of duties, and fees you can explain and justify. If the arrangement relies on pressure, secrecy or promises about outcomes, walk away. A smaller, honest placement with a physician who knows your work is worth more than an expensive one that cannot produce a specific letter.
