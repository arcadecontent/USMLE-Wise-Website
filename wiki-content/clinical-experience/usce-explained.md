US clinical experience, or USCE, means time you spend in a US clinical setting such as a hospital or clinic. For international medical graduates (IMGs), it is the closest thing to showing a program that you already know how US care works. It is not a formal requirement of the exams or of ECFMG certification, but many programs weigh it, and it is the usual way IMGs get US-based letters of recommendation.

The glossary has a short definition of [[usce|USCE]]; this guide explains how it fits into your plan.

## What counts as USCE

"USCE" is not a regulated term. Programs and applicants use it for several different arrangements, and the key difference is how much you are allowed to do.

| Type | What you generally do | Typical features |
|---|---|---|
| [[observership|Observership]] | Watch clinicians work, attend rounds and conferences | No hands-on patient care, no orders, often short |
| Externship or clerkship style | Take a more active student-like role, depending on the host | Often tied to a school or hospital, may need extra eligibility and insurance |
| Hands-on role | Take part in patient care under supervision | Usually requires a license, a training status or an employment role |
| Research or non-clinical work in a US clinical department | Contribute to a project, sometimes with clinic exposure | Not clinical experience unless the work includes direct clinical teaching or supervision |

The next guide, [Observership vs externship vs hands-on experience](/wiki/clinical-experience/observership-vs-externship-vs-hands-on), explains the differences in detail and why the label on a placement can be misleading.

## Why programs care

Programs reviewing IMG applications are often trying to answer a few practical questions: Will this person understand how a US team works? Can someone in the US vouch for their clinical judgment and professionalism? Can they communicate with patients and colleagues in English?

Time in a US setting can offer evidence on those points, mostly through a letter from a supervising physician who watched you work. That is the real product of USCE. A certificate on its own tells a program very little.

> [!NOTE] The NRMP Program Director Survey, run every two years, asks programs which factors they use to choose applicants to interview and to rank. In the 2024 survey, the most commonly endorsed interview factors included USMLE Step 1 pass (90% of respondents), the MSPE (85%) and specialty-specific letters of recommendation (84%). Letters are where USCE usually helps. The factor lists in the survey instrument are fixed, so the survey does not by itself show how much weight any particular amount of USCE carries.

## What USCE cannot do

It is easy to treat USCE as a magic ingredient. A realistic view helps.

- **It does not replace exam performance.** Many programs screen on exam results before they read the rest of an application. See [how programs screen applications](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters).
- **It does not guarantee an interview or a match.** Programs differ, and no amount of USCE can promise an outcome.
- **A longer placement is not automatically better.** One month with a physician who knows you well and writes a specific letter usually helps more than several unsupervised weeks.
- **A paid placement is not stronger because it was paid.** What matters is the supervision and the letter, not the price.

## What is different for IMGs

US graduates complete clinical rotations as part of their degree and get letters from those rotations automatically. IMGs have to create that access themselves, usually after graduation, with their own time and money. That is why this topic is so prominent for IMGs and also why it attracts businesses that sell placements. Our guide to [how to get USCE](/wiki/clinical-experience/how-to-get-usce) covers legitimate routes and how to check a placement.

> [!IMG TIP] Before you spend money or time, write down what you actually need: a letter in your target specialty, familiarity with US workflow, or both. Then look for placements that can deliver that. Choosing a placement because it is advertised heavily is the most common way applicants end up with experience that does not help.

## Visa and legal points to check

Visitors to the US cannot work, and what a visitor visa allows for an observership is narrower than many applicants assume. We will not cover the visa side here; confirm with the host institution and a qualified immigration professional before you travel. Also, state law and hospital policy govern what any non-licensed person may do with patients, so follow the host's rules exactly.

## How to plan around it

1. Decide your target specialty and the programs you plan to apply to (see [how to identify IMG-friendly programs](/wiki/application/identifying-img-friendly-programs)).
2. Check those programs' websites for stated USCE expectations, including how recent it must be.
3. Work backward from the ERAS season so your letters are ready on time (see the [ERAS timeline](/wiki/application/eras-timeline)).
4. Pick placements that offer real supervision and a realistic chance of a letter.
5. Keep records: dates, supervisor names, duties and any evaluation forms.

If you cannot get USCE, you still have an application. Strong exam results, research, clear communication and a well-targeted program list matter too. Do not assume you must buy a placement to be considered.
