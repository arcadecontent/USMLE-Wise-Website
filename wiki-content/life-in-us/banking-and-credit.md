You can open a US checking account within your first week and begin building credit in your first month, but you will start with no credit score at all. That matters because landlords, utility companies and lenders look at credit, and the effects show up in your first housing and phone contracts. This page gives a sequence that works for most new residents. It is general information, not financial advice.

## Step 1: open a checking account for payroll

Your employer will want direct deposit details early. Before you go to a bank:

- Ask your program's HR whether they use a particular payroll bank or whether any bank is fine.
- Bring your passport, visa documents, proof of your US address (a lease, a letter from the program or a temporary housing receipt) and, if you have it, your Social Security number. See [SSN and ITIN](/wiki/life-in-us/ssn-and-itin).
- Ask whether the bank can open an account before your SSN arrives. Policies differ between banks, and I cannot give a rule that holds for all of them.

When you compare accounts, ask about monthly fees and how to avoid them, minimum balances, overdraft charges, ATM fees, and whether you can send money abroad. Credit unions and large banks can both work. Check that the institution is federally insured, which you can verify with the FDIC or NCUA.

> [!IMG TIP] Opening a US account before you arrive is sometimes possible through some banks that serve international newcomers. Whether a bank allows it, and what it needs, changes. Treat this as something to ask about, not something to count on.

## Step 2: understand what "no credit" means

A credit report is a record kept by credit bureaus of the accounts you have in the US and how you pay them. A credit score is a number derived from it. A new arrival has no report, so lenders cannot score you. This is different from a bad score. It only means a gap.

The CFPB identifies the factors it highlights for a good score: paying bills on time, keeping balances low relative to your credit limit, and the length and mix of your history. Time matters, and no product skips it.

## Step 3: start a credit file

The CFPB's guide to building credit from scratch describes several common starting points. Each has a cost and a catch.

| Option | How it works | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Secured credit card | You pay a deposit that usually sets your limit. The CFPB describes ranges that vary by card | Does the issuer report to all major credit bureaus? Annual and monthly fees? Does the deposit come back? Can it become a regular card? |
| Credit builder loan | The lender holds the loan money in a locked account while you make payments, then releases it. The CFPB notes terms of months to a couple of years | Fees, interest, and whether payments are reported |
| Store or limited-history card | Easier approval, often a low limit | Interest rates, fees, and reporting |
| Authorized user | You are added to someone else's card | Only helps if their account is in good standing and reported. Their behaviour affects you |

The CFPB advises that secured cards can be costly, and that if you can qualify for a regular card with fewer fees, that may be better. As a new resident with a stable job, you may find some issuers offer a starter card even with no history, so check the terms before paying a deposit.

### Habits that matter more than the product

- Pay the full statement balance on time, every month. Set autopay so you do not miss a payment.
- Keep your balance well below the limit. The CFPB's published guidance mentions staying under roughly a third of your limit as a common target.
- Do not apply for many accounts at once, as each application can leave a mark.
- Do not carry a balance to "build credit." That is a myth. Paying in full builds the same history without interest.

## Step 4: protect and check your credit

- You can get free credit reports from the three nationwide bureaus through the federal site AnnualCreditReport.com. Check them for errors a few months after you start.
- Be careful with identity theft. A new file with no activity can be a target. Consider a credit freeze, which the bureaus offer free of charge, and unfreeze when you apply for credit.
- Beware companies that promise to "fix" or "create" credit for a fee. The CFPB warns consumers about credit repair promises.

## Where lack of credit bites first

Landlords, utilities, phone plans and car lenders often run a credit check. Without a history you may be asked for a larger security deposit, a co-signer, proof of income, or prepayment. See [housing for residents](/wiki/life-in-us/housing-for-residents). Your offer letter or contract, as proof of income, helps in these conversations.

For phone plans, prepaid plans avoid a credit check but do not build credit.

## Sending money home and managing the early budget

Compare transfer providers on total cost, including the exchange rate margin, not only the stated fee. Keep your first months simple: one checking account, one card used for small regular purchases, a budget that includes loan repayments from home if you have them.

## When to get help

If you are unsure about a contract or a product, a nonprofit credit counselling service or your hospital's financial wellness resources can explain terms. Residents also sometimes get free or discounted tax and financial education through their GME office. Ask.
