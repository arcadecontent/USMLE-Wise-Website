Residency letters of recommendation examples are most useful when you can see why one letter is strong and another is generic. A strong letter describes specific things the writer saw you do, compares you honestly with a named group, and says how ready you are for residency. A weak letter praises traits without evidence. Below are annotated excerpts of weak and strong letters from three writers [[img|international medical graduates]] commonly use (a US clinical attending, a research mentor and a home-country professor), plus how to choose and brief a writer, a request-email template and the facts about waiving your right to see a letter.

> [!WARNING] Every excerpt on this page is an **illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team**. None is a real physician's letter, and no real names, hospitals or schools appear. Real letters are confidential, and we publish none. Do not copy these, and do not give them to a writer as a script. A letter must be the writer's own assessment of what the writer observed.

This page owns the examples and templates. The rules of the process (how entries, Letter IDs and assignments work) are on [letters of recommendation: who, how many and how to ask](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation). For getting a good letter out of a US placement specifically, see [turning US clinical experience into a letter](/wiki/clinical-experience/turning-usce-into-a-letter).

## What makes a residency letter of recommendation strong

AAMC and the Organization of Program Directors Association (OPDA) published joint guidelines for writing a narrative letter for a residency applicant. They are recommended guidance, not requirements, and they do not cover the standardized forms some specialties use. They are still the best primary-source picture of what a good letter does, and the table below follows them. You can use it to read your own letters, or to explain to a writer what you hope they can cover.

| Element | What the guidelines ask a writer to do | Weak sounds like | Strong sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relationship and context | Describe their role, when and how often they worked with you, the setting and their background assessing candidates | "I have known her for several years." | "I supervised her on my inpatient team on most days for [N] weeks." |
| Specific observed behavior | Ground comments in specific behaviors: the situation, the behavior and the consequences | "She works well with others." | "When she noticed X, she did Y, and the plan changed." |
| Comparison | If comparing, name the comparison group and support it with evidence | "One of the best students." | "Among the international graduates I have supervised over [N] years, ..." |
| Readiness | Assess performance against expectations for your level of training | "She will be an excellent resident." | "At the level I expect of a strong new intern." |
| Interest in the specialty | Describe the observed reasons you are pursuing it | Nothing | "She told me she wants X, and what I saw fits." |
| Growth and concerns | Describe challenges or areas for improvement, progress and response to feedback | Nothing, or a hidden warning | "Early on she did X; after we agreed on Y, she improved." |
| Format | Use letterhead, give position and contact details, sign and date | Unsigned, no way to reach the writer | Complete, with email and phone |

The same guidelines list what to avoid: vague or generic praise, unsupported endorsements such as recommending you "for any residency program", weak context ("I have known him for several years"), personal or sensitive details not tied to your strengths, and language that differs between applicants with similar strengths. They also tell writers to ask the applicant's permission before including private information.

Opinion: a small, honest growth area with evidence of improvement usually makes the praise around it more believable than none at all. A letter with no weakness mentioned anywhere can read like a template.

## Example 1: US clinical attending (internal medicine)

This is the letter most IMGs want, and it is the one most often written badly because the writer saw you for a short time. The attending in this composite supervised an observer and says so.

**Weak excerpt (composite)**

> To the selection committee:
>
> I am writing to recommend [Applicant] for residency. She spent [N] weeks on our inpatient service. She was always on time, polite and hardworking, and got along well with everyone on the team. She is a good listener and a quick learner. I recommend her for any residency program without reservation.

**Why it is weak**

- The only context is a duration. We learn nothing about how often the writer saw her, what her role was, or why the writer's judgment counts.
- It lists traits (on time, polite, hardworking) with no observed behavior. "Good listener" and "works well with others" are the kind of vague praise AAMC's guidelines tell writers to replace with specifics.
- There is no comparison group, no statement of readiness and no reason she wants the specialty.
- "Any residency program" is an unsupported endorsement. A reader cannot tell this letter from a form letter.

**Stronger excerpt (composite)**

> To the selection committee:
>
> I am an attending physician in internal medicine at [US teaching hospital], where I supervise residents and visiting learners on the inpatient service. [Applicant] joined my team as an observer for [N] weeks, and I worked with her on most days, on rounds and in afternoon case discussions.
>
> On her third day, she noticed that a patient's discharge medication list left out a drug the patient had mentioned taking at home, and she asked me whether we should clarify it. We did, and it changed the plan. After that, she checked home medications for every patient she followed, and by the second week she brought me a short list of questions before rounds each morning.
>
> Compared with the international graduates I have supervised on this service over [N] years, she was among the most prepared. Her presentations were organized, she read about each patient, and she could say clearly what she did not yet know. As an observer, she did not carry patient care responsibility, so I cannot comment on her procedural skills or independent decision-making. I can say that her clinical reasoning on rounds was at the level I expect from a strong new intern in the first months of training.
>
> [Applicant] told me she wants internal medicine because [reason, in her own words, such as following a diagnostic problem over several days and explaining long-term care to patients], and what I observed was consistent with that. Early on, her presentations ran long. After we agreed on a one-minute structure, they became concise. I expect her to do well as a first-year resident, and I would be glad to have her on my team.
>
> [Name, credentials]  
> [Title, department, institution]  
> [Email | phone]

**Why it works**

1. **Context first:** the writer's role, the setting, how often they met, and the learner's status as an observer.
2. **A specific incident** in situation, behavior and consequence form, which is exactly the shape AAMC's guidelines describe.
3. **A named comparison group** (international graduates this attending has supervised) with a time span, not "one of the best".
4. **Honest limits:** it says what the writer could not observe. That candor makes the rest more credible, and it is true to what an observer can do. See [observership vs externship vs hands-on](/wiki/clinical-experience/observership-vs-externship-vs-hands-on).
5. **Readiness at a stated level** (a new intern), as the guidelines recommend.
6. **Reason for the specialty,** as the writer heard it.
7. **A small growth area and the fix,** followed by a plain expectation about residency. It ends with a human statement, not a slogan.

## Example 2: research mentor

A research letter helps most when you apply to programs or specialties that value research, and when it says plainly what it can and cannot judge. See [how programs view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research).

**Weak excerpt (composite)**

> [Applicant] worked in my research group for [N] months. He is intelligent, hardworking and dedicated to research. He contributed to a paper and was a valuable member of the team. He will be a great asset to any program.

**Why it is weak**

- It names no project, no task and no result. "Contributed to a paper" gives no status and no role.
- Every adjective is a claim with no evidence.
- It does not say what the writer can judge (research habits) and what the writer cannot (clinical skills), so a reader may assume it speaks for both.

**Stronger excerpt (composite)**

> I am a professor of [field] and have led a clinical research group at [university hospital] for [N] years. [Applicant] worked with me weekly for [N] months as a research assistant on a retrospective cohort study of [condition] outcomes.
>
> His role was specific. He built the data collection form with our statistician, screened charts against the inclusion criteria, and drafted the methods and results sections of the manuscript, which is [status]. During our first data review, he found that a discharge code was used inconsistently across two units. He documented the problem and proposed how to handle it before the analysis, which changed how we defined the outcome. He is [author position] on the manuscript, which reflects his contribution to the analysis and drafting.
>
> Among the research assistants I have supervised in the past [N] years, he needed little supervision for data work and was reliable about deadlines. Early in the project he hesitated to challenge a senior colleague's interpretation. By the end he raised concerns directly and in writing. I did not observe [Applicant] caring for patients, so I cannot comment on his bedside skills. I can speak to his scientific reasoning, accuracy and follow-through, and I believe they will serve him well in a residency that values quality improvement and clinical research.
>
> [Name, credentials]  
> [Title, department, institution]  
> [Email | phone]

**Why it works**

1. **Scope is explicit:** the writer says what they observed and what they did not.
2. **The role is concrete,** with tasks that match what the applicant should also put in the experience entry and Scholarly Work. A mismatch between the letter and the application is easy to spot. See [authorship and author order](/wiki/research/authorship-and-author-order).
3. **One real finding** (the inconsistent code) shows judgment, not just effort.
4. **The comparison group is named** (research assistants the writer supervised), with a time span.
5. **A mild, honest growth point** with evidence of change.

## Example 3: home-country professor

Letters from your own faculty show reliability over time, but US readers may not know your school or its grading, so the best ones explain both. This composite is for internal medicine.

**Weak excerpt (composite)**

> [Applicant] was a student in our department. She ranked high in the class and is a very good student with excellent character. She attended all of the lectures and was never absent. She is recommended for postgraduate training abroad.

**Why it is weak**

- It describes a student, not a clinician. Attendance is not performance.
- "Ranked high" has no number, group or basis, and it gives a US reader no context for the school.
- "Training abroad" names no specialty, so it cannot be tied to a residency.
- It reports nothing the writer saw in the clinic or on the ward.

**Stronger excerpt (composite)**

> I am a professor of medicine at [Medical school], [city, country], and I have taught the internal medicine clerkship for [N] years. For readers who are not familiar with our system: in this clerkship, students take histories and examine patients themselves, present to the resident and consultant, and write the admission note, which I countersign.
>
> [Applicant] rotated through my unit for [N] weeks in [Month Year], and I supervised her on the ward and in the outpatient clinic twice a week. I regularly watched her take histories and examine patients before presenting her assessment and plan to me. On one admission, she noticed [an overlooked symptom] that the team had missed, raised it politely with the resident, and the plan was changed. Patients and nurses sought her out because she explained the plan in plain language.
>
> Among the students I have supervised in this clerkship over [N] years, she was in the strongest group. Her final clerkship grade was [grade], on a scale where [explain what the scale means]. She has chosen internal medicine because [reason she gave]. She still works to be faster in busy outpatient sessions, and she has improved steadily as she took more of the visit herself. I recommend her for internal medicine training with confidence.
>
> [Name, credentials]  
> [Title, department, institution]  
> [Email | phone]

**Why it works**

1. **It explains the system** in two sentences, so a US reader can judge the level of responsibility.
2. **It reports supervised clinical work,** not lectures, and states how often the writer saw her.
3. **It translates the grade** instead of assuming the reader knows the scale.
4. **It names the specialty** and a reason.
5. **It is consistent with the [[mspe]],** which matters: a letter that contradicts the MSPE damages the credibility of both.
6. **Letterhead and contact details** let a program verify the letter. AAMC's checklist for writers includes letterhead, position and contact information.

## Standardized letters, surgery and chair letters

- **Standardized letters.** Some specialties use forms from their national societies, which MyERAS calls SLOE, SLOR, eSLOE and SEL. When you create a letter entry you choose Standardized or Narrative. If you pick "General" as the specialty, neither option is available, and Plastic Surgery - Integrated does not offer the narrative option. The excerpts above are narrative, and AAMC's guidelines do not cover standardized forms. If your specialty uses one, the writer follows that society's instructions. Check the specialty's own guidance.
- **Surgery and other specialties.** Specialties differ in what they ask for, so check the national guidance and each program's page. AAMC's guidelines ask writers to confirm the specialty and tailor the letter to it. Practice: a letter from a physician in your specialty is usually more persuasive than a general one.
- **Department chair letters.** For every letter entry, MyERAS asks whether it must fulfill a Department Chair requirement. That is a flag on a letter, not a separate document type. Check each program's page to see whether it asks for a chair letter, and from which department, then ask early.

## How to choose your writers

Choose for how closely they watched you work, not for title. A detailed letter from someone who supervised you closely usually beats a short one from a famous name who barely knew you (practice). In the {{fact:eras_cycle_year}} cycle you can assign up to {{fact:eras_letters_per_program}} letters to each program, so build a lean set around your best evidence.

| Writer | What they can credibly say | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| US clinical attending, in your specialty | Your reasoning, work habits and fit in a US team | A short placement may produce only a general letter |
| US clinical attending, other specialty | Professionalism, communication, teamwork | Less weight for the specialty you apply to |
| Research mentor | Scientific reasoning, reliability, authorship role | Cannot judge bedside skills |
| Home-country professor or supervisor | Your performance over time, in your own system | Needs to explain the system to US readers |
| Department chair | Standing in the department, when a program asks for one | Should know you, not just sign |

1. **List everyone who supervised you closely,** with dates and what they saw.
2. **Rank them** by how closely they observed you, how well they match your specialty, and how likely they are to write strongly.
3. **Ask a graceful question:** "Could you write me a strong letter?" Accept an honest no or a hesitant answer. A lukewarm letter can hurt.
4. **Avoid relatives and personal friends** as writers.
5. **Build a set,** not a pile. A typical IMG set (practice, not a rule) leans on US clinical letters in the specialty, plus one that vouches for you over a longer time, such as a home professor or research mentor. Programs publish their own requirements, so check each one.
6. **Do not add a weak extra letter** just because there is a slot.

## How to brief a writer

AAMC's guidelines tell writers to request your CV and a draft personal statement if you have not provided them, so send both with your request. A brief helps the writer remember specifics, but it must stay a brief. The writer should write from what they observed.

Send, in one message:

- **Your master CV** (see the [ERAS CV template](/wiki/application/eras-cv-template-and-examples)).
- **Your personal statement draft** or its main themes.
- **The specialty** and the kinds of programs you are targeting.
- **A short list of specific moments** from your work together, in the same shape the guidelines ask writers to use: what happened, what you did, and what changed.
- **What you hope they can speak to,** limited to what they actually saw.
- **Context you want covered,** such as an exam attempt or a gap. Writers should ask your permission before including private or sensitive details, so tell them what you are comfortable with them mentioning.
- **The logistics:** your MyERAS Letter Request Form, the date you hope for, and whether you waived your right to view.

Do not write the letter for the writer, script praise, or ask them to describe skills they did not see.

## Request email templates for residency letters of recommendation

These are composite templates. Replace everything in brackets, and keep your own voice.

**Template 1: the ask**

> Subject: Request for a residency letter of recommendation: [Your name]
>
> Dear Dr. [Surname],
>
> Thank you for supervising me during [rotation or project] at [site] from [Month Year] to [Month Year]. I am applying to [specialty] residency in the {{fact:eras_cycle_year}} ERAS cycle, and I am writing to ask whether you could write a strong letter of recommendation for me.
>
> I understand if you do not feel you saw enough of my work to write a strong letter, and I would appreciate your telling me honestly.
>
> If you are able to, I will send my CV, a draft of my personal statement and a short list of the cases and projects we worked on together. I would be grateful if the letter could be uploaded by [date]. If that date is difficult, please tell me and I will adjust my plan.
>
> Thank you for considering this.
>
> Sincerely,  
> [Your name]  
> [Email | phone]

**Template 2: after the writer says yes**

> Subject: Letter packet and ERAS request: [Your name]
>
> Dear Dr. [Surname],
>
> Thank you for agreeing to write for me. Attached are my CV, my draft personal statement, a note on the specialty and programs I am targeting, and a short list of moments from our work together with dates.
>
> I have sent the Letter Request Form through MyERAS (please check your spam folder). It includes a Letter ID that works once, so please use that form to upload through the AAMC Letter Writer Portal. A designee may upload for you if that is easier. [I have chosen to waive my right to view the letter. / I have chosen not to waive my right to view the letter.]
>
> The date I am hoping for is [date]. Please tell me if you need anything else from me.
>
> With thanks,  
> [Your name]

**Template 3: a polite reminder**

> Dear Dr. [Surname],
>
> I wanted to check in about my letter for [specialty] residency, which I was hoping to have uploaded by [date]. I am happy to resend the request form or any materials. Thank you again for your time.
>
> [Your name]

AAMC says following up with writers is the applicant's responsibility, so a reminder is normal. Thank the writer when the letter is uploaded.

## Waiving your right to view your letters

In MyERAS, each letter entry asks you to choose Yes or No for "I waive my right to view my Letter of Recommendation." What the 2027 guide says and does not say:

| Point | What the guide says |
|---|---|
| Effect of waiving | Applicants who waive "are not permitted to view or obtain" the letter "under any circumstances." |
| Who can upload | Only entries with the right to view (not waived) may be uploaded by the applicant. Your writer or their designee otherwise uploads. |
| When you decide | When you create the entry. The waiver indicator appears on the Letter Request Form. After you confirm an entry for upload, errors can only be fixed by creating a new entry. |
| Imported letters | A letter imported from a previous season keeps its original viewing rights. These cannot be changed. |
| Whether programs prefer waived letters | Not stated in the guide sections we reviewed, and we could not confirm there whether programs see your choice. |

Practice and opinion: many advisors recommend waiving, because a letter you cannot see tends to be read as more candid. Whatever you choose, the more important decision is the writer. If you waive, you cannot check what they wrote, so ask the question in your request ("Could you write me a strong letter?") and listen for hesitation.

## After the writer agrees

Your statuses in MyERAS go from Not Confirmed for Upload, to Confirmed for Upload, to Uploaded (or Imported from a previous season). You are emailed when a letter is uploaded, and the upload is watermarked with the uploader's name and the date and time. Only uploaded letters can be assigned. AAMC states that assignments to programs you have applied to cannot be changed, and that letters cannot be replaced after they are assigned to an applied program. Programs are not guaranteed to view letters assigned after your initial application, so finish your sets early. The full process is on [letters of recommendation](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation) and the calendar is on the [ERAS timeline](/wiki/application/eras-timeline).

## What is different for IMGs

- **You need an ERAS token before MyERAS works,** so start the [token process](/wiki/ecfmg/eras-token) before you ask writers for uploads.
- **Explain your system.** A home-country writer who describes the grading scale and the student's level of responsibility makes the letter usable to US readers.
- **Use institutional letterhead and contact details.** AAMC's guidelines include them in the writer's checklist, and programs may verify.
- **Be honest about observership limits.** A letter that says "I observed her" is more believable than one that implies independent care she could not have provided.
- **Ask for English.** Programs read English. If a letter is in another language, check each program's instructions about translation.
- **No one can promise you a letter.** Be wary of any placement that promises one. See [turning US clinical experience into a letter](/wiki/clinical-experience/turning-usce-into-a-letter).

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Asking someone who barely supervised you | Choose the person who watched you work |
| Giving a writer only a few days | Ask several weeks ahead, with a clear date |
| Sending one request form to several writers | Each form has a unique, single-use Letter ID |
| Writing or scripting the letter | Send a brief of facts, not prose |
| Assigning a weak fourth letter | Fewer, stronger letters |
| Not tracking statuses | Check MyERAS regularly and remind politely |
| Forgetting to thank the writer | Send a short note once it is uploaded |

A letter of interest or letter of intent to a program is a different document, written by you, not by a recommender. See [letter of interest and letter of intent](/wiki/application/letter-of-interest-and-letter-of-intent).
