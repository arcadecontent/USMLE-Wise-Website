The best ERAS experiences section examples share one habit: each entry tells a program director what you personally did, in what setting and at what scale, in plain words they can scan in seconds. Below are weak and stronger versions of entries for the six experience types [[img|international medical graduates]] use most (work, research, volunteering, US clinical experience, leadership and teaching), then how to write the most meaningful reflections and how to choose which experiences to list. Some applicants still call this the ERAS activities section, and the examples work the same way.

This page owns the examples. The how-to, including every field and how programs see the section, is in [writing the ERAS Experiences section](/wiki/application/eras-experiences-section). Read that first if you have not seen the section in MyERAS yet.

> [!WARNING] Every sample here is an **illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team**. None is a real applicant's text. Do not copy them. Programs may ask about any line you submit, and AAMC treats copying sample text from websites in the personal statement as plagiarism. Copying experience descriptions is a worse idea for the same reason: you cannot defend a line you did not live.

## What the Experiences section asks for

In the {{fact:eras_cycle_year}} MyERAS cycle you can add up to {{fact:eras_experiences_max}} selected experiences and flag up to {{fact:eras_most_meaningful_max}} of them as most meaningful. Each experience has a position title, organization, approximate dates, frequency, location and setting, an experience type, an optional primary focus area and key characteristic, and a description of your activities and responsibilities. Each most meaningful experience adds a short reflection that answers "What made this experience most meaningful?"

The 2027 user guide sets the description at 750 characters and the most meaningful reflection at 300 characters. AAMC changes limits between cycles, so check the limit shown in MyERAS before you finalize anything. Every stronger sample below fits those limits, but your own text with real numbers filled in may not.

## How to read these ERAS experiences section examples

- **Square brackets mark facts only you can supply**, such as the number of patients, your supervisor's role or a study's status. Fill them with real figures you can defend, or cut the sentence.
- Each pair shows a **weak version** and a **stronger version** of the same imaginary entry.
- Each pair ends with what changed. That part is the lesson. The wording is not.
- Where this page says "AAMC says", it is quoting the 2027 user guide (a rule or stated guidance). Where it says "practice", it is what many applicants and advisors do. Where it says "opinion", it is ours.

## Work experience examples

Work covers paid clinical, nonclinical, business and entrepreneurial roles. Many IMGs have a year or more of paid clinical work at home, and programs unfamiliar with your hospital need help seeing the setting and your level of responsibility.

**Entry fields (composite):** Type: Work | Title: Medical officer, emergency department | Organization: [Hospital name] | Frequency: Daily

**Weak version**

> Worked as a medical officer in the emergency department. Saw many patients with different problems. Learned about teamwork, communication and handling stress.

**Stronger version**

> Full-time medical officer in a busy public-hospital emergency department that serves a mostly rural, low-income population. I assessed and stabilized undifferentiated patients, led the first minutes of resuscitations under consultant supervision, and arranged transfers when the hospital lacked a service. After two night shifts with confusing handovers, I wrote a one-page handover checklist that my unit kept using after I left.

**What changed**

- The setting and population are stated, so a reader with no knowledge of your hospital can picture the job.
- "Under consultant supervision" states the level of responsibility without overclaiming.
- The last sentence is a concrete contribution. If you have no true equivalent, leave it out rather than invent one.
- The weak version lists soft skills ("teamwork, communication"). Those are claims. The stronger version gives evidence and lets the reader conclude them.

## Research experience examples

Research entries often fail by saying "assisted with research". Programs want to know your actual role (design, data collection, analysis, writing), the status of the work, and whether you understand it. The same work also goes in Scholarly Work if it produced a poster, abstract or paper, so keep the two consistent. See [how programs view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research) and [authorship and author order](/wiki/research/authorship-and-author-order).

**Entry fields (composite):** Type: Research | Title: Research assistant | Organization: [University hospital] | Frequency: Weekly

**Weak version**

> Research assistant in the department of medicine. Helped with data collection and writing a paper on diabetes. Learned research skills and statistics.

**Stronger version**

> Research assistant on a retrospective cohort study of [condition] outcomes at a university hospital, supervised by [a consultant physician]. I screened charts against the inclusion criteria, built and cleaned the dataset, ran the descriptive analysis with our statistician, and drafted the methods and results. Status: [accepted as a poster at (conference) / manuscript submitted]. I can explain the design and its main limitation: [limitation].

**What changed**

- The study design is named, so the reader can judge the work.
- Four specific tasks replace "helped with".
- The status is stated honestly, and the last sentence signals that you can discuss it in an interview. Programs may check any publication you list.
- If your role was small, say so plainly (for example "data entry and literature search"). An accurate small role beats an inflated one that falls apart in an interview.

## Volunteering and service examples

Volunteer/service/advocacy is for unpaid experiences. Programs read these entries for sustained commitment and for how you treat people, so long, regular service usually says more than a single event.

**Entry fields (composite):** Type: Volunteer/service/advocacy | Title: Volunteer clinic assistant | Organization: [Community clinic] | Frequency: Weekly

**Weak version**

> Volunteer at a free clinic on weekends. Helped patients and learned about healthcare disparities.

**Stronger version**

> Weekly volunteer at a community clinic offering free primary care to people with no insurance or regular doctor. I took vital signs and histories, helped patients understand their medicines in their own language, and called patients who missed follow-up visits. When I noticed that many patients with diabetes stopped treatment when supplies ran out, I helped the clinic pharmacist set up a monthly refill day.

**What changed**

- "Helped patients" became three actions a reader can picture.
- The last sentence shows initiative: you saw a problem and did something specific. It also sets up a strong most meaningful reflection (see below).
- "Learned about healthcare disparities" is a conclusion. The stronger version shows the evidence instead.

## US clinical experience and observership examples

AAMC places structured observerships, clerkships, away rotations and sub-internships under Education/training. Be exact about what you did. Programs distinguish observation from hands-on work, and an inflated description is easy to expose. See [observership vs externship vs hands-on](/wiki/clinical-experience/observership-vs-externship-vs-hands-on) and [how much US clinical experience programs want](/wiki/clinical-experience/how-much-usce-programs-want).

**Entry fields (composite):** Type: Education/training | Title: Observer, inpatient internal medicine | Organization: [US teaching hospital] | Frequency: Daily

**Weak version**

> Observership in internal medicine at a US hospital. Shadowed attendings and residents on rounds. Learned about the US healthcare system and saw many interesting cases.

**Stronger version**

> [N]-week observership on an inpatient internal medicine teaching service, supervised by an attending physician. This was an observation role without independent patient care. I joined rounds daily, followed [N] patients each day from admission to discharge, and presented one case review to the team each week. I wrote practice assessments and plans outside the medical record, and my supervisor reviewed them with me. I learned how the resident, attending and consulting teams share decisions.

**What changed**

- The role is labeled honestly as observation, which builds trust for everything else you claim.
- It shows engagement (following patients, presenting, practicing plans) without implying you treated patients.
- It names the supervisor's role, which supports the letter you hope to get. See [turning USCE into a letter](/wiki/clinical-experience/turning-usce-into-a-letter).
- "Saw many interesting cases" says nothing. A system-level lesson at the end is more useful.

## Leadership examples

Leadership has no type of its own. AAMC lists student government and clubs under "Other extracurricular activity, club, hobby", and societies and associations under "Professional organization". Choose the type that matches what you did, and show the leadership in the description.

**Entry fields (composite):** Type: Other extracurricular activity, club, hobby | Title: President | Organization: [Medical students' society]

**Weak version**

> President of the medical students' society. Organized events and led committee members.

**Stronger version**

> Elected president of my school's medical students' society for one year and led a committee of [N] students. I set the annual calendar, negotiated lecture space with the faculty, and started a mentoring scheme that paired every first-year student with a senior student. When our budget fell short, I redesigned our annual conference as a one-day event instead of canceling it.

**What changed**

- "Elected" and the committee size show the scale of the role.
- Each sentence is a decision or an action, not a duty. Duties (organized events) describe the job. Actions describe you.
- The budget sentence shows problem solving under constraint, which is the quality leadership entries are meant to show.

## Teaching and tutoring examples

Teaching/mentoring covers teaching assistant roles, tutoring and formal instruction of others. Programs use these entries to judge whether you will teach junior colleagues and students, which is part of residency.

**Entry fields (composite):** Type: Teaching/mentoring | Title: Teaching assistant, clinical skills course | Organization: [Medical school] | Frequency: Weekly

**Weak version**

> Teaching assistant. Taught students and helped with exams.

**Stronger version**

> Teaching assistant for the third-year clinical skills course. I ran weekly small-group sessions on history taking and chest examination, observed each student, and gave structured feedback using the course checklist. After students said they got too little hands-on practice, I changed the format so each student examined a practice partner before I demonstrated. I also wrote practice cases for revision sessions.

**Writing about tutoring.** Paid or informal tutoring is a valid entry if you gave it real time. Explain what you actually did, not just that you did it.

**Weak version**

> Tutor. Taught biochemistry to students for money.

**Stronger version**

> Paid tutor for second-year students preparing for [school exam], working with small groups each week. I found each student's weak topics from their practice results, wrote short topic summaries, and tracked what they could explain without notes. I learned to ask students to teach a topic back to me before I moved on.

**What changed**

- Both stronger versions describe a method, not only a topic. A method shows how you teach.
- Feedback and changes you made in response show reflection, which program directors value.
- "For money" is not shameful, but it adds nothing. State the paid role in the experience type and move on to what you did.

## Most meaningful experience examples

AAMC says to focus the reflection on why the experience was meaningful and how it affected you. Programs want introspection. If you tagged a focus area or key characteristic to a most meaningful experience, the reflection should explain why you chose it. Programs see the reflection next to the standard description, so the description still needs to say what you did.

**Weak version**

> This experience was meaningful because I learned a lot about patients and teamwork. It confirmed my wish to become a physician and showed me the importance of caring for others.

**Stronger version (reflecting on the volunteering entry)**

> Watching patients stop treatment because they could not afford refills changed how I think about care: a good plan fails if the patient cannot follow it. I now ask early about cost, language and transport, and I want to build that habit into every clinic visit in residency.

**Stronger version (reflecting on the research entry)**

> Cleaning the dataset taught me how often a tidy chart hides missing information. I became more careful about what a number can and cannot show, and more comfortable saying "I do not know yet." I want to bring that habit to quality improvement work in residency.

**What changed**

- The weak version could describe anyone's experience. The stronger versions contain a detail only this person could write (refills, a tidy chart).
- Each stronger version follows the same three steps: what changed in how you think or act, the moment behind it, and how you will carry it into residency.
- Neither repeats the description. The reflection adds meaning to the facts above it.

## Position title, organization and dates

These fields look administrative, but programs read them first.

| Situation | What to write (practice) |
|---|---|
| Official job or role | The title you officially held and the organization that employed or ran it. |
| Informal or unofficial role | A plain descriptive title, such as "Volunteer clinic assistant", rather than an invented formal title. |
| Several organizations involved | The one that ran the activity, with the others named in the description. |
| Family business or caregiving | A plain descriptive title (for example "Assistant manager, family pharmacy") and the setting. AAMC lists business and entrepreneurial roles under Work, and gives serving as a caregiver while working or in school as an example for the optional Impactful Experiences question, so decide where it fits better. |
| Local titles that US programs may not know | Your real title, with a short explanation of the level of responsibility in the description. |

Dates should match your CV, MSPE and transcript. A mismatch invites a question you did not plan for. See the [MyERAS walkthrough](/wiki/application/myeras-walkthrough).

## Hobbies and interests examples

AAMC gives a short free-text field for this in the {{fact:eras_cycle_year}} cycle (its limit is shown in MyERAS) and says that a hobby you devoted significant time and effort to can also be one of your experiences.

**Weak version**

> Reading, movies, traveling, music.

**Better version**

> Cooking regional dishes from my grandmother's recipes, long-distance running, and playing violin in a community orchestra. Ask me about my worst recipe ever.

**Why it works:** specific interests give interviewers an easy, friendly opening. Opinion: casual hobbies are fine if they are true and you enjoy talking about them. Do not list anything you would not want to discuss on interview day.

## Impactful Experiences examples (optional question)

This optional question is for major challenges or obstacles you overcame, from any point in your life, such as family or financial background, community setting, limited access to education or serving as a caregiver. AAMC says programs do not expect everyone to answer, that what you write should not be the same as your personal statement, and that anything you share may be discussed in interviews. Write only what you are comfortable discussing, and check the limit shown in MyERAS.

**Weak version**

> I faced many challenges growing up and overcame them through hard work. This made me the person I am today.

**Stronger version**

> I was the first person in my family to attend university. I worked evenings in a family shop through most of medical school to cover costs, and I learned to study in short blocks between customers. That routine is why I am comfortable with long, interrupted days on call. I mention it because it shaped how I manage time.

**What changed**

- The weak version names no obstacle and shows no effect. The stronger one gives one fact, one habit it built and one link to residency.
- It does not ask for sympathy and does not repeat a personal statement story. If this experience is your whole personal statement (see [personal statement examples](/wiki/application/personal-statement-examples)), choose a different angle for one of them.
- Practice: do not use this box to explain an exam failure or a gap. Those need a short, factual explanation elsewhere. See the [personal statement guide](/wiki/application/personal-statement-guide).

## How to choose your most meaningful experiences

Choosing is harder than writing. AAMC says to consider your experiences "as a complete set" and notes that programs are not looking for one type of applicant. A method that works:

1. **Make a master list** of everything you did since medical school started, with dates, supervisors and what you personally did. Do not filter yet.
2. **Score each one** on the questions in the table below.
3. **Cover different sides of you.** A balanced set usually includes a clinical anchor (US experience or a home-country clinical role), research if you have it, service, teaching or leadership, and one human entry such as a long-term hobby or caregiving role.
4. **Pick your three most meaningful** from experiences you can discuss for several minutes, that show different qualities, and that add insight beyond your personal statement and MSPE.
5. **Merge small related activities** into one entry. Drop anything you cannot discuss.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How much time did I give it? | Sustained commitment reads as stronger than a single event. |
| What was my level of responsibility? | Programs want to see what you personally did, not what the team did. |
| Can I discuss it for two minutes without notes? | Interviewers may ask about any entry. |
| Does it show something the rest of my application does not? | AAMC advises that most meaningful descriptions should not repeat your MSPE or personal statement. |
| Is it different from my other entries? | A set of three similar entries wastes two slots. |

**A composite portfolio.** Here is how an imaginary applicant might arrange eight entries. It is a structure example, not a target. You do not have to fill all {{fact:eras_experiences_max}} slots.

| # | Type | Short title | Most meaningful? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Education/training | Observer, inpatient internal medicine (US) | Yes |
| 2 | Work | Medical officer, emergency department | |
| 3 | Research | Research assistant, retrospective cohort study | Yes |
| 4 | Volunteer/service/advocacy | Weekly volunteer, community clinic | Yes |
| 5 | Teaching/mentoring | Teaching assistant, clinical skills | |
| 6 | Other extracurricular activity, club, hobby | President, medical students' society | |
| 7 | Professional organization | Member, national medical students' association | |
| 8 | Other extracurricular activity, club, hobby | Long-distance running | |

> [!IMG TIP] If a US clinical experience gave you a strong supervisor, consider flagging it as most meaningful and make sure your letter from that site is strong. Entries and letters support each other when the details match.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Listing duties ("responsible for patient care") | Describe two or three things you did and one result. |
| Calling observation hands-on work | Say what your role was. Programs distinguish the two. |
| Padding all {{fact:eras_experiences_max}} slots with trivial entries | Fewer, real entries read better. |
| Copying the same paragraph into your personal statement, CV and experiences | Add insight each time. AAMC says overlap in topics is fine but each section should add something. |
| Using unexplained local terms | Spell out abbreviations and translate local titles. |
| Dates that differ from your MSPE or transcript | Check every date against official documents. |
| Pasting text from a formatted document | Draft in a plain text editor first, then paste. |

## What is different for IMGs

- **Explain the setting.** Programs may not know your hospital, school or health system. One clause on setting and scale helps more than adjectives.
- **State your level of responsibility.** Titles such as intern, house officer or medical officer mean different things in different countries.
- **Be exact about US experience.** Observation, externship and hands-on roles are different things to a US program director.
- **Do not hide real work.** Years of independent clinical work at home are an asset. Describe them concretely.
- **Keep dates consistent.** If a gap is visible, see [gap years and how to explain them](/wiki/application/gap-years-and-how-to-explain) and keep the explanation short and factual.

## Next step

Keep one master list of facts, draft your entries in a plain text editor, and read each one aloud. If an entry could describe anyone else's experience, add a detail only you know.
