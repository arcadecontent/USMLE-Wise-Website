Yes, you can apply to more than one specialty, and many applicants do. **Each specialty is its own set of applications, with its own fees, signals and tailored documents, but you rank everything on one NRMP rank list.** The mechanics are manageable; the hard parts are cost, time and telling two honest stories.

## How common it is

AMA, citing AAMC ERAS data, reports that about 42% of applicants applied to more than one specialty in the 2026 cycle. Among IMGs the share was about 19.2%. Cross-applicants submitted more applications on average (130.5 versus 114.3 for IMGs overall). Internal medicine, family medicine and transitional year were the most common specialties applicants cross-applied to. This is descriptive data, not proof that cross-applying helps or hurts.

## How ERAS handles it

### Fees

The AAMC 2027 guide says fees are based on the number of programs applied to per specialty, and pricing resets with each additional specialty. Two specialties means two fee schedules. Check the current rates on AAMC's page, and see [ERAS and NRMP costs](/wiki/application/eras-and-match-costs).

### Personal statements

The guide says personal statements may be used to personalize your application for a program or for different specialties. You can create as many as you like and assign each to the right programs. Plan one statement per specialty. A single statement trying to serve two fields usually serves neither. See the [personal statement guide](/wiki/application/personal-statement-guide).

### Letters of recommendation

When you request a letter, you can select the specialty it will be assigned to. The field helps you organize and is visible only to you, your Designated Dean's Office and the letter author, not to programs. If a letter works for several specialties, choose "General." Some specialties use standardized letters, and the options differ by specialty. See [letters of recommendation](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation).

### Specialty questions

Some specialties require specialty-specific questions before you can apply. Responses are visible only to programs in that specialty.

### Program signals

AAMC says you can send program signals for each specialty to which you intend to apply, and that each participating specialty gets a fixed number. The signal count screen shows Available, Assigned and Applied for each specialty, and AAMC advises using the maximum allotted signals per specialty. Participation by specialty and by program is optional. See [signaling explained](/wiki/application/program-signaling-explained).

### What each program can see

We did not find an official statement from AAMC on what a program can see about your applications to other specialties. Do not assume it cannot be known. Interviewers may ask why you applied to another specialty, and some institutions restrict applicants to one specialty. Read each program's rules.

## How NRMP handles it

- **One primary list.** NRMP says you can rank programs across multiple specialties. The algorithm tries to place you in the most preferred program on your list "regardless of the specialty of the program."
- **Rank by preference.** NRMP's guidance is to rank by preference, not by where you expect to match, and to rank only programs you would accept.
- **Alternate specialty.** If you are in a competitive field and would want a residency even if you do not match in your first choice, NRMP suggests also ranking your most preferred programs in an alternate specialty.
- **Preliminary and advanced programs.** Rules are different for programs that start at PGY-2. See [preliminary and transitional years](/wiki/application/preliminary-and-transitional-years).
- **Match is binding.** If you match, you are committed to that program. See [how the Match algorithm works](/wiki/interviews-match/how-the-match-algorithm-works).

> [!KEY] You rank by true preference across specialties. If you would prefer a categorical position in your backup specialty over a risky place in your first choice, put it higher. NRMP states the algorithm favors your preference order, so the list is where your real priorities appear.

## When it makes sense

| Situation | Reading |
|---|---|
| First choice is highly competitive for IMGs | A second specialty can be a real hedge |
| Scores or attempts limit the first choice | Choose a second specialty whose screening you meet |
| You are truly torn between two fields | Reasonable, but resources double |
| Two fields chosen only to apply more widely | Often weak; each statement will read thin |
| You need visa support | Check sponsorship in both specialties first |

NRMP's 2024 data for US seniors, reported by AMA, show matched seniors ranked about 1.2 specialties on average and unmatched about 1.6. This is an association; it does not show that fewer specialties cause a match. IMG data are in NRMP's IMG Charting Outcomes.

## Costs and trade-offs

- **Money.** Applications, transcripts and interview travel multiply. Check [ERAS and NRMP costs](/wiki/application/eras-and-match-costs) before deciding.
- **Time.** Two statements, two sets of letters, and interview preparation for each specialty. AMA notes the added work.
- **Interview season.** Overlapping interviews can mean choosing between days.
- **Consistency.** You must be able to explain both choices honestly. Interviewers will ask.
- **Programs may weigh commitment.** Some may be sceptical of unclear interest. Your signals and statement help show commitment to each specialty, so use them honestly.

## An honest plan

1. Decide your first-choice specialty and why.
2. Decide whether a second specialty is a real fit, not only a hedge. See [choosing a backup specialty](/wiki/specialties/backup-specialties).
3. Check program rules for both: attempts, years since graduation, visa, USCE.
4. Write a separate statement for each.
5. Assign letters carefully, using "General" only for letters that really work in both.
6. Allocate signals in each specialty.
7. Build one rank list by preference. See [rank order list strategy](/wiki/interviews-match/rank-order-list-strategy).

> [!IMG TIP] If an interviewer asks about your other specialty, answer in one honest sentence about your interest in both and why this program fits. Do not tell each program it is your only choice if it is not.

> [!WARNING] Do not say you are applying only to one specialty if you are applying to two, and do not misstate facts in either application. The ERAS application is certified, and AAMC investigates allegations of fraudulent or unethical conduct.
