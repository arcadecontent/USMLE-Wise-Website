A residency letter of interest is a short email you send a program before it invites you to interview, and a letter of intent is a message you send after the interview that tells one program it is your top choice. Neither is required, NRMP does not ban them, and several specialties discourage them, so check each program's stated policy before you send anything.

This page explains the difference, what NRMP actually says, who to address and when, and gives illustrative templates for international medical graduates (IMGs). It owns the templates. The wider rules about contact after interviews are in [post-interview communication](/wiki/interviews-match/post-interview-communication), and the formal way to show interest in your application is covered in [program signaling explained](/wiki/application/program-signaling-explained).

> [!NOTE] Every letter on this page is an illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team. None is a real applicant's text, and no names in them are real. Do not copy them. Programs can receive many similar messages, and a letter that could have been written by anyone does little for you.

## Letter of interest vs letter of intent

The two documents are often mixed up, and the difference matters because specialties treat them differently.

| | Letter of interest | Letter of intent |
|---|---|---|
| When | Before the program has invited you to interview | After you have interviewed |
| What it says | Why you want this program and what you bring to it | That this program is your first choice, if it truly is |
| Main risk | Looks like pressure or a form letter, and many programs say they do not want pre-interview outreach | Reads as a bid for a rank position, which NRMP does not allow either side to solicit |
| Other names | Pre-interview email, expression of interest | Post-interview "first choice" message |

A third kind of message is easy to forget: a **short update** after you submit, such as a new exam result, publication or clinical experience. Some specialty guides, such as family medicine's, make room for updates even where they discourage letters of interest.

## What NRMP says (rule)

Rule means what the official body requires. These points come from the NRMP Match Participation Agreement for applicants and the NRMP Match Code of Conduct for applicants.

- **You may express interest.** Applicants may voluntarily communicate their interest to a program. The code says applicants may freely express interest in a program.
- **You may not solicit a commitment.** Applicants may not ask a program for a verbal or written statement implying a commitment to rank them. In the same way, a program may not solicit a statement from you implying a commitment to rank the program.
- **Programs cannot make your list depend on a statement.** Programs may not ask you to reveal your ranking preferences, other programs you applied to or interviewed with, or your signals. They may not suggest that your place on a rank order list depends on a written or verbal statement of your preference.
- **After interviews, keep contact limited.** You may exchange clarifying information, but you may not engage in post-interview communication to influence or find out a program's ranking intentions.
- **What you say must be accurate.** You are responsible for the completeness, timeliness and accuracy of what you tell programs. Misleading statements can be treated as violations.
- **Matching is binding.** A match creates a binding commitment on both sides. Your rank list should reflect your true preferences. A letter saying a program is your first choice does not change how the algorithm works and does not bind either side.

NRMP's documents do not mention letters of interest or letters of intent by name. They set limits on what each side may ask for or require. The AMA, in its Match communication guidance, notes that NRMP rules do not expressly prohibit an applicant from sending a message of this kind. That is why program and specialty guidance, not NRMP, usually decides whether a letter is wise.

> [!WARNING] Match violations are reported to NRMP and investigated. Telling several programs each is your first choice would be inaccurate, and asking a program to confirm where you rank is soliciting a commitment, which the rules do not allow. For how the algorithm treats your list, see [how the Match algorithm works](/wiki/interviews-match/how-the-match-algorithm-works).

## Should you send one? (practice)

Practice means what specialties and programs ask for. It is not an NRMP rule, and it changes each cycle.

The Program Directors Association Guides for Residency Applicants (published through CMSS with the Organization of Program Directors Associations, AAMC and AMA) give specialty-specific advice on applicant communication. They vary. In the current guides we checked:

- **Diagnostic radiology:** pre-interview letters of interest are generally not encouraged, and signals are largely the preferred way to show interest. Post-interview communication is generally not required, and programs usually give guidance.
- **Family medicine:** pre-interview communication should generally be confined to the application, though applicants may notify programs of major updates. Applicants are discouraged from sending letters of interest or letters of intent to rank. A program may not respond to messages.
- **Other specialties:** guidance ranges from "discouraged" to "follow the program's lead", and some specialties ask programs to publish their own preferences on their websites. The AMA's summary of the guides names dermatology, emergency medicine, internal medicine and orthopaedic surgery among the specialties that strongly discourage post-interview communication.

Treat that list as a sample, not a complete map. Open the current guide for your specialty and read the "applicant communications" section before you write a word.

> [!NOTE] Opinion (USMLE Wise team): for most applicants the strongest move is to spend your signals well and keep your application accurate and up to date. A letter helps most when it carries something a signal cannot: a real connection, an update, or a reason the program could not guess from your application.

### A quick decision guide

| Situation | What we suggest (opinion) |
|---|---|
| The program's website says it does not accept letters or outreach | Do not send one |
| Your specialty's guide discourages letters of interest or intent | Do not send one. Use signals and your application instead |
| The program invites contact or says it welcomes expressions of interest | A short letter of interest is reasonable |
| You have a real update (new result, publication, completed clinical experience) | Send a brief update only if the program accepts updates |
| You have a genuine tie to the program or region and no policy is published | Optional. One short message, easy to ignore |
| You interviewed, the program is truly your first choice, and the program or specialty allows it | A short, honest message is optional. Do not ask where you rank |
| A letter would simply repeat your personal statement | Skip it |

For IMGs, one extra point: a letter cannot change a program's published requirements, such as exam, years since graduation or visa sponsorship. Read the requirements first. See [program screening filters](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters) and [identifying IMG-friendly programs](/wiki/application/identifying-img-friendly-programs).

## Who do you send a letter of interest to?

Follow the program's published policy first. In order:

1. **The contact the program names.** Many programs list an applicant contact or a coordinator email on their website or in the interview invitation. Use exactly that.
2. **The program coordinator.** The AMA's guidance notes that logistics and scheduling questions are appropriate for program coordinators, and coordinators are usually the route programs expect.
3. **The program director.** Address the letter to the program director by name if the program says to write to the director, or if the coordinator's contact page directs applicant letters to the director.

Opinion: if the program publishes nothing at all, a single short message to the coordinator, addressed to the program director, is the lowest-risk route. Programs differ, so this is not a rule.

Do not:

- Email several faculty members, the chief residents or current residents with the same request.
- Message people through social media or personal phone numbers.
- Send the letter again if there is no reply. A program that does not reply has usually decided not to.

Find the director's correct name and title on the program's website. A wrong name is the fastest way to look careless.

## When should you send a letter of interest?

- **After your ERAS application is submitted.** The program needs your application in front of it to give the letter context. See the [ERAS timeline](/wiki/application/eras-timeline) for the season.
- **Before or around the time the program reviews applications and sends invitations,** and only if the program accepts pre-interview contact.
- **Once.** One message per program. If the program gives a date after which it no longer reads messages, respect it.
- **A letter of intent, if you send one at all, goes after the interview,** while programs are still building their lists. Check the NRMP calendar for your cycle. Do not send letters after your list is certified expecting them to change anything.

## Residency letter of interest example (pre-interview)

A structure we suggest (opinion): who you are and that you applied, one genuine reason for this program, one link to your experience, and a polite close that expects nothing. Aim for about 100 to 150 words.

**Template 1: Letter of interest based on a real connection** (illustrative composite from the USMLE Wise team)

> **Subject:** Interest in [specialty] residency at [program name], [your name], AAMC ID [number]
>
> Dear Dr. [Last name],
>
> I am an international medical graduate from [school, country] and have applied to your [specialty] program this season. I am writing to say why [program name] is on my list. I understand that you may be unable to reply.
>
> During my [observership or elective] in [city], I [one specific thing you did or learned]. That experience is the reason I am drawn to the way your program [one real, verifiable feature from the website, for example its continuity clinic in a community setting]. It matches the work I did in [your own experience].
>
> My full application is in ERAS. Thank you for considering it.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your name], MD
> [School and graduation year], [email], [phone]

**Template 2: Short application update** (illustrative composite)

> **Subject:** Application update, [your name], AAMC ID [number]
>
> Dear [Ms. or Mr. or Dr. Last name],
>
> I applied to your [specialty] program this season. Since I submitted, I have [one concrete update: a new exam result, an accepted publication, a completed clinical experience]. I wanted you to have this information. It is not in my submitted application, and I am happy to send the document if you would like it.
>
> Thank you for your time.
>
> [Your name], AAMC ID [number]

**Template 3: Geographic or personal tie** (illustrative composite)

> **Subject:** Interest in [program name], [your name], AAMC ID [number]
>
> Dear Dr. [Last name],
>
> I applied to your [specialty] program. I am writing because of a connection that my application does not show: my [family member or partner] lives in [city or region], and I plan to settle there. I have also [one concrete link, such as a rotation or work in the region].
>
> I selected [program name] because [one real feature of the program]. Thank you for considering my application.
>
> [Your name], AAMC ID [number]

Use Template 3 only for a tie that is real. If you list a geographic preference in ERAS, make sure it agrees with the letter. See the [MyERAS walkthrough](/wiki/application/myeras-walkthrough) for how geographic preferences work.

### Adapting the letter to your specialty

Keep the same shape, and change only the real reason. For internal medicine, the reason might be a patient-care or training-structure feature the program publishes. For anesthesiology, it might be a particular clinical setting or teaching approach you have seen firsthand. Never invent a feature. If you cannot name one from the website or from time you spent there, the letter will read as generic, so send nothing.

## Letter of intent for residency: example (post-interview)

A letter of intent says one thing the other messages do not: that a program is your top choice. It is only appropriate when all of these are true: the program or specialty allows it, you will rank the program first, and you are not asking anything of the program in return.

**Template 4: Letter of intent (only if true and allowed)** (illustrative composite from the USMLE Wise team)

> **Subject:** Thank you, [your name], AAMC ID [number]
>
> Dear Dr. [Last name],
>
> Thank you for the chance to interview for [specialty] at [program name] on [date]. I enjoyed meeting [name one person] and hearing about [one specific thing discussed].
>
> After the interview, I am even more interested in your program because [one honest reason tied to what you learned]. [Program name] is my first choice, and I intend to rank it first.
>
> I understand that this message does not obligate either of us and that I should not expect a reply. Thank you again.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your name]

If it is not true, or the program or specialty says not to send one, use the safer note instead.

**Template 5: Post-interview note of continued interest, with no ranking statement** (illustrative composite)

> **Subject:** Thank you, [your name], AAMC ID [number]
>
> Dear Dr. [Last name],
>
> Thank you for speaking with me on [date]. Our conversation about [specific topic] made me more interested in [program name], especially [one specific aspect]. I appreciated the chance to interview.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your name]

For the rules on thank-you notes and what programs may and may not ask you after an interview, see [post-interview communication](/wiki/interviews-match/post-interview-communication). The same page covers what to do if a program asks you to commit.

## How a letter differs from a preference signal

A program signal is a formal, limited, ERAS-recorded way to show interest at the moment you apply. A letter is an informal message that any program can ignore. They are not substitutes.

| | Program signal | Letter of interest |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Inside ERAS, set when you apply | Email to a program |
| How many | A fixed number per specialty and cycle | Unlimited, but each costs credibility |
| Who sees it | Only programs you signal and apply to | Whoever opens the email |
| Is it standardized? | Yes: programs may filter by it | No: it depends on who reads it |
| Can you add a reason? | In a few specialties, a short signal explanation | Yes, but keep it brief |
| Does the specialty endorse it? | AAMC advises using all your signals | Varies by specialty; many discourage |

Do not use a letter to restate a signal. "I signaled your program" tells the reader nothing new. And do not use the signal explanation field, where ERAS offers one, to promise that you will rank a program first; the signaling guide covers that rule. See [program signaling explained](/wiki/application/program-signaling-explained) for how many signals you get and how to spend them.

> [!KEY] A signal tells a program where you want to interview. A letter of interest, at its best, tells a program something specific about you and that program that your application does not show. If you have nothing to add, add nothing.

## What not to do

- **Do not ask where you rank,** or ask the program to say you are on its list. NRMP bars soliciting a commitment, and a program's reply does not bind it anyway.
- **Do not tell several programs each is your first choice.** It is dishonest, it is a Match risk, and it does not change your rank list.
- **Do not send a letter the program has said it does not accept.**
- **Do not email many people at one program,** or follow up repeatedly.
- **Do not use a mass-mail template** with the wrong program name, wrong specialty or wrong city left in.
- **Do not name other programs or offers** as leverage. It can read as pressure, and programs may not ask where else you applied or interviewed.
- **Do not restate your CV or personal statement.** The program has them.
- **Do not exaggerate or invent** a tie, rotation or feature. Accuracy is your obligation under the Match agreement.
- **Do not use a letter of interest to explain a visa issue, an exam attempt or a gap.** Those belong in your application and are answered accurately in the interview. For how to answer them, see [interview questions with sample answers](/wiki/interviews-match/interview-questions-with-sample-answers).
- **Avoid flowery or deferential wording** such as "I humbly request your esteemed consideration." Opinion: plain, direct, polite English usually reads better to US program staff.

> [!IMG TIP] If your English is not your first language, write plainly and read the message aloud before you send it. A short, clear message of three or four sentences is stronger than a long formal one.

## A practical checklist

1. Check the program's website and your specialty's current applicant-communication guide.
2. Decide whether you have something real to say that a signal and your application do not.
3. Find the correct recipient and spelling of the name.
4. Write 100 to 150 words (opinion). Include your name and AAMC ID in the subject line and signature.
5. Read it aloud. Remove anything you could not defend if a program asked you about it.
6. Send once. Move on.
7. Keep a record of what you sent, in the same tracker you use for invitations. See [managing interview invitations](/wiki/interviews-match/managing-interview-invitations).

Letters of interest sit next to your [letters of recommendation](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation) in the application, but they are a different thing: a letter of recommendation is written by someone else, and a letter of interest is your own message to a program.
