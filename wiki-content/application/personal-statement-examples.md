The most useful residency personal statement examples are annotated ones: you read a complete statement, then see what it does well, what you would change and what an international medical graduate ([[img|IMG]]) should watch for. Below are three illustrative statements in three different structures, written by the USMLE Wise team, followed by the mistakes we see most and a short section on points that are specific to IMGs.

This page owns the examples. For the how-to (what a statement is for, a working structure and the ERAS mechanics), start with the [personal statement guide](/wiki/application/personal-statement-guide). If you are applying to internal medicine, see the [internal medicine personal statement examples](/wiki/application/internal-medicine-personal-statement-examples) too.

> [!WARNING] Every statement on this page is an **illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team**. None was written by a real applicant, none is a real person's story, and no hospital, program, school or mentor in them is real. **Do not copy these.** AAMC tells applicants not to copy information from example statements found on websites. In our opinion, a borrowed paragraph is also easy for a reader to spot, because it does not sound like the rest of your application.

## How to use residency personal statement examples without copying them

Examples are good teachers and bad templates. Use them like this:

1. **Read once for structure only.** Ask where the opening begins, where the specialty first appears, how the middle paragraphs are ordered and what the last two sentences do. Ignore the topic.
2. **Read again for detail.** Notice how much work one small, concrete detail does compared with a general claim.
3. **Read the annotation and check yourself.** Decide which of the problems it lists also apply to your draft.
4. **Close the page and write from your own notes.** List five real moments from your clinical work, research, teaching or life that shaped your interest in the specialty. Write about the one you remember most clearly.
5. **Compare only structure afterwards.** Once you have a rough draft, return to an example to check shape, not wording.
6. **Delete any sentence you did not write.** If a phrase from an example is stuck in your head, rewrite it with your own facts or cut it.

| Use examples for | Do not use examples for |
|---|---|
| Seeing different ways to open and close | Copying or lightly rewording sentences |
| Learning how a specific detail carries a paragraph | Importing a story that is not yours |
| Spotting clichés in your own draft | Choosing your specialty or your theme |
| Calibrating tone and length | Treating one structure as the only correct one |

## What these examples have in common (practice and opinion)

There is no official format, so everything here is our opinion drawn from common advising practice, not a rule. All three statements below do the same few things:

- They open with something specific instead of a quotation or a general claim about medicine.
- They give a concrete reason for the specialty, not "I like helping people."
- They show character through what the writer did, not through adjectives.
- They are honest about what the writer does not yet know.
- They end plainly, with a sentence about the kind of training and colleague the writer wants to be.
- They are written in short, clear sentences that survive being read quickly.

The three statements use different specialties on purpose, to show that the structure transfers while the content does not. If your specialty is internal medicine, the [internal medicine examples](/wiki/application/internal-medicine-personal-statement-examples) apply the same ideas to that field.

## Three structures at a glance

| Example | Specialty shown | Structure | Works best when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Family medicine | Opens on a clinical moment | You have a vivid, specific patient memory | Turning one story into a lecture |
| 2 | Psychiatry | Path and purpose | Your route to the specialty was not direct | Spending too long on the journey |
| 3 | Neurology | Mentor and research driven | Research or a mentor shaped how you think | Overclaiming your role |

## Example 1: A clinical-moment opening (family medicine)

*Illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team. Not a real applicant's statement. Do not copy.*

> The woman in bed six had been admitted three times in two months with the same problem, and each time she went home with a new prescription and no clearer idea of why she kept coming back. In my third week as a house officer on the medical ward of a district hospital, I was told to discharge her again. Before I did, I sat down and asked what happened in the days before she got sick. She told me she had stopped taking her blood pressure tablets when the bus fare to the clinic became more than the cost of the tablets.
>
> That conversation changed what I thought my job was. The diagnosis had never been the problem. The problem was everything between the hospital and her kitchen table. For the rest of that rotation I asked one more question of every patient I discharged: what will make this hard to follow at home? I started writing the answer at the top of the discharge note, above the medication list, where the next doctor would see it first.
>
> After graduation I worked for two years in a primary care clinic serving farming families and a growing number of older people living alone. In one morning I might manage diabetes follow-up, an antenatal visit and a vaccination clinic. I learned to value the patient I would see again in three months more than the one I would never see again. When a child I had treated for asthma returned, it was because her mother trusted that I would remember the inhaler problem from last time. Family medicine is the only specialty where that kind of memory is the whole point.
>
> In the United States I completed clinical rotations in an outpatient teaching clinic where residents followed the same patients across years. I watched a second-year resident rebuild a diabetes plan around a patient's night-shift schedule, and I recognized the habit I had started on that ward: treat the circumstances, not only the disease. Those weeks also showed me what I still need to learn. I have not yet worked with shared decision making through an interpreter, or with the insurance barriers my patients here will face. I want a residency that teaches those skills directly, in a community setting, with a continuity clinic from the first year.
>
> I will bring reliability and a habit of noticing who is missing from the clinic schedule. I hope to practice in a community that does not have enough primary care doctors, where the woman in bed six, wherever she lives, can reach a doctor who remembers her.

### Annotation: Example 1

**What works**

- The opening is a single patient and a single decision, told plainly. The reader learns the writer's values from an action (asking one more question), not from an adjective.
- The change in thinking is stated in one sentence ("the diagnosis had never been the problem") and then proved with a repeatable habit (the note above the medication list).
- The middle ties the specialty to the work: continuity, mixed clinic days, the same patient again in three months.
- The writer names what they still need to learn. That reads as self-awareness, not weakness.

**What to change**

- "Family medicine is the only specialty where that kind of memory is the whole point" is an overreach. Faculty in other fields would disagree, and it is a mild criticism of other specialties. Replace it with what you value, for example "a specialty built on knowing the same patients over time."
- The last paragraph is a little generic. Name one specific thing the writer wants to do, such as a continuity clinic with a particular patient population, so it could not be pasted into another application.
- The phrase "wherever she lives" is a soft way of avoiding a commitment, which is sensible. If you do mention where you intend to practice, only say what you are certain about.

**What an IMG should watch for**

- Keep hardship in service of the specialty story. Here the cost of a bus fare is a clinical insight, not a plea for sympathy.
- Describe your US role exactly as it appears in ERAS. "Completed clinical rotations" must match the role, setting and dates you list in the [Experiences section](/wiki/application/eras-experiences-section).
- The reader sees years of practice in the middle paragraph. If you have been out of school for some time, this is how you show you are clinically current. See the [gap years guide](/wiki/application/gap-years-and-how-to-explain).

## Example 2: A path-and-purpose narrative (psychiatry)

*Illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team. Not a real applicant's statement. Do not copy.*

> I did not choose psychiatry. I arrived at it by following one question through three different jobs. The question was why some patients get better when the treatment is the same.
>
> In medical school I assumed I would train in emergency medicine. I liked the pace and the clarity. As an intern in a busy emergency department I noticed a group of patients who came back again and again with chest pain, palpitations and dizziness that every test failed to explain. The senior doctors were kind to them, but the visit ended when the results came back normal. I began staying a few minutes longer. Most of these patients were not asking for another test. They were asking whether anyone had noticed how frightened they were.
>
> To understand what I was missing, I took a six-month post on the inpatient psychiatry service of a general hospital. There I saw what a good interview can do. A senior psychiatrist spent a full morning with a man who had not spoken to anyone for days. By noon he was describing the loss that had started everything. She did not use a trick. She used attention, structure and patience, and she wrote it all down clearly enough that the next clinician could continue where she stopped.
>
> After graduation I spent a year preparing for the USMLE Steps while working part-time as a research assistant on a study of depression screening in primary care. I helped collect and enter data and sat in on team meetings. The study taught me how much illness goes unseen because no one asks, and how a short, well-chosen question can change a patient's path.
>
> I am looking for a residency that gives me strong supervision in psychotherapy and in medication management, and a wide range of patients, including those with serious medical illness alongside their psychiatric illness. I would like to learn consultation work in a general hospital, where the two meet.
>
> My strengths are listening, careful documentation and follow-through. I do not need my patients to be easy. I want to be the doctor who stays a few minutes longer and knows what to do with that time.

### Annotation: Example 2

**What works**

- The path is organized around one question, so the three jobs read as a single story instead of a list.
- The first paragraph states the answer plainly. A reader skimming in under two minutes still gets the point.
- The writer shows how their view of psychiatry formed through an observation (a senior psychiatrist's interview), not through a general claim about the field.
- The year after graduation is mentioned in one sentence with what the writer did, which is the right way to handle a quiet stretch.
- The ending names the training the writer wants (supervision in psychotherapy, consultation work) without flattery.

**What to change**

- The path takes up most of the page. A reader should meet the specialty by the second paragraph. Trim the emergency department section by a few sentences.
- The sentence about wanting a "wide range of patients" is vague. Add one specific population or setting the writer has already worked with.
- The final line repeats the earlier phrase "a few minutes longer". A repeated image can work, but only if the ending adds something new, such as what the writer would do with the time.

**What an IMG should watch for**

- If you change specialty, show the bridge from your prior experience to this one, as this statement does, instead of only announcing the destination. A reader should not wonder whether you are applying out of necessity.
- Do not use the path paragraphs to explain why you left your country. Keep the motivation tied to the training you want.
- Give the post-graduation year a clear, factual line here and the dates in ERAS. See [gap years and how to explain them](/wiki/application/gap-years-and-how-to-explain).

## Example 3: A mentor and research-driven narrative (neurology)

*Illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team. Not a real applicant's statement. Do not copy.*

> For eighteen months I measured how stroke survivors recovered movement in their hands, and for most of that time I thought I was doing a research project. I understood what I was really doing only when a patient asked me, after a long assessment, whether his hand would ever hold a cup again. I realized I could answer him with more honesty than I could have a year earlier.
>
> The project was led by a neurologist at my university hospital who ran a small rehabilitation research group. She gave me a specific job: I was responsible for the standardized motor assessments at each follow-up visit and for keeping scoring consistent between assessors. It was unglamorous work, and she checked it closely. Early on she returned a set of my scoring sheets with half the entries circled. My scores were not wrong, she said, but I had not written down why I had given each one. Since then I write my reasoning beside every judgment I make, in research and in the clinic.
>
> By the end of the study I had helped prepare a conference abstract and contributed to a manuscript draft. I was not the lead investigator and I do not claim to be. What I took from the work was a way of examining patients: reliable observation first, interpretation second.
>
> That habit shaped my clinical rotations. On the neurology ward I was most at ease at the bedside, doing the long, detailed examination that others found tedious: tone, power, reflexes and the way a patient walks into the room. I was also the one who sat with families afterward, because the uncertainty of neurological illness is as hard to carry as the disability.
>
> I am applying to neurology because I want a career that combines careful localization at the bedside with the slow work of understanding recovery. I would like to continue clinical research in stroke rehabilitation, but I know that residency comes first and that clinical competence is the priority. I am looking for a program with strong clinical supervision and, for those who want it, protected time to keep a project going.

### Annotation: Example 3

**What works**

- The research is not presented as a trophy. The writer describes a specific job, a specific correction from the mentor and a change in habit that carried into clinical work.
- The writer states their actual role ("I was not the lead investigator and I do not claim to be"). Honest scope makes everything else more believable.
- The research is linked back to the bedside in the fourth paragraph. Without that link, a reader may wonder if the writer wants a research career more than a clinical one.
- The close puts clinical competence first and research second. In our opinion, that ordering is reassuring to a reader choosing a resident for clinical training.

**What to change**

- Add a one-line sense of the result or the lesson of the project, in plain terms, without inflating it. As written, the reader learns about the process but not what the group found.
- "Slow work of understanding recovery" is a nice phrase but abstract. Replace it with a concrete example from a rotation.
- Check that every item mentioned appears in your ERAS Experiences and publications entries with accurate dates, and describe the status of any manuscript exactly as it is today.

**What an IMG should watch for**

- Describe your authorship accurately. Only list or claim what you can document, and keep your wording identical across the statement, the CV entries and your interview answers.
- Do not name a mentor to impress. Describe what they taught you. If you are asking this person for a letter, the statement and the letter should tell a consistent story. See [letters of recommendation](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation).
- Research helps some applications but does not replace clinical evidence. If you have a lot of research, the statement still has to show clinical readiness.

## Common mistakes in residency personal statements

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starting with a quotation, a dramatic hook or "ever since I was a child" | Programs read many of these, and it says nothing specific about you | Start with the first real moment you can describe in two or three plain sentences |
| Writing a medical lecture about a disease | The reader is a physician and wants you, not a summary | Use the patient to show how you think, then move on |
| Retelling your CV in paragraphs | The Experiences section already lists it | Pick one or two experiences and explain what they changed in you |
| Long accounts of hardship or of how you came to the US | They crowd out the specialty story | One honest sentence, then back to the specialty |
| Adjectives instead of evidence ("hardworking", "compassionate") | Anyone can claim them | Show an action that makes the point |
| One statement for every specialty | A statement that could fit any specialty reads as no specialty | Write a separate statement for each specialty you apply to |
| Overclaiming research or leadership | Interview questions will test it | State your actual role in plain words |
| Phrasing that sounds translated or overly formal | It hides the person | Short, direct sentences, and a read by a US physician |
| Typos, odd characters and formatting that does not paste | It signals a lack of care | Draft in a plain text editor and check the MyERAS preview |

The [personal statement guide](/wiki/application/personal-statement-guide) covers the underlying principles in more detail, including a revision process.

## How long should a residency personal statement be?

There is no official target length. MyERAS allows up to {{fact:eras_ps_char_limit}} characters in the {{fact:eras_cycle_year}} cycle, including spaces and punctuation, but that is a ceiling, not advice. Programs see the page count in the preview, and in our opinion a statement that runs to several pages suggests the writer cannot choose what matters.

The three examples above run roughly 300 to 450 words each, which is about a page or a little less. That is our illustration of a common practice, not a requirement. Ask a mentor in your specialty what is customary, and judge your own draft by whether every paragraph adds something the rest of the application does not.

## Residency personal statement examples for IMGs: your path, gaps and why the US

An IMG statement has the same job as any other, plus a few extra decisions. These are practice and opinion, not rules.

- **Explaining your path.** If your route to the specialty was not direct, one paragraph that connects where you started to where you are going is enough. Example 2 does this by following one question. Keep it forward-looking.
- **Why the US.** One honest sentence about the training you want and cannot get elsewhere is fine. Link it to the specialty, for example the kind of supervision, patient population or systems you want to learn. Do not criticize your country's health system or other programs.
- **Gaps and delays.** A reader will notice a date jump, so address it briefly and factually, and fill the dates in ERAS. Never hide, shift or invent dates. The [gap years guide](/wiki/application/gap-years-and-how-to-explain) has a structure for this, and [years since graduation](/wiki/application/years-since-graduation) explains why programs ask.
- **Failed attempts.** The statement is not the place for a long explanation. If you mention one, say it in a sentence and show what changed. See [failed attempts on your application](/wiki/application/failed-attempts-on-application).
- **Visa and return plans.** Do not make commitments you are not certain about. Programs may ask you about them later.
- **Language.** Short, clear sentences read as confident. Ask a US physician to read for phrasing that sounds translated or overly formal.

> [!IMG TIP] Read your draft aloud and mark every sentence that could appear in anyone's statement. Replace each with something only you could write: a patient, a decision, a correction a mentor gave you.

## Before you submit: a six-point check

1. Could any paragraph be pasted into another specialty's statement unchanged? If yes, rewrite it.
2. Does every claim match your ERAS Experiences, your CV and your letters?
3. Is there a sentence you did not write, or one you cannot explain in an interview?
4. Have a mentor in your specialty and a non-medical reader both read it?
5. Does it look right in the MyERAS preview, in plain text, with no strange characters?
6. Is the right statement assigned to the right programs? The [MyERAS walkthrough](/wiki/application/myeras-walkthrough) shows where documents are assigned.

> [!NOTE] No statement, however polished, can guarantee an interview or a match. A statement is one part of an application that also includes exam results, clinical experience, letters and timing.
