A good emergency medicine personal statement shows one specific person making decisions in an acute, uncertain, team-based setting, and says plainly why emergency medicine (EM) and not a neighboring specialty. This page gives two annotated emergency medicine personal statement examples written by the USMLE Wise team, a look at what EM programs tend to look for (as practice and opinion, not rule), an EM checklist, and the points that matter most to international medical graduates ([[img|IMGs]]).

One thing is different from most specialties: EM programs take applications through ResidencyCAS, not ERAS, so the mechanics of the statement are not the ones in most online guides. The next section covers that carefully. Check the current cycle's instructions before you rely on any mechanical detail on this page.

This page builds on others. The [personal statement guide](/wiki/application/personal-statement-guide) covers what a statement is for and a working structure. The [residency personal statement examples](/wiki/application/personal-statement-examples) page shows how to use examples without copying them, and the [internal medicine personal statement examples](/wiki/application/internal-medicine-personal-statement-examples) page does the same job for that specialty. For competitiveness, SLOE advice and the application route, see [emergency medicine for IMGs](/wiki/specialties/emergency-medicine).

> [!WARNING] Every statement on this page is an **illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team**. None was written by a real applicant, none is a real person's story, and no hospital, program, school, colleague or mentor in them is real. **Do not copy these.** The ResidencyCAS help center says plagiarizing any part of your essay is a violation of its code of conduct and may bring sanctions, and AAMC tells ERAS applicants not to copy from example statements on websites. In our opinion, a borrowed paragraph is also easy for a reader to spot, because it does not sound like the rest of your application.

## Emergency medicine personal statements go through ResidencyCAS: what changes

NRMP says ResidencyCAS provides application services for programs in Emergency Medicine and Obstetrics and Gynecology, while ERAS covers the other specialties that take part in the Match. NRMP also says you must register with both NRMP and the application service the programs you want require. ResidencyCAS runs separately from ERAS, and its applicant help center is hosted by Liaison, not on AAMC's ERAS site. This is the rule for the {{fact:eras_cycle_year}} cycle, and these things can change, so check the current cycle's instructions on the ResidencyCAS help center, on NRMP and on CORD.

The table compares what the ResidencyCAS help center says about the statement with what most ERAS guides (including ours) describe.

| Point | ResidencyCAS (EM), per its help center | ERAS (most other specialties) |
|---|---|---|
| How many statements | One per specialty, shared with all programs in that specialty | As many as you like, with one assigned to each program |
| Tailoring to one program | You cannot personalize it for individual programs. Use the Signals feature in the Program Materials section to show interest | Possible by assigning a different statement to a program, which should be used sparingly |
| Length limit | Shown in the application itself, with a character and word count under the field. Check it there | {{fact:eras_ps_char_limit}} characters in the {{fact:eras_cycle_year}} cycle, which is a ceiling and not a target |
| Formatting | Tabs, italics and multiple spaces are not saved. Type a double return between paragraphs | Draft in a plain text editor and check the preview |
| Editing after you submit | The help center says you cannot edit your essay after you submit | Can be edited, but programs may not see the update |
| Copying | Plagiarism may bring sanctions | AAMC says not to copy from example statements |

Four practical consequences follow, and the first two are our opinion.

1. **Write one strong EM statement and stop.** Because it goes to every EM program, do not name a single program, city or mission in it. Put program-specific interest into signals and, later, into interviews and any program communication.
2. **Proofread harder than you would in ERAS.** If you cannot edit after submitting, two readers should check it before you click. CORD's IMG guide names spelling and grammar errors as a major pitfall for IMGs and advises having someone read your application and then having someone read it again.
3. **Do not assume the ERAS character limit applies.** Use the limit shown in the ResidencyCAS application.
4. **A backup specialty is a separate application.** If you also apply to a specialty that uses ERAS, you will write a separate statement for it. See [applying to multiple specialties](/wiki/application/applying-to-multiple-specialties) and [backup specialties](/wiki/specialties/backup-specialties).

For IMGs, ResidencyCAS also involves ECFMG steps, such as an access link and the ECFMG Status Report, according to the ResidencyCAS Quick Start Guide. Confirm the current steps on ECFMG's pages. They affect your documents and timing, not the statement itself.

## What is a good EM personal statement? What programs tend to look for

No page can tell you what a particular program director will prefer, and none of this is a rule. Programs report the factors they use to select applicants for interview in NRMP's Program Director Survey, so check the current edition on nrmp.org for how personal statements and other documents are weighed. We do not quote a figure here.

CORD's IMG guide is clear that the SLOE (Standardized Letter of Evaluation) from an emergency department with a residency program is the key document and that you should not expect interview offers without one. So a statement does not carry your application. In our opinion, its job is to make a reader who already respects your SLOE and scores believe you understand the specialty, will be good to work with at three in the morning, and want this training for a clear reason.

What follows is common advising practice and our opinion. Readers of an EM statement often look for the themes in this table.

| Theme | What it can look like | The weak version |
|---|---|---|
| Why EM, specifically | You can say why you want undifferentiated patients, first-hour decisions and shift work, and why not internal medicine, anesthesia or critical care | "I like the variety and the adrenaline" |
| Comfort with uncertainty | A patient who looked fine and was not, or a diagnosis you held open until the picture was clear | A tidy case with no doubt in it |
| Reassessment and judgment | Going back to a patient, changing a plan, knowing when to call for help | "I make quick decisions under pressure" |
| Teamwork | Specific credit to nurses, paramedics, technicians, consultants and the team you handed over to | "I am a team player" |
| Communication in short encounters | Telling a frightened family something honest in two sentences, or working through an interpreter | "I have good communication skills" |
| Reliability on a hard shift | A concrete moment of steadiness, ownership or follow-through | "I thrive in chaos" |
| Realistic view of the work | Awareness of nights, crowding and the emotional load, and a plan to sustain yourself | A hero story in which you alone save a patient |
| Why this training path (IMGs) | One forward-looking sentence about what you want from US training | A long hardship story |

Two further points, both opinion. First, EM is a shift-based specialty with limited continuity, so a statement built on long relationships with the same patients will read as aimed at another field. Second, naming other specialties you considered is fine and often helpful, as long as you describe what EM offers instead of criticizing the others.

## How to start an emergency medicine residency personal statement

Start with one specific moment, in two or three plain sentences. The patterns below are tone illustrations only. Write your own from your own experience.

| Opening type | How the idea can sound | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| A patient you went back to | "She was triaged as stable, and I almost walked past her." | Do not turn it into a case presentation |
| A handoff | A question from the oncoming doctor that exposed what you had not said | Keep it about what you changed afterward |
| A team moment | A nurse or paramedic who made you look again, and was right | Credit them by role and do not cast yourself as the hero |
| A hard conversation | Telling a family something difficult in a crowded department | Protect privacy and keep the focus on what you learned |

Avoid quotations, "ever since I was a child," a dictionary-style definition of emergency medicine, slogans about adrenaline or "saving lives," and a long list of conditions. A reader who has opened many statements this season is looking for a person, not a prologue.

## Emergency medicine personal statement examples: two illustrative composites

The two statements below use different structures on purpose. Example 1 opens on a single clinical moment. Example 2 follows a handoff and a path, including a brief gap. Both are written to be read in under two minutes, and both run roughly 300 to 450 words, which is our illustration of a common length and not a requirement.

## Example 1: A clinical-moment opening

*Illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team. Not a real applicant's statement. Do not copy.*

> The woman in the corner of the waiting room had been triaged as stable, and by every number on her chart she was. I was the only doctor on a night shift in the emergency unit of a district hospital, with a resuscitation under way behind the curtain, and I almost walked past her. What stopped me was how quiet she had become. When I took her pulse again it had crept up, and her abdomen, which had been soft hours earlier, was not.
>
> She went to theatre within the hour. I do not think of her as a rescue. I think of her as a lesson in what emergency medicine asks of you: to hold several patients in your head at once, and to go back to the ones who look fine.
>
> That became my habit. I reassess every patient I have written down as stable, and I tell the nurse watching them what change should make her call me. In my two years in that emergency unit I learned to run a night with few staff and few tests, to ask for help early, and to hand over a patient so clearly that the next doctor did not need to ask me anything. I also learned that the most frightened person in the room is often a relative, and that two honest sentences to them are worth more than a long explanation.
>
> During a clinical rotation in a teaching emergency department in the United States, I saw the same principles in a different system: interpreters available on a screen within a minute, consultants reachable around the clock, and a team that decided together who could safely wait. I also saw what I have not yet learned. I have little experience with bedside ultrasound, with crowded departments where admitted patients wait for beds, or with the paperwork that surrounds every decision here. I want a residency that teaches those skills directly and early.
>
> I am drawn to emergency medicine because the work begins at the front door, with a patient whose diagnosis nobody knows yet, and the first hour matters most. I do not need the dramatic cases. The quiet ones need us as much.
>
> I will bring steadiness on a bad night, a habit of going back, and the willingness to say that I need help.

### Annotation: Example 1

**What works**

- The opening is one patient and one decision. The writer's values appear through an action (going back to reassess), not through adjectives.
- The lesson is stated in one plain sentence and then proved with a repeatable habit: telling the nurse what change should prompt a call.
- The middle names real EM skills (running a night with limited resources, asking for help early, handing over clearly, talking to families) without listing them as a CV.
- The writer says what they still need to learn and what training they want. That reads as self-awareness.
- The last sentence is a promise about behavior, not an adjective.

**What to change**

- "She went to theatre within the hour" is vague, and the story could read as a rescue by one doctor. Make the nurse's part visible, for example whose observation or action mattered, and keep clinical details minimal.
- Remove anything that could identify a patient or a hospital.
- "I do not need the dramatic cases" is a nice line but slightly defensive. Replace it with one concrete example of a quiet case from the writer's own work.
- The middle paragraph carries several skills at once. If the statement runs long, keep the handoff and the family example and cut the rest.

**What an IMG should watch for**

- Describe being "the only doctor on a night shift" exactly as your employer and your CV would. Overstated responsibility is easy to test in an interview.
- Describe your US role exactly as you list it elsewhere in your application (observer, student or hands-on, and the setting). It should also match the story your SLOE and other letters tell. See [turning US clinical experience into a letter](/wiki/clinical-experience/turning-usce-into-a-letter).
- Limited resources appear here as context for a skill, not as a complaint about your country's system. Keep it that way.

## Example 2: A handoff and a path, with a brief gap

*Illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team. Not a real applicant's statement. Do not copy.*

> At the end of a night shift I gave a handoff that taught me what I want from emergency medicine. I had written everything down: the working diagnosis, the results, the plan. The oncoming doctor asked one question. "What are you worried about?" I had not written that, and I realized I had been worried about one patient for hours without telling anyone.
>
> I graduated several years ago and have worked in emergency departments for most of the time since. I once expected to train in internal medicine, because I enjoy reasoning, and I considered anesthesia, because I like procedures and a calm room in a crisis. What I found in emergency medicine was both, at the front door, with the added work of talking to patients and families in the first minutes of a very bad day. A senior nurse taught me to look at a patient from the doorway before I touch them. A paramedic taught me to ask what the scene looked like, not only what the vital signs were.
>
> The shifts I remember best are the ones that ended badly. After a patient died in the resuscitation room, our nurse manager insisted that the team spend a few minutes together before returning to the floor. I resisted at first, because the waiting room was full. I now think those minutes are why the same team could work together again an hour later, and I run them when I am in charge.
>
> I took a year away from clinical work to prepare for my USMLE Steps and to care for a parent. When I returned, I worked in a regional emergency department and then completed a clinical rotation in an emergency department in the United States. I mention the year because a reader will wonder about it. The time was spent on those two things, and I have worked in clinical roles without interruption since.
>
> I am looking for a residency that gives me graded responsibility, close supervision in procedures and ultrasound, and a department that serves a diverse community where interpreters are part of daily work. I do not expect to be the best at the dramatic cases. I want to be very good at the ordinary ones.
>
> I will be the resident who asks the oncoming doctor what they are worried about, and who writes my own worry down for the next shift.

### Annotation: Example 2

**What works**

- A single handoff frames the whole statement, and the closing line returns to it. The structure is easy to follow in a quick read.
- "Why EM and not the neighbors" is answered without criticizing internal medicine or anesthesia: the writer says what EM combines.
- Teamwork is shown through two small lessons from a nurse and a paramedic, credited by role, and through a debrief habit the writer carries into leadership.
- The gap takes three short sentences: what it was, what the writer did, and what has happened since. There is no apology and no over-explanation.

**What to change**

- The path paragraph is long. Trim the specialties the writer considered to one sentence.
- "A department that serves a diverse community" is generic. Name one specific thing the writer wants, such as supervised ultrasound training or a particular patient population they have already worked with.
- The debrief story would be stronger with one more concrete detail about what the writer changed in their own practice.
- The last line repeats an idea from the opening. That can work, but only if it adds something new, such as what the writer would do with the worry.

**What an IMG should watch for**

- Put exact dates and roles in your application, not in the statement. The statement explains, and the application documents. Dates must be accurate, and you must never hide or shift them. See [gap years and how to explain them](/wiki/application/gap-years-and-how-to-explain).
- Share only as much about a family reason as you are comfortable being asked about in an interview.
- Several years since graduation can trigger screening filters at some programs. A statement cannot change a hard filter, so build a realistic program list as well. See [how programs screen applications](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters).

## An emergency medicine personal statement checklist

Use this after your first full draft. It is our checklist, not an official one.

| Check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The opening is a specific moment in an acute setting, not a childhood story or a quotation | It signals the kind of work EM is |
| You say what is specific about EM (undifferentiated patients, first-hour decisions, shift teamwork) using your own example | "Variety" and "adrenaline" could describe any specialty |
| One moment shows reassessment, asking for help or changing your mind | Judgment under uncertainty is central to the specialty |
| Teamwork is shown through a specific action, with credit to nurses, paramedics, technicians and colleagues | Emergency care depends on the whole team |
| A short, hard conversation appears (fear, bad news, a language barrier) | Communication in short encounters is a core skill |
| You say honestly what you still need to learn | Programs train residents, so openness reads as maturity |
| The writing could not be pasted into an internal medicine, anesthesia or surgery statement | A good statement fits one specialty |
| No program, city or mission is named | ResidencyCAS sends one statement to every EM program |
| Your US emergency department role is described exactly as elsewhere in your application | Inconsistency raises questions at interview |
| Any gap or exam issue is one factual sentence at most, with dates elsewhere | The statement should not become a defense |
| Two readers have proofread it, one of them a US physician | You cannot edit after submitting, and CORD names spelling and grammar as a major pitfall |
| The text is plain, with no tabs, italics or odd characters | ResidencyCAS does not save that formatting |
| No detail could identify a patient or a hospital | Privacy and professionalism |

## Common mistakes in emergency medicine personal statements

- **The trauma-hero story.** A rescue in which you alone saved a patient. Credit the team, and credit the observation that changed the plan.
- **Adrenaline language.** "I thrive in chaos" is a claim without evidence. Show a moment of calm instead.
- **Saying EM is the only specialty that does something.** Faculty in other fields will disagree. Say what you value instead.
- **A disease lecture.** The reader is a physician. Use the patient to show how you think, then stop.
- **Retelling your CV.** Your experiences are already in the application. Pick one or two and explain what they changed in you.
- **Criticizing other specialties, programs or countries.** It reads as a lack of judgment.
- **Lifestyle as the only reason.** Wanting shift work is a real and common reason, in our opinion, but it cannot be the whole answer to why EM.
- **A long story about hardship or about how you reached the US.** One honest sentence is enough.
- **Writing as if the statement replaces the SLOE.** It does not. Spend your effort on the letters too, as described in [letters of recommendation](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation).
- **Overclaiming research or leadership.** State your real role in plain words.
- **Language that sounds translated or too formal.** Short, clear sentences read as confident.

## Emergency medicine personal statement points for IMGs

An IMG statement has the same job as any other, plus a few extra decisions. These are practice and opinion, not rules.

- **Your background.** CORD's IMG guide encourages IMGs to mention their particular background and to use personal, clinical, cultural and language experiences to stand out. In our reading, that means one concrete detail (a language you use with patients, a type of emergency care you have actually delivered), not a long account of your life.
- **Why the US.** One honest, forward-looking sentence about the training you want and cannot get elsewhere is fine. Do not criticize your country's health system.
- **US clinical experience.** If you have it, show what you learned in one specific example. The people who write your letters will describe you too, so the stories should agree. See [how to get US clinical experience](/wiki/clinical-experience/how-to-get-usce).
- **Gaps and failed attempts.** The statement is not the place for a long explanation. Say what happened in a sentence if you mention it at all, and show what changed. See [failed attempts on your application](/wiki/application/failed-attempts-on-application).
- **Visa and return plans.** Do not make commitments you are not certain about. CORD notes that visa and financial factors affect hiring, and programs may ask you later.
- **A second specialty.** If you also apply to another specialty, write a separate statement for it. The [internal medicine personal statement examples](/wiki/application/internal-medicine-personal-statement-examples) show how one specialty's statement differs from another's.
- **Language.** Ask a US physician to read for phrasing that sounds translated or overly formal.

> [!IMG TIP] Read your draft aloud and underline every sentence that could appear in an internal medicine or anesthesia statement unchanged. Replace each with something only emergency medicine, and only you, could supply: a patient you went back to, a handoff question, a correction from a nurse.

## How long should an emergency medicine personal statement be?

There is no official target length. The ResidencyCAS help center says the limit is shown in the application, with a character and word count under the statement field, and that you cannot save an essay that exceeds it. Use that limit and not the ERAS one, and treat it as a ceiling, not a goal.

The two examples above run roughly 300 to 450 words each, about a page or a little less. That is our illustration of a common practice, not a requirement. Ask a mentor in emergency medicine what is customary, and judge your draft by whether every paragraph adds something the rest of your application does not. Draft in a plain text editor, and check the final text for odd characters before you submit.

> [!NOTE] No statement, however polished, can guarantee an interview or a match, and EM is a specialty where IMGs face real obstacles that a statement cannot remove. The statement is one part of an application that also includes exam results, SLOEs and other letters, clinical experience and timing. See [residency personal statement examples](/wiki/application/personal-statement-examples) for more structures in other specialties.
