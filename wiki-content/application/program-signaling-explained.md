A program signal lets you tell a specific residency program, at the moment you apply, that you are particularly interested in it. Each specialty gives you a fixed number, programs see which ones you sent them, and AAMC advises using all of them. AAMC's official term is **program signals** (earlier AAMC materials called them preference signals). This page also covers the separate **geographic preferences** question, which people often call "geographic signals".

> [!NOTE] Signal counts, tiers and participating programs change by cycle. The figures here are from AAMC's 2027 materials. Always check AAMC's current table before you plan.

> For the 2027 Match, NRMP says Emergency Medicine and Obstetrics and Gynecology programs use ResidencyCAS rather than ERAS, and all other specialties use ERAS. If you are applying to either of those two specialties, the ERAS steps, fees and signaling described on this page may not apply to them. Read the [Emergency Medicine](/wiki/specialties/emergency-medicine) and [OB-GYN](/wiki/specialties/obstetrics-and-gynecology) guides, and confirm the current system with NRMP.

## How signaling works (rules)

- You can send signals only for specialties you apply to and to **participating programs**. You cannot signal a program that has chosen not to take part.
- **Signals go to the institution, not a track.** You cannot signal the preliminary versus categorical track separately.
- **Only programs you signal and apply to see your signal.** Standard signals show as "Yes"; tiered signals show as "Gold" or "Silver". Programs that you did not signal receive no signal information.
- **Tiers.** Some specialties use gold (most preferred) and silver (preferred) signals. In 2027, AAMC lists tiered signaling for internal medicine (3 gold, 12 silver), anesthesiology (5 gold, 10 silver), dermatology (3 gold, 25 silver), diagnostic radiology (6 gold, 9 silver), child neurology, vascular surgery - integrated, and some fellowships.
- **Editing.** For programs you have applied to, you can edit the signal until the cycle's opening date, after which it locks. Signals for saved programs can be changed, and are not final until submitted with an application. If you withdraw an application, the signal cannot be reassigned.

## Which specialties use program signals?

AAMC says it requires each interested specialty to agree to participate, and that participating specialties are trained to use signals as part of a holistic review. Participation is also optional for individual programs within a specialty, which is why you cannot send a signal to a program that has opted out.

For the 2027 residency season, AAMC's list includes these specialties (names only; check the current list for counts and tiers):

- **Tiered (gold and silver):** Anesthesiology, Child Neurology, Dermatology, Diagnostic Radiology, Internal Medicine, Vascular Surgery - Integrated.
- **Single tier:** Family Medicine, General Surgery, Interventional Radiology - Integrated, Neurodevelopmental Disabilities, Neurological Surgery, Neurology, Orthopedic Surgery, Otolaryngology, Pathology, Pediatrics, Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, Plastic Surgery - Integrated, Psychiatry, Public Health and General Preventive Medicine, Radiation Oncology, Thoracic Surgery - Integrated, Transitional Year, Urology, and several combined programs such as Internal Medicine/Pediatrics and Internal Medicine/Psychiatry.

July and December cycle fellowships also have their own signal lists. In MyERAS, the View All Signals link groups specialties as single tier, two-tier and not participating, and no signals can be assigned in a not participating specialty. Emergency Medicine and OB-GYN are outside this system for 2027, as noted above. Check the current cycle's ERAS instructions, because specialties join and leave.

## Which specialties have signal statements? The program signal explanation

A **program signal explanation** (PSE, sometimes called a signal statement) is a short text, up to 300 characters, saying why you are signaling a program. In the 2027 guide it applies to four specialties: anesthesiology, plastic surgery - integrated, PM&R and radiation oncology. In those specialties AAMC says a signal without an explanation will not be saved. The explanation must not be used to state ranking preferences or intentions. AAMC lists the PSE among pilot projects it continues to evaluate, so check the current cycle's ERAS instructions to see which specialties still use it.

A practical structure (opinion, not an AAMC rule): one concrete connection, such as a rotation, a mentor, research or a place you lived, then one feature of the program that fits your goals, then stop. Do not repeat your personal statement and do not say you will rank the program.

*Illustrative framework, not a script:* "[Concrete connection]. [One program feature] fits my goal of [specific goal]."

## What programs do with signals

Programs are told to use signals as **one piece of information** and not as the only screening tool, but programs can filter by signal. In AAMC's survey of the 2026 cycle, 96% of responding programs said they used signals to help decide whom to interview, and 90% said signals helped them find applicants they would otherwise overlook. AAMC also reports that sending a signal was associated with a higher probability of an interview in all participating specialties, and that programs varied widely in how they used it.

AAMC says to contact a program directly if you want to know how it is using signals in the 2027 cycle.

## ERAS signal strategies: where to spend your signals (practice)

AAMC advises considering your application strength, career goals, personal circumstances, and each program's competitiveness, mission and goals. For IMGs, the following adds structure (this is our opinion, not a rule).

1. **Start from a realistic list.** Build your list first. See [building your program list](/wiki/application/building-a-program-list).
2. **Spend signals on programs that fit and could realistically say yes:** programs with a record of taking IMGs, that sponsor your visa type, and whose exam requirements you meet. See [identifying IMG-friendly programs](/wiki/application/identifying-img-friendly-programs).
3. **Prioritize programs where you have a real connection:** you rotated or did an observership there, have a mentor or letter writer there, or have family or geographic ties. AAMC specifically says to signal home and away rotation programs.
4. **Do not waste signals on programs that will not interview you anyway:** for example, programs with a hard USMLE cutoff you do not meet or that do not sponsor your visa.
5. **Do not signal only the most competitive programs.** A signal does not rescue an application that fails a program's screen.
6. **Use gold on programs where you most want to train and have a plausible chance;** use silver on the next group, if your specialty is tiered.

| Type of program | Signal it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Where you rotated, observed or have a mentor or letter writer | Usually yes | A real connection, and AAMC says to signal rotation programs |
| IMG-friendly, sponsors your visa, you meet its requirements | Yes | Realistic chance, and the signal helps you stand out from similar applicants |
| Hard cutoff you do not meet, or no sponsorship for your visa | No | The screen would stop the application anyway |
| Very competitive program with no realistic fit | Few or none | A signal does not rescue an application that fails a screen |
| Program that routinely interviews most IMGs who meet its requirements | Lower priority | Meeting the requirements matters more than the signal |

> [!IMG TIP] Signals matter most for programs that receive many applications and use filters. For a program that routinely interviews most IMG applicants who meet its requirements, a signal helps less than meeting the requirements.

## Geographic preferences (often called geographic signals)

AAMC does not call this a signal. The MyERAS question is **geographic preferences**, and it sits in the Biographical Information section, separate from program signals. A **signal** goes to specific programs; a **geographic preference** is a statement about US Census divisions. The rules in the 2027 guide:

- You choose US Census divisions (up to three) or select that you do not have a division preference, then describe your preference or lack of one in text.
- **If you select a division,** only programs in that division, and that you apply to, see your answer. All other programs see nothing.
- **If you select "I do not have a division preference,"** every program you apply to sees that.
- **If you skip the question,** no program sees anything.
- Some specialties do not receive geographic or setting preference answers in 2027 (otolaryngology, orthopedic surgery and plastic surgery - integrated).
- Programs can filter applicants by geographic division, although AAMC says programs are told to use preferences only as pieces of information in review and interview selection.

AAMC also reports, from its 2024 cycle data, that most participating programs read "no preference" as willingness to go anywhere, and that in specialties that took part, an interview invitation was more likely when your stated preference matched the program's location than when you chose no preference. That is a general pattern, not a guarantee. A separate **setting preference** (urban, suburban or rural) is shared with all programs you apply to if you answer it. See the [MyERAS walkthrough](/wiki/application/myeras-walkthrough).

### How to explain a geographic preference

AAMC says programs look for evidence of a social support system, so your explanation should say honestly why a region suits you. Our suggestions (practice and opinion):

1. **Give the true reason.** For example family or a partner there, earlier training or clinical experience in that region, or a community you belong to.
2. **Be specific and brief.** One or two sentences are enough. Programs read many of these.
3. **If you have no tie, do not invent one.** Choose "no division preference" and give a true reason, such as being willing to relocate and putting training fit first. AAMC advises explaining why you have no preference.
4. **Keep it consistent.** If you name a division, most of your applications should be there, and the same story should match your personal statement and what you say in interviews.
5. **Do not use it for ranking promises,** and keep work authorization questions in the work authorization section.

*Illustrative framework, not a script:* "[Tie to the region]. [Why that region supports my training or well-being]. [Optional: I am open to other regions where the program is a good fit.]"

## Common mistakes

- Leaving signals unused.
- Signaling a program that is not on your final application list.
- Signaling a program that does not accept your visa or exam history.
- Assuming a signal can be changed after the lock date.
- Using the explanation field to promise you will rank a program first.
- Naming a geographic division you have no real connection to.

## Where signaling fits in the season

Plan your signals after you finish your program list and before you certify and submit. The [ERAS timeline](/wiki/application/eras-timeline) shows where this falls. Signaling is a decision to make with a mentor in your specialty if you can find one, since specialty norms vary.
