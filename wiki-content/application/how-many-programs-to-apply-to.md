Nobody can give you a trustworthy single number, because the right count depends on your specialty, your profile and how many programs can actually consider you. The reliable approach is to **count eligible programs, not total programs**, then balance your chances and your budget.

## What the published data say

NRMP's 2025 Charting Outcomes applicant survey reports several patterns that matter here:

- Median numbers of applications are highest among applicants who preferred internal medicine, plastic surgery, general surgery and psychiatry.
- Applicants in the "Other Applicant Types" group, which includes IMGs, submitted higher median numbers of applications than US MD and DO seniors, whether or not they matched, which NRMP says is consistent with previous years.
- Across all applicant types, unmatched applicants reported applying to more programs than matched applicants.
- US MD and DO seniors reported being offered and attending considerably more interviews than other applicant types.

This tells you two things. IMGs generally apply more broadly than US graduates, and applying to more programs does not by itself explain who matches. We have not quoted a specific median because it varies by specialty and year, so use the NRMP report for the figure that matches your specialty.

> [!NOTE] These are survey results from one year and reflect self-reported behavior of applicants with different profiles. They describe what people did, not what you should do.

## A method for deciding

### Step 1: Count eligible programs

Start from the program list method in [building your program list](/wiki/application/building-a-program-list). Remove every program that cannot take you because of visa, exam rules, graduation year or USCE requirements. What is left is your **eligible pool**. See [identifying IMG-friendly programs](/wiki/application/identifying-img-friendly-programs) for how to check.

If your eligible pool is small, a large application count is not possible. Consider whether another specialty or a different timeline is needed.

### Step 2: Classify your profile (opinion)

Use your own honest read of these factors, ideally with a mentor in your specialty:

| Factor | Stronger | Weaker |
|---|---|---|
| Exam record | First-attempt passes, solid Step 2 CK | Any failed attempt or marginal score |
| Years since graduation | Recent | Long gap |
| US clinical experience | Recent, hands-on, with strong letters | None or observation only |
| Visa need | None, or J-1 only (more programs accept) | H-1B required (fewer programs) |
| Specialty competitiveness | Less competitive for IMGs | Highly competitive for IMGs |
| Research and connections | Relevant and verifiable | None |

A weaker profile in a specialty with many applicants generally calls for a longer list within the eligible pool and a stronger reliance on safer programs. A stronger profile can be more selective. This is a guide to thinking, not a formula.

### Step 3: Set the tiers

Divide your eligible programs into reach, target and safer tiers. Many IMG advisors recommend that most applications go to target and safer programs, with a small number of reach programs where you have a connection. See the program list guide for details.

### Step 4: Fit the budget

Application cost grows with the number of programs. ERAS charges fees that increase with program count, and there are additional costs for the NRMP, transcripts, exams and interviews. Check AAMC's and NRMP's current fee schedules, and the [total cost of matching](/wiki/start-here/total-cost-of-matching) for a fuller budget.

Decide your ceiling before you add programs. If the ceiling is below your list, cut the programs with the weakest evidence of fit, not the ones where you have a connection.

### Step 5: Use signals to concentrate

Signals tell programs where you most want to train, and AAMC advises using all of them. Your signals should go to programs in your target and safer tiers where you have a real chance. See [program signaling explained](/wiki/application/program-signaling-explained).

### Step 6: Plan for interviews, not just applications

An application list should assume that only a share will lead to an interview and that you must be able to attend them. Before finalizing, check:

- Time and travel costs of interviews, which may be virtual or in person depending on the program.
- Visa or travel planning if you are outside the US.
- Whether your list is geographically concentrated enough to schedule interviews efficiently.

## Signs you are over- or under-applying

| Sign | Likely issue |
|---|---|
| Many programs on the list have rules you do not meet | Over-applying to ineligible programs |
| Few programs where your profile matches published requirements | Narrow pool: rethink specialty, timeline or experience |
| Every program is a reach | Imbalanced list |
| You cannot afford interviews | Over-spent on applications |

> [!WARNING] Beware of anyone who gives a flat number such as "apply to everything" or guarantees outcomes at a certain count. Without regard to eligibility, that advice wastes money.

## Reapplicants

If you are applying again, use what happened last season: where you got interviews, where you did not and why. See [reapplicant strategy](/wiki/application/reapplicant-strategy).

## Summary of the method

1. Build the eligible pool.
2. Assess your profile honestly.
3. Tier the pool.
4. Set a budget ceiling.
5. Concentrate signals.
6. Check interview logistics.
7. Review with a mentor and adjust.
