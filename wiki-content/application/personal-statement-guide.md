A residency personal statement is a short essay that tells programs who you are, why you chose this specialty and what kind of resident you will be. In ERAS you write it in plain text, assign it to programs, and a program director may read it in under two minutes, so it must be clear, specific and honest.

> [!NOTE] There is no single official format. Everything below about structure and length is common practice or our opinion, labeled as such. The hard rules are only the ERAS mechanics in the next section.

## The ERAS mechanics (rules)

From the AAMC 2027 applicant user guide:

- You create statements in the **Documents** area of MyERAS. Each has a title (only you see it) and content (programs see it).
- The limit is **28,000 characters**, including letters, numbers, spaces and punctuation.
- You can create **any number** of statements, and you assign **one statement per program**.
- Writing outside MyERAS should be done in a **plain text editor**, because text from word processors can bring formatting characters that do not paste cleanly.
- The preview shows the statement as programs will see it, including the page count.
- You can edit a statement at any time, but edited statements are flagged on the program side and programs are not guaranteed to review the updated version.
- AAMC explicitly says not to copy information from example statements found on websites.

Because programs see page count, a statement that runs to several pages signals that you cannot prioritize.

## What a personal statement is for (practice)

Programs already have your scores, MSPE, letters and experiences. The statement is the one place in the application written in your voice. A good one answers three questions:

1. **Why this specialty?** Not "I like helping people." What specifically drew you to this field's work?
2. **Why are you ready?** What have you done that shows you understand the job?
3. **What will you be like to work with?** Show character through what you did, not adjectives.

## A working structure (opinion)

This is one reliable pattern, not a mandate.

1. **Opening (one short paragraph):** a specific moment, patient situation or decision that started your interest. Keep it concrete and short. Avoid dramatic hooks and generic quotations.
2. **Development (one to two paragraphs):** what you did next that deepened the interest, such as clinical work, research, teaching or a project. Connect each to a skill the specialty uses.
3. **Fit and values (one paragraph):** the kind of training environment you want and what you will contribute. Be specific enough that it could not be pasted into another specialty.
4. **Close (two or three sentences):** where you hope to go in your career and a confident, plain finish.

If you are a [[img]], one paragraph can address the path that brought you to the US system, but keep it forward-looking.

## Handling IMG-specific content

- **Why the US:** a sentence of honest motivation is fine. Long accounts of hardship in your country or of how you came here often crowd out the specialty story.
- **Visa or return plans:** do not make commitments you are not certain about. Programs may ask later.
- **Gaps, delays and failed attempts:** the statement is not the place for a long explanation. State what happened in a sentence if needed, say what you learned, and move on. See [how programs screen applications](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters) to understand why these items get noticed.
- **Different specialty than your background:** show the path from your prior experience to this field, not only the destination.

## What to avoid

- Life-story chronology that starts in childhood.
- Medical-textbook lectures about a disease.
- Clichés: "ever since I was a child", "passion for medicine", "I am a hard worker".
- Criticizing other specialties, programs or countries.
- Claims of exceptional ability with no evidence.
- Details you do not want to be asked about in an interview.
- Repeating your experience descriptions. AAMC advises each section should add something new.
- Copying or lightly editing someone else's statement.

## Using more than one statement

Because ERAS lets you assign statements by program, use this for real reasons:

| Situation | Approach |
|---|---|
| Applying to one specialty | One main statement, possibly one lightly adjusted for programs with a specific mission or location tie |
| Applying to two specialties | Separate statements; each specialty reads only its own |
| Preliminary or transitional year applications | A short, distinct statement about what you want from a first year |

Do not write ten customized statements that you cannot proofread. Quality control matters more than volume. The [MyERAS walkthrough](/wiki/application/myeras-walkthrough) shows where documents are assigned.

## Revising: a process that works

1. Write a rough draft without worrying about length.
2. Cut it to about one page of plain text.
3. Read aloud. Mark every sentence that could appear in anyone's statement and replace it with something only you could write.
4. Have two readers: someone who knows medicine, and someone who does not, to catch jargon and fuzziness.
5. Have a mentor from your specialty check whether the tone is normal for the field.
6. Proofread the final text in MyERAS preview for spelling and special characters.

> [!IMG TIP] Ask a US physician to read it specifically for phrasing that sounds translated or overly formal. Clear, short sentences read as confident.

## Common IMG mistakes

- Writing for a general audience rather than the specialty.
- Spending the first half on why you want to leave your country.
- Listing achievements already on the CV.
- Submitting without a native or highly fluent proofreader's pass.
- Treating the 28,000-character limit as a requirement.

## When to finish

Aim to have a final draft well before the application opens, so you are not editing under deadline. Your letters and experiences take longer to arrange, so use the [ERAS timeline](/wiki/application/eras-timeline) to schedule the statement alongside them.
