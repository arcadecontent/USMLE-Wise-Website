A lower score narrows your options but does not close the door. **The strategy is to stop competing where scores decide the outcome, apply where the stated rules include you, and strengthen the parts of the file you can still change.** That means a smaller, better-researched program list, strong recent clinical evidence, and a specialty choice that fits your profile.

> [!NOTE] We do not define a "good" score here. Passing standards and score distributions change, and the right comparison is your specialty and program type. See [Step 2 CK scores and what they mean](/wiki/exams/step-2-ck-score-and-what-it-means).

## What the data says, and does not say

NRMP's 2026 Program Director Survey (12.7% response rate, 861 programs) found:

- 92% of programs required IMGs to have taken Step 1 and 84% required Step 2 CK for interview consideration.
- Of programs requiring Step 1, 97% required a pass result for IMGs.
- Of programs requiring Step 2 CK, 32% required a target score for IMGs.

So a pass is the gate at most programs, and a minority set a numeric target. That fits the practical advice: **the score matters most when a program has published a cutoff, and less when it does not.** The survey cannot tell you any one program's cutoff.

NRMP's 2024 IMG Charting Outcomes states a related point: matched IMGs tend to have higher Step 1 and Step 2 CK scores than unmatched ones, but a high score is not a guarantee and a low score is not a bar, and in less competitive specialties IMGs with scores slightly above passing matched to their preferred specialty. Both statements are true at once: scores are associated with success, and many applicants succeed without high scores.

## Step 1: Diagnose which weakness you have

| Weakness | What to look at |
|---|---|
| Low Step 2 CK but passed first time | Specialty choice, programs without cutoffs, strong clinical evidence |
| Passed after a failed attempt | See [failed attempts](/wiki/application/failed-attempts-on-application) |
| Late exams or long gap | See [gap years](/wiki/application/gap-years-and-how-to-explain) and [years since graduation](/wiki/application/years-since-graduation) |
| Everything is average, no US ties | Build US clinical evidence and letters |
| Low scores plus high ambition | Reconsider specialty or add a realistic fallback |

## Step 2: Choose the specialty for your profile

Specialties differ greatly in how IMGs fare and in how much they screen on score. Look at the match data in [IMG match rates by specialty](/wiki/specialties/img-match-rates-by-specialty), and at the guides for [internal medicine](/wiki/specialties/internal-medicine), [family medicine](/wiki/specialties/family-medicine), [pediatrics](/wiki/specialties/pediatrics) and [psychiatry](/wiki/specialties/psychiatry). This is a matter of fit as well as numbers: a specialty you can see yourself in for decades will also read better in your statement and interviews.

NRMP recommends that applicants in competitive fields also rank their most preferred programs in an alternate specialty. If your first-choice specialty is a reach, plan a second one honestly. See [choosing a backup specialty](/wiki/specialties/backup-specialties) and [applying to more than one specialty](/wiki/application/applying-to-multiple-specialties).

## Step 3: Build the list around filters you meet

1. Collect programs in your specialty and read each one's requirements.
2. Remove programs with a cutoff or rule you do not meet.
3. Keep programs with no stated cutoff, a track record with IMGs, and visa support if you need it.
4. Include a mix of competitive and less competitive programs, as NRMP suggests for rank lists.
5. Put your signals on programs where you pass the filters and have a real reason to be there. See [signaling](/wiki/application/program-signaling-explained).

See [identifying IMG-friendly programs](/wiki/application/identifying-img-friendly-programs) and [building your program list](/wiki/application/building-a-program-list).

## Step 4: Strengthen what you can still change

- **Recent clinical evidence** with a strong letter. See [turning USCE into a letter](/wiki/clinical-experience/turning-usce-into-a-letter).
- **Letters** that describe specific work. See [letters of recommendation](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation).
- **Research with a defined role,** as a supplement. See [presenting research](/wiki/application/research-on-your-application). NRMP says applicants do not need publications to match.
- **A coherent statement** that explains your direction without hiding facts.
- **Interview skill.** A file that reaches an interview can be won back by communication. See [common interview questions](/wiki/interviews-match/common-interview-questions).
- **Step 3,** if your timeline and profile support it. See [Step 3](/wiki/exams/step-3).

## Step 5: Do not try to change what is fixed

A passed Step generally cannot be retaken, so you cannot improve a passing score. Do not look for loopholes. Do not misstate a score, date or attempt. Your USMLE transcript is the record programs see.

> [!WARNING] Be careful with services that promise a match for a fee or that advertise guaranteed interviews. Nobody can guarantee outcomes, and the cost of a failed season is large. See [reapplying after not matching](/wiki/application/reapplicant-strategy).

## Step 6: Plan for the realistic outcomes

Decide in advance what you will do if you do not match: a second attempt with changes, a preliminary route, or another specialty. See [if you do not match](/wiki/interviews-match/if-you-do-not-match) and [preliminary and transitional years](/wiki/application/preliminary-and-transitional-years). A plan reduces panic during SOAP.

> [!IMG TIP] If you have limited money for applications, a list of programs you can verify as realistic is usually worth more than extra applications to programs that filter you out. Check the cost with [ERAS and NRMP costs](/wiki/application/eras-and-match-costs).
