A poster or oral presentation is often the quickest way to turn a project into a visible result. It is also an easy place to make careless mistakes about status, consent and conference quality. This guide covers finding venues, writing the abstract, making the poster and describing the result honestly. Posters count for less than peer-reviewed papers in most readings, but they are real work and often a first step. See [how programs actually view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research) for what the data says.

## Choosing a conference

Common options for IMGs:

- **Specialty societies** (for example internal medicine, pediatrics or psychiatry organizations) with national or regional meetings and resident or trainee abstract tracks.
- **Regional and state chapters** of those societies, which are often more accessible.
- **Institutional research days** at the host hospital or university.
- **International meetings** in your own region.

What to check:

1. **Who runs it.** A recognized society or institution is a good sign.
2. **Whether abstracts are reviewed.** A conference that accepts every submission and charges a fee is closer to a pay-to-present event.
3. **Where abstracts are published.** Some meetings publish abstracts in a journal supplement. Check that the journal is real (see [predatory journals](/wiki/research/predatory-journals)).
4. **Eligibility.** Some competitions require membership or a US training status. For example, ACP's national abstract competitions have their own rules about eligibility and fees, so read the current ones on the ACP site.
5. **Cost and attendance.** Registration, travel and visa needs. Virtual options may exist.

Be cautious of unsolicited emails inviting you to speak at conferences with broad names and no society behind them. Opinion: one accepted abstract at a recognized meeting is worth more than many presentations at events nobody can verify.

## Writing the abstract

Conference abstracts are short, often a few hundred words, and usually follow the call's exact rules. Read the instructions before you write because limits on words, tables and images differ between meetings.

A standard research abstract has:

1. **Background or purpose**: the question in one or two sentences.
2. **Methods**: design, setting, sample and main analysis.
3. **Results**: actual numbers, not "significant improvements."
4. **Conclusions**: what the data support and no more.

A case report (clinical vignette) abstract usually has: introduction, case description, discussion and learning points. The [case reports](/wiki/research/case-reports) guide covers consent and privacy.

Rules to follow:

- **Do not include identifiable patient information.** The ICMJE standard on privacy and consent applies to abstracts and slides too.
- **Do not fabricate or pre-fill results.** Submit only what you have.
- **Agree authors in advance** and list only people who meet the criteria. See [authorship and author order](/wiki/research/authorship-and-author-order).
- **Follow deadlines**; late abstracts are rarely accepted.

## Building the poster

Posters are read from a distance in a few seconds. Aim for:

- A clear title that states the finding or topic.
- A short introduction, methods, results and conclusion, with generous white space.
- One or two figures or tables that carry the main result.
- Text large enough to read from a couple of meters.
- A QR code or contact line if the meeting allows it.
- A 60-second spoken summary and a 3-minute version for questions.

Check the conference's size, orientation and file format, and whether it is printed or electronic. Do not cut corners on references and acknowledgments.

## Presenting

- Know your numbers and limitations.
- Be honest about what you did and what the team did.
- Invite questions, and write down contacts, since poster sessions are among the better places to meet mentors.
- If you are an IMG attending in person, confirm your visa or travel status well ahead. See [B-1/B-2 for interviews and exams](/wiki/visas/b1-b2-for-interviews-and-exams) for how visitor visas work in general, and check the host's guidance on whether a conference trip is appropriate for your status.

## After the conference

- **Turn it into a paper** if the project supports it. ICMJE says an abstract or poster presented at a scientific meeting does not prevent a journal from considering a complete report. Mention the abstract in the cover letter. See [how to publish a paper](/wiki/research/how-to-publish).
- **Record it correctly** for your CV and ERAS: title, authors in order, meeting name, date, location, and whether it was a poster or oral presentation. The NRMP counts abstracts, presentations and publications, and programs may read them differently. See [presenting research on your ERAS application](/wiki/application/research-on-your-application).
- **Keep proof**: the acceptance email, the program page and the abstract book. Programs may ask about anything you list.

> [!WARNING] Do not list a presentation you have not given or an abstract that was only submitted as accepted. Misrepresenting research is an integrity problem and can be reported to ECFMG as irregular behavior.

## Realistic value

A poster shows that you can complete a project and communicate it. It rarely changes an application on its own, but it supports a coherent research story, gives you something concrete to discuss in interviews and can lead to a paper. Priorities, in our opinion: finish the work, present it at a verifiable venue, then publish it.
