Programs generally read research as evidence of curiosity, persistence and the ability to finish work, and they weigh it alongside everything else in the application. The honest answer to "how much does research count?" is that it depends on the specialty and the program, and the NRMP data does not give a single weight. This guide explains how the types of output differ and how quantity and quality are read.

## What the NRMP data does and does not say

- **Counts are self-reported.** NRMP's 2024 Charting Outcomes report for IMGs says applicants report the number of research experiences themselves, and these are "not verified or evaluated and quality may vary greatly."
- **Publications, abstracts and presentations are lumped together.** The NRMP count includes peer-reviewed articles, abstracts, poster sessions and invited national or regional presentations. (NRMP's 2026 reporting separates abstracts, presentations and publications into three items, which suggests the field recognizes the difference.)
- **Some programs check, most probably do not.** NRMP notes that some programs may verify and even review publications for applicants of interest, but most probably do not.
- **Large numbers exist.** NRMP observes that some applicants report dozens or even hundreds of items.
- **No clear dose-response.** Among IMGs, whether matched applicants reported more research than unmatched ones varied widely by specialty, with unmatched applicants exceeding matched ones in more than half of the specialties.
- **Programs list research among many factors.** The program director survey instruments include "demonstrated involvement and interest in research" and "interest in academic career" among dozens of items programs may consider for interviews and ranking. The published 2024 and 2026 summaries highlight other factors, such as exam results, the MSPE, letters, interpersonal skills and interview performance, as the top considerations.

Read together, these points mean research is one signal among many, and its weight shifts by program.

## What counts: a practical hierarchy

This is common practice and partly opinion; programs differ.

| Output | What it shows | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed publication | A project reached external review and acceptance | Journal quality and your role matter |
| Conference abstract | Work accepted for presentation by a conference committee | Check whether it was reviewed; abstracts are shorter and less rigorous than papers |
| Poster or oral presentation | You presented work to an audience | Venue (national, regional, local) matters |
| Manuscript under review or in preparation | Work in progress | Label it exactly as that, and be ready to describe it |
| Ongoing project without output yet | Participation and skills | Count it as research experience, not as a result |
| Case report | A published or presented clinical case | Valuable if peer reviewed, but generally less substantial than original research |

If you are listing work, use the correct category and status in ERAS and read the current AAMC instructions for how each type is entered.

## Quantity versus quality

Programs reading hundreds of applications look at research quickly. What they tend to notice, in our experience-based view and not in official data:

- **Role and depth.** Did you design, analyze or write, or did you do data entry on a project of ten authors?
- **Coherence.** Research in or near your chosen specialty reads as sustained interest.
- **Outlet.** A recognized journal or conference reads differently from a venue no one has heard of.
- **Consistency with the rest of the application.** Research that matches the personal statement, letters and interview answers is more credible.
- **Padding.** Many marginal items, repeated versions of the same work, or work in unfamiliar outlets can create doubt rather than admiration.

> [!WARNING] Be cautious with journals that solicit manuscripts aggressively, promise fast acceptance for a fee or have no clear editorial process. Publishing in such outlets can reflect poorly on an application. Check any journal's editorial board, indexing and policies before you submit.

## Authorship integrity

Programs, mentors and journals expect authorship to follow contribution. The widely used ICMJE criteria require all four of the following: substantial contributions to the work, drafting or critically revising it, final approval of the version to be published, and agreement to be accountable for the work. Contributors who do not meet all four should be acknowledged instead.

What follows from this:

- Be able to describe, in plain language, what you did on every item you list.
- Do not list yourself as an author on work you could not discuss.
- Never pay for a place on a paper or for a promised publication.
- If your role changed during a project, say so accurately.
- ECFMG's irregular behavior policy covers misrepresentation, so accuracy is also a certification issue.

## Work in progress and what to say about it

Many IMGs have ongoing projects at application time. That is normal. In ERAS and in interviews:

1. Label the status exactly: in preparation, submitted, under review, accepted, published.
2. Do not list a manuscript as "submitted" if it has not been.
3. Describe your role and the question the project asks.
4. Be ready to discuss methods, results so far and what you learned.

Programs generally respond better to a clear, honest account of an unfinished project than to an inflated one.

## How it varies by specialty and program

- Specialties with higher competition or stronger academic cultures often show higher reported research counts in NRMP's specialty pages.
- University programs and programs with fellowships may care more than community programs.
- Some programs state expectations on their websites. Others weigh it informally.
- Within a program, different faculty may value research differently.

Use [how to identify IMG-friendly programs](/wiki/application/identifying-img-friendly-programs) and program websites to see which programs highlight scholarly activity, and see [how programs screen applications](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters) for the earlier filters that research cannot bypass.

## Using this on your application

- Present fewer, clearer items rather than a crowded list. See [the ERAS experiences section](/wiki/application/eras-experiences-section) for how to write them.
- Use the personal statement and interviews to explain what the research taught you.
- Ask your research mentor for a letter if they supervised you closely.
- Do not assume research can replace exam strength or US clinical letters.

> [!IMG TIP] For each research item, write one sentence on the question, one on your role and one on the result or current status. If you cannot, that item may not be ready to list.
