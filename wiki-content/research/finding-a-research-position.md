IMGs find research positions mainly by approaching investigators directly, going through a university or hospital's formal channels, using personal and school networks, or joining remote or retrospective projects. The best positions have a named mentor, a defined project and a clear understanding of authorship from the start. Whether research is worth the time for you is covered in [why research matters for IMGs](/wiki/research/why-research-matters-for-imgs).

## Know what you want first

Before searching, decide:

- **Your specialty interest.** Research in your target specialty reads more coherently, though any real project can teach skills.
- **The kind of work.** Chart review, systematic review, database analysis, laboratory work, clinical trial support and case reports each need different skills and time.
- **Your constraints.** Time zone, visa status, hours per week and whether you can work in person.
- **Your goal.** A mentor and letter, a publication, a poster, or a skill set. Be realistic about what a short project can produce.

## Route 1: Direct outreach

Cold email is common because it works often enough when it is specific.

1. **Find people whose work you can describe.** Search PubMed and department pages for investigators with recent papers in your area of interest. Read one or two of their papers.
2. **Write a short, specific email.** Say who you are, what you read of theirs, what you can contribute (skills, hours, language, statistics, software), what you want to learn and your availability. Attach a CV.
3. **Offer something concrete.** For example: screening abstracts for a review, cleaning data, helping with literature searches, drafting methods.
4. **Follow up once or twice.** Busy investigators often respond after a reminder.
5. **Expect many no replies.** That is normal and not a judgment of you.

Avoid mass email with generic text. It is both ineffective and visible.

## Route 2: Institutional channels

- **Departments and research offices.** Many have pages for research assistants, volunteers or visiting scholars, with application procedures.
- **Residency programs.** Some programs run research tracks or collaborative projects and may accept IMGs for research-only roles.
- **Research coordinators.** Coordinators often know which labs need help and handle onboarding.
- **Fellowships and structured research years.** Some are paid, some are not; terms vary widely. Read the terms before applying.

Institutional programs usually require approvals, training in human-subjects research and data privacy, and sometimes a visa. That paperwork is a sign of legitimacy, not an obstacle to avoid.

## Route 3: Networks

Faculty at your own school, alumni already in the US, co-residents from your country, and mentors you met during clinical experience can introduce you to a project. A warm introduction works better than a cold email. Ask if a physician you worked with during USCE has a project (see [how to get USCE](/wiki/clinical-experience/how-to-get-usce)).

## Route 4: Remote and retrospective projects

Chart reviews, database studies, systematic reviews and meta-analyses can often be done remotely, but only with proper data access and approval. A legitimate project has:

- A named principal investigator at an institution.
- Ethics or institutional review board approval or a documented exemption where needed.
- A clear plan for data access that respects patient privacy.

If the project involves patient data, you should not receive identifiable records over informal channels.

## Questions to ask before you commit

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| Who is the principal investigator and who supervises me day to day? | Accountability and a possible letter |
| What is the project and where is it in the process? | A project at the idea stage may take years |
| What will I do, how many hours, and by when? | Prevents scope creep |
| What authorship can I expect and what would earn it? | Avoids disputes later |
| Is there ethics approval? | Required for most human-subjects work |
| Who will be the corresponding author and how are decisions made? | Clarifies roles |
| Is the target a peer-reviewed journal, a conference, or both? | Sets expectations |

## Authorship integrity

Most journals follow the ICMJE criteria. To be an author you must meet all four: substantial contribution to the conception, design, data acquisition, analysis or interpretation; drafting or critically revising the work; final approval of the version to be published; and agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work. Contributors who do not meet all four should be acknowledged rather than listed as authors.

In practice that means:

- Ask early what you are expected to do and what authorship that earns.
- Do not expect a place on a paper for doing a task that does not meet the criteria, and do not offer one.
- Do not pay for authorship, to buy a place on a paper, or for a guaranteed publication. That is not research experience, and ECFMG's irregular behavior policy treats misleading or improper submissions seriously.
- Describe your role accurately in ERAS.

> [!WARNING] Offers that promise "guaranteed authorship," "first author for a fee," or a fixed number of publications for payment are a serious red flag. They may involve ghost authorship, fabricated work or unreliable journals. They can harm your application and your reputation, so walk away.

## What counts at the stage you are in

Not everything reaches publication, and that is fine. Programs and ERAS let you list work in different stages. See [how programs view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research) for how the categories are read and how to describe work in progress honestly.

> [!IMG TIP] Keep a running record of what you contributed to each project: tasks, dates, supervisor, outputs. When it is time to complete ERAS or ask a mentor for a letter, accurate notes make both easier.
