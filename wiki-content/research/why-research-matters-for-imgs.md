Research can help an IMG application, but how much depends on the specialty, the program and the rest of your profile. It rarely replaces exam results or letters, and it rarely makes up for an application with other major weaknesses. This guide helps you decide when research is worth your time and when it is not.

## What the data shows

The NRMP's 2024 Charting Outcomes report for international medical graduates describes applicants who matched, and did not match, to their preferred specialty. A few points matter for planning:

- **Research is common among IMGs.** Non-US IMGs averaged 2.9 research experiences in their applications, and 76.4% reported at least one. US IMGs (US citizens and permanent residents who studied abroad) averaged 2.4, with 64.7% reporting at least one.
- **The numbers are not verified.** NRMP states that applicants self-report these counts and that the experiences are "not verified or evaluated and quality may vary greatly."
- **More is not clearly better.** NRMP reports that, among both US and non-US IMGs, whether matched applicants reported more research than unmatched applicants varied widely by specialty, with unmatched applicants exceeding matched ones in more than half of the specialties reported.
- **Counts of abstracts, presentations and publications can be very large.** NRMP notes that some applicants report dozens or even hundreds, and that this item is self-reported and may include peer-reviewed articles, abstracts, poster sessions and invited presentations. It adds that some programs may independently verify publications for applicants they are interested in, "but most probably do not."

Take these as context, not targets. The report describes who matched, not why. Applicants who did not match differed from those who did in many ways beyond research.

## When research tends to matter more

These are patterns widely discussed in the field, not rules.

- **Competitive specialties.** Programs in specialties with more applicants than positions may use research to distinguish otherwise similar candidates. In NRMP's report, average counts of abstracts and publications are far higher in some specialties than in others.
- **Academic programs.** University programs with research missions and fellowship pipelines often value scholarly work. The program director survey lists "demonstrated involvement and interest in research" and "interest in academic career" as factors that programs may consider.
- **Applicants with otherwise modest profiles.** Research can add context, such as sustained work and a mentor who can write a letter, but it does not reliably offset concerns that programs screen on first.

## When research matters less

- **Many community-based programs and specialties** focus on exam results, letters, communication and fit, and they may place less weight on publications.
- **When it would displace something more urgent.** If your Step scores are not yet in place, a visa timeline is looming or you have no clinical letters, research may not be the best use of the next six months.
- **When it is low quality or padded.** A long list of marginal items, or work in outlets with questionable standards, can read poorly. Check any journal's credentials before you submit.

## What research can do for you besides the CV line

- It gives you a mentor who knows your work and can write a letter. Letters from research mentors can be useful, particularly when they discuss reliability and analytical skills.
- It builds skills in reading studies, writing and presenting.
- It gives you something concrete to discuss in interviews.
- It can open doors in a specialty you want to join.

## How to decide how much to invest

1. **Check your target specialty.** Look at how IMGs in that specialty present research, using NRMP's specialty pages and program websites. See [choosing a specialty as an IMG](/wiki/specialties/choosing-a-specialty).
2. **Rank your priorities.** Exams, USCE and letters, and timeline constraints come before research if they are not in place.
3. **Pick quality over count.** One project where you did real work, learned something and can speak about it beats many items with an unclear role.
4. **Set a time limit.** Define what you want from the project and when you will stop.
5. **Ask for an honest picture of authorship before starting.** What will your role be, and what authorship does that justify? (See [how programs view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research) for how authorship is read.)

> [!IMG TIP] Ask a mentor or a recent matched IMG in your target specialty what research was on their application and how they used it in interviews. Individual stories are not data, but they help you ask better questions of the program websites.

## What not to do

> [!WARNING] Do not pay for authorship, for a place on a paper or for a "guaranteed publication." Authorship should reflect real intellectual contribution. Misrepresenting your role on an application can have serious consequences, including under ECFMG's irregular behavior rules, and programs can notice patterns that look inflated.

## The bottom line

Research is a tool, not a requirement. For some specialties and programs it matters a lot; for others it is a modest plus. Do the work if you can do it well, if you have a mentor and a plan, and if it does not crowd out higher-priority tasks. Then present it accurately.
