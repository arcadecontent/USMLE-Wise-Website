Publishing a paper is a sequence of steps: define a question, do or document the work, write it to the right reporting standard, pick a suitable journal, submit, survive peer review and revise. Most delays come from the middle steps, and most avoidable rejections come from poor journal fit and incomplete reporting. For a first project, a [case report](/wiki/research/case-reports) or a retrospective study with a mentor is usually more realistic than a trial. This guide covers the path and the norms that apply to everyone. Whether it is worth the time is covered in [why research matters for IMGs](/wiki/research/why-research-matters-for-imgs).

## Step 1: Agree on the plan with your mentor

Before writing, settle:

- The research question and study type.
- Who is doing which part, and who will be first and corresponding author. See [authorship and author order](/wiki/research/authorship-and-author-order).
- Approvals: ethics board or exemption, data access, patient consent where needed.
- A realistic timeline, including what happens if you move away.

> [!IMG TIP] Put the authorship plan in an email to the team early. Disagreements are far easier to resolve before the work is done.

## Step 2: Use the right reporting guideline

The EQUATOR Network lists reporting guidelines by study type, so use the one that fits and follow its checklist while you write. Examples:

| Study type | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Case report | CARE |
| Observational study (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional) | STROBE |
| Systematic review | PRISMA |
| Randomized trial | CONSORT |

Many journals ask for the completed checklist at submission. Following it also makes your paper more complete and more likely to survive review.

## Step 3: Write the manuscript

The standard structure is IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion, plus an abstract, references and declarations (funding, conflicts of interest, ethics, consent, author contributions). Tips:

- Write Methods first. It is the most concrete section.
- Keep the Introduction short and end it with a clear aim.
- Report results without interpretation, and discuss limitations honestly.
- Cite sources you have actually read.
- Disclose any use of AI tools as the journal requires. ICMJE says chatbots should not be listed as authors, and humans remain responsible for AI-assisted text, including its accuracy.
- Use plain, precise language. Clear English matters more than complicated English.

## Step 4: Choose a journal

Choose before you finish writing, because journals differ on format, word limits and article types.

1. Look at where similar papers were published. Check the reference lists of your key sources.
2. Read the journal's aims and scope, and its author instructions, including whether it accepts your article type.
3. Be realistic about reach. A mentor can advise on whether a journal is a sensible target.
4. Verify the journal using [Think. Check. Submit.](https://thinkchecksubmit.org/), DOAJ and the NLM Catalog. See [predatory journals](/wiki/research/predatory-journals) for the full checklist.
5. Check fees. Open access journals often charge an article processing fee, and some waive it. Know the cost before you submit.

## Step 5: Submit

Most journals use an online system. You typically provide:

- The manuscript, figures, tables and checklist.
- A **cover letter** stating the paper's contribution and disclosing related work, such as a preprint, a conference abstract or an earlier report from the same dataset.
- Author details, contributions, conflicts of interest and funding.
- Ethics approval and consent statements.
- Suggested reviewers, if requested.

Submit to **one journal at a time**. ICMJE states that duplicate submission can lead to rejection and, if discovered after publication, to retraction. A conference abstract or poster is not treated as duplicate publication, and preprints are not either, but both should be disclosed.

## Step 6: What happens after submission

1. **Editorial screen.** The editor checks scope and basic quality. Many papers are rejected here without review ("desk rejection").
2. **Peer review.** External experts assess the work. Reviewers are typically anonymous, though policies vary.
3. **Decision.** Common outcomes are accept, minor revision, major revision or reject. Direct acceptance on the first round is uncommon.
4. **Revision.** You revise and send a point-by-point response letter.
5. **Acceptance, proofs and publication.** You check proofs carefully, then the paper is published online, sometimes before it appears in an issue.

Timing varies widely by journal and field. Do not plan an application around a paper that has not yet been submitted.

## Responding to reviewers

- Answer every comment, even the ones you disagree with.
- Be polite and specific. Quote the change and where it is in the manuscript.
- If you decline a request, explain why with evidence.
- Meet the deadline, or ask for an extension.

A "major revision" is a positive signal that the editor wants a revised version, not a rejection.

## If it is rejected

Rejection is normal, including for experienced authors. Read the comments, fix genuine problems and choose a journal that fits better. Do not resubmit unchanged to many journals in sequence without thinking, and do not be tempted to pay for acceptance. Aggressive promises of fast acceptance are a warning sign (see [predatory journals](/wiki/research/predatory-journals)).

## After publication

- Check that your name, affiliation and the author order are correct. Request corrections through the journal, not by editing citations yourself.
- Confirm that the paper is findable in PubMed if the journal is indexed. Indexing varies by journal, and not every journal is in PubMed.
- Describe the work accurately on your CV and in ERAS. See [research on your ERAS application](/wiki/application/research-on-your-application) and [how programs actually view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research).

## Honest expectations

Publishing takes persistence more than brilliance. A smaller project completed with a good mentor and a verified journal is better than a larger one that never finishes. For a poster as an intermediate step, see [posters and conferences](/wiki/research/posters-and-conferences).
