A predatory journal is one that charges authors to publish while skipping the peer review, editorial standards and transparency that real journals provide. There is no official definition or single approved blacklist, so the safest approach is to verify each journal yourself before submitting. This matters for IMGs because a publication in such an outlet can weaken the credibility of your whole research record. For the submission process itself, see [how to publish a paper](/wiki/research/how-to-publish).

## Why it matters for your application

- **Programs may recognize the outlet.** Program directors and faculty see many applications. NRMP data show that research counts are self-reported and unverified, and some programs may check items for applicants they are interested in. See [how programs actually view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research).
- **It can signal poor judgment.** A paper in an outlet with no real review may be discounted, and a list dominated by such items can create doubt about the rest of your application. This is common practice and partly opinion, not an official rule.
- **Your name stays attached.** A paper in a poor journal is hard to remove and may not be findable in standard databases.
- **The work may be unusable.** Some journals will not accept the same work again once it has appeared elsewhere. A paper that was never really reviewed also cannot be cited with confidence.
- **Scams overlap.** Paid authorship and "guaranteed publication" schemes often send authors to such outlets. See [authorship and author order](/wiki/research/authorship-and-author-order) and [research fellowships](/wiki/research/research-fellowships).

## The verification checklist

Work through this before you submit anywhere unfamiliar. A single red flag is a reason to look harder, not a verdict.

### 1. Think. Check. Submit.

[Think. Check. Submit.](https://thinkchecksubmit.org/) is an initiative that gives authors a checklist for deciding whether a journal or publisher is trustworthy. Its approach: think about whether the journal is right for your work, check it against the checklist, and submit only if you can answer yes to the checklist questions. Use its journal checklist before you commit.

### 2. DOAJ

The Directory of Open Access Journals only lists open access journals that meet its criteria. DOAJ says these include an ISSN, a named editorial board, stated peer review and clearly disclosed author charges, and it re-evaluates and removes journals that no longer comply. Search by title or ISSN at [doaj.org](https://doaj.org/). Being listed is a good sign. Not being listed is not proof of a problem, since subscription journals are outside DOAJ's scope.

### 3. The NLM Catalog

The National Library of Medicine's NLM Catalog lets you look up a journal and see its status. Two things are easy to confuse:

- **PubMed** includes citations from MEDLINE, from journals deposited in PubMed Central, and from other sources.
- **MEDLINE** is the curated subset of journals selected by NLM after a scientific review, and its records are indexed with MeSH headings.

NLM Catalog filters for journals "currently indexed in MEDLINE" exist, and NLM's selection process uses a technical review committee. Check the current catalog interface and NLM's MEDLINE pages for exact steps. A journal's own claim that it is "indexed in PubMed" should be confirmed by searching for the journal's title and ISSN in PubMed itself.

### 4. The journal's own site

- **Named editorial board** with real affiliations that you can confirm on the institutions' own sites. Ask whether those people actually know they are listed.
- **Clear peer review description**, including who reviews and how long it takes.
- **Transparent fees**, stated before submission, with any waivers explained.
- **Contact details** with a real address and a working, professional email.
- **Policies** on ethics, consent, conflicts of interest, corrections and retractions, that refer to bodies such as ICMJE and COPE.
- **Stable, consistent name and ISSN**, checked at issn.org.

### 5. How it contacted you

- Unsolicited emails flattering you as an "esteemed" expert.
- Promises of acceptance in days.
- Pressure to submit by a deadline.
- Invitations for topics far outside your work.

### 6. Check real papers

Open a few recent articles. Are they readable, with methods and references? Are the authors from varied institutions? Are issues published regularly with dates that make sense? Are corrections and retractions visible?

### 7. Ask a person

Ask your mentor or a medical librarian. A librarian at a university or hospital can often verify a journal in minutes, and a mentor will know the field's recognized outlets.

## Warning signs summary

| Sign | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Fees hidden until after acceptance | Transparency is a basic expectation |
| Acceptance in days, little or no review | Real review takes time |
| Fake or unverifiable editorial board | Suggests no real oversight |
| Claims of indexing you cannot confirm | Misleading |
| Mimics the name of a well-known journal | Easy to confuse |
| Very broad scope covering all of medicine | Hard to review credibly |
| Aggressive solicitation | A business model based on volume |

## What not to assume

- **A fee does not make a journal predatory.** Many legitimate open access journals charge article processing fees. The question is whether the fee is disclosed, the review is real and the claims are true.
- **Impact factors can be faked.** Fake metrics exist. Confirm metrics from the provider, not from the journal's site.
- **A big publisher is not a guarantee.** Large publishers also have journals of uneven quality, so judge the specific journal.
- **No list is perfect.** Third-party lists can be out of date, disputed or commercial. Use them as a prompt to look closely, not as the final word.

## If you are unsure

1. Do not submit yet.
2. Ask your mentor or a librarian.
3. Pick a society journal or a journal you have verified through the steps above.
4. If you find a problem after submission, withdraw in writing before publication, following the journal's withdrawal policy.

> [!WARNING] Paper mills and authorship sellers often work together with questionable journals. COPE describes paper mills as organizations that sell manufactured manuscripts or authorship. Do not pay to be added to a paper, and do not accept a "guaranteed" publication.

## Bottom line

You do not need a blacklist. You need a habit: before submitting, confirm the journal in DOAJ or the NLM Catalog, run it through Think. Check. Submit., read a few papers, and ask someone with experience. A smaller number of credible outputs reads better than a long list of doubtful ones.
