To make a scientific research poster, choose one clear message, arrange short sections in columns that read left to right and top to bottom, put your key result and one strong figure in the center, and keep the text brief. This page covers layout, where the results go, affiliations, results tables, figures, a QR code and how to present your poster in about two minutes.

It builds on [posters and conferences](/wiki/research/posters-and-conferences), which covers choosing a conference, writing the abstract and describing the presentation honestly on your application. Here the focus is the poster itself. Most of the layout guidance below comes from university library guides, which agree on the basics and differ on details, so the page says when a figure is one library's suggestion.

## What a research poster is for

A poster has two jobs. It lets a passer-by understand your project in about a minute, and it gives you something to point to while you talk. The University at Buffalo's library guide puts it plainly: a poster presentation is not the pasting of a scholarly article on poster board. A poster that tries to be the whole paper is read by nobody.

For IMGs, a poster is often the quickest visible output from a project, and a good poster session is one of the better places to meet mentors. How programs weigh posters is covered in [how programs actually view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research).

## Before you design: the conference's rules and your abstract

1. **Read the conference's poster instructions.** Size, orientation (portrait or landscape), print or electronic format, templates and the file type differ between meetings. Design to the specification, not the other way around.
2. **Match your submitted abstract.** The poster should present the same question, methods, results and conclusion as the abstract that was accepted. Do not change the numbers or the conclusion.
3. **Agree the authors and their order** with your team before the poster is printed. See [authorship and author order](/wiki/research/authorship-and-author-order).
4. **Check who must approve the poster.** Many institutions want to see it first, and some require approval to use their name or logo.
5. **Plan the printing time.** Large-format printing takes time and can go wrong, so do not leave it to the last day.

## How to create a research poster: the layout

Berkeley's library guide recommends a linear flow from left to right and top to bottom, with text and images aligned in columns and a logical progression of ideas. The University of Illinois guide gives similar advice, and suggests organizing the poster around the major points of your research.

A standard scientific layout follows the IMRaD structure of a paper, in short blocks:

| Section | What goes there | Practice notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Title and header** | A title that states the finding or topic, the authors, affiliations and contact details | Readable from a distance. Avoid a very long title |
| **Introduction or background** | The problem and your question, in a few sentences | End with a one-sentence aim |
| **Methods** | Design, setting, sample, main outcome and analysis | A small flow diagram or schematic often says more than a paragraph |
| **Results** | The main finding, one strong figure and, if needed, a small table | The largest area on the poster |
| **Conclusions** | What the data support, one or two limitations and the next step | Short, numbered or bulleted |
| **References, acknowledgments, funding** | A short list, with the full list on a handout or behind a QR code | Declare funding and conflicts as the conference requires |

Berkeley describes a common pattern as introduction and methods in the left column, results in the middle and the remaining text in the right column. If your poster is landscape with three columns, that works well. Portrait posters often use two or three columns, stacked in the same order.

> [!TIP] Make the logical order obvious to a stranger who has never seen your project. Number the sections or use clear headings, and use alignment and white space, not arrows, to guide the eye.

## Where do the results go on a research poster?

There is no rule that results belong in the top right. The question comes up because posters read left to right, and people worry that the key finding will be lost. The answer, in our opinion:

- **Put the key result in the central block,** where the eye lands, and give it the largest area. This matches the common pattern above.
- **Lead the block with the finding,** not just the word "Results." A heading such as "[Outcome] was higher in [group A] than [group B]" tells a passer-by what you found. Use your own true finding.
- **Make one figure the hero.** Place it at eye level, which is usually the middle of the poster, not at the bottom.
- **Offer an optional takeaway box** at the top or end with the one-sentence message. Some conferences or templates ask for one.
- **Follow the conference template.** If the meeting prescribes a layout or reading order, use it.

## How to show affiliations on a poster

The University of Illinois guide says every poster must include institutional affiliation, along with your name and contact information. In practice:

- **Under the title,** list each author's name. Use matching superscript numbers when authors belong to different institutions, and list the institutions beneath.
- **List the institution where the work was done.** If the conference or your mentor asks for a current affiliation too, ask how they want it shown. Do not list an affiliation you do not hold.
- **Use logos only with permission.** An institution may have rules about its name and branding, and crowded rows of logos make a poster look cluttered. Ask your mentor.
- **Add a professional contact line:** an email you check, and optionally a profile such as ORCID. See [PubMed and ORCID setup](/wiki/research/pubmed-and-orcid-setup).
- **Put the author order your team agreed.** For disputes and what each position means, see [authorship and author order](/wiki/research/authorship-and-author-order).

> [!IMG TIP] If you are between positions or doing unpaid research, say so to your mentor and ask how the institution wants you listed. Write your role accurately, because programs may ask about anything you list. See [research on your ERAS application](/wiki/application/research-on-your-application).

## Building a scientific poster results table

A table on a poster is not a copy of the table in your paper. Opinion and practice:

- **Use a table only for exact values** that a reader needs to compare, and use a figure for patterns and trends.
- **Keep it small.** Include only the rows and columns that support your message. Put the rest in the paper or a handout.
- **Give it a descriptive title,** and put units and sample sizes in the headers. Berkeley's guide recommends descriptive titles and legends for visuals.
- **Use large, plain text** with enough row spacing to read from a distance. Align numbers by their decimal point.
- **Highlight the key row or number** in bold or a muted color. Do not make everything bold.
- **Report what your analysis actually produced,** including the effect measure and its uncertainty, in the same form as your abstract. Do not add or round up results for the poster.
- **Remove clutter.** Fewer lines and borders are easier to read.

## Figures that carry the result

- **One main figure** that a stranger can understand in a few seconds.
- **Label axes and units,** and keep fonts large enough to read from a distance.
- **Remove chart clutter.** Berkeley suggests dropping grid lines from graphs and using muted colors sparingly.
- **Write a short caption:** CMU's library guide advises using just a few sentences to describe figures and using graphics to tell the story where possible.
- **Choose colors that work for color-blind readers,** and check that the figure is still readable in grayscale. This is practice, not a rule.
- **Use high-resolution images.** Blurry images look worse on a large print than on a screen.

## Text, fonts, color and white space

Library guides agree on the main mistake: too much text. The Illinois guide calls "too much information" the number one mistake, and CMU's guide says less is more and leaves white space around all text and figures.

- **Write short sections** in plain language, using bullets where they help.
- **Font sizes differ between guides.** Illinois suggests at least 24 point for text and at least 72 point for the title, while Berkeley suggests roughly 22 to 28 point for body text on a single-sheet poster. Check the conference's requirements, and test by reading the print from a couple of meters away.
- **Use one or two fonts** and a restrained color scheme. Consistency in layout, colors, fonts and sizes looks professional.
- **Leave empty space** between sections. Crowded text is hard to read.
- **Keep references short.** Put the full list on a handout or behind a QR code.

## Using a QR code on a poster

Yale's library guide says QR codes can link to external resources such as a digital copy of the poster, supplementary files, a published paper, a lab or personal website, a survey or contact information, and that a code should be at least 5 inches square on the poster. Practical steps:

1. **Decide what it opens:** a PDF of the poster, the abstract, your contact page or the paper if it is published.
2. **Label it,** for example "Scan for the PDF and references."
3. **Host the file somewhere stable.** Use a PDF link that will keep working, and set sharing as view-only.
4. **Test it** with a phone at print scale, from a short distance, before you send the file to the printer.
5. **Check the rules.** The conference may limit QR codes or links, and a journal may have a policy on what has been shared before submission. ICMJE says its recommendations do not prevent a journal from considering a complete report after an abstract or poster displayed at a scientific meeting, and that authors must tell a journal about work already reported elsewhere, so mention the poster in your cover letter if you later submit a paper. See [how to publish a paper](/wiki/research/how-to-publish).
6. **Do not link to patient data,** identifiable images or anything you do not have permission to share.

## Medical research posters by project type

| Project | Main visual | Extra points (practice) |
|---|---|---|
| **Cohort, case-control or cross-sectional study** | One bar or line chart, or a simple table | A short flow of how many participants were included, and the main limitation |
| **Randomized or interventional study** | The primary outcome figure | A small participant flow diagram |
| **Systematic review or meta-analysis poster** | A forest plot, with a flow diagram of the studies screened and included | The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram shows the flow of information through the phases of a review, including records found, kept and dropped with reasons |
| **Case report or case series** | A timeline, plus a figure or image with identifiers removed | Consent and privacy. See [case reports](/wiki/research/case-reports) |
| **Quality improvement project** | A run chart or before-and-after chart | The change tested and what you would do next |

Use the reporting guideline that fits your study type, because posters follow the same logic as papers.

## Privacy, consent and honesty

- **Remove identifiable patient information** from text, images and slides. ICMJE says patients have a right to privacy that should not be violated without informed consent, that names, initials and hospital numbers should not be published, and that masking the eye region in a photograph is inadequate protection of anonymity. Its page is about publication, but the same care is sensible on a poster that hangs in a public hall. See [case reports](/wiki/research/case-reports).
- **Present only what you have.** Do not pre-fill results, and do not include analyses that were not done.
- **Say what you did and what the team did.** If you are asked, be accurate about your role.
- **Acknowledge funding and collaborators.** Follow the conference's rules.

> [!WARNING] Do not list a poster you have not presented, or an abstract that was only submitted, as accepted. Misrepresenting research is an integrity problem. See [posters and conferences](/wiki/research/posters-and-conferences).

## How to present your poster in two minutes

Opinion: prepare a 30 second version, a two-minute version and answers to likely questions. A two-minute version might run like this:

| Part | Time (opinion) | What to say |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | About 15 seconds | The question and why it matters, in one or two sentences |
| **Methods** | About 25 seconds | What you did, with whom and how many, pointing to the design |
| **Key result** | About 50 seconds | Point to the main figure and say the finding in plain words |
| **Meaning and limitation** | About 20 seconds | What it suggests, what it does not show and what comes next |
| **Invite questions** | About 10 seconds | "What would you like to know more about?" |

An illustrative template, not a script to copy: "We wanted to know whether [question]. We looked at [design and sample]. Here is what we found: [finding, pointing to the figure]. That suggests [meaning], but [limitation]. I would be glad to answer questions."

Presenting tips:

- **Stand beside the poster,** not in front of it, and point to the figure as you speak.
- **Do not read the poster aloud.** The poster is the visual, and you are the explanation.
- **Have a one-page handout** with a QR code and your contact details.
- **Ask for names and emails,** because poster sessions are where mentors and collaborators appear.
- **Take a photo of the poster** and of the program page for your records.

## Questions to expect

1. Why did you ask this question?
2. How was the sample chosen, and how big was it?
3. What are the main limitations?
4. What did you do yourself in this project?
5. What is the next step, and will it be published?

Prepare an honest, one-sentence answer to each.

## A pre-print checklist

1. The conference's size, orientation and file format match.
2. The title states the finding or topic, and the authors, affiliations and contact details are correct.
3. The numbers match the abstract and the data.
4. One main figure and, if needed, one small table.
5. Text is short, large and readable from a couple of meters.
6. Patient information is removed and consent is documented.
7. The QR code scans, and the file it opens is the right one.
8. Someone outside your field has read it for sense and spelling.

## After the poster

Record the presentation accurately on your CV and in ERAS, with the meeting, date, location and authors, and keep the acceptance email and program page as proof. If the project supports it, turn it into a paper, as described in [how to publish a paper](/wiki/research/how-to-publish).

## Common mistakes

- **Pasting the paper onto the poster.** Show the story, not every detail.
- **Burying the result** in a tiny figure or a long table.
- **Changing the numbers** between the abstract and the poster.
- **Using too many fonts, colors or logos.**
- **Forgetting the affiliation or contact line.**
- **Not testing the QR code,** or linking to something you cannot share.
- **Leaving printing to the last day.**
- **Reading from the poster** instead of speaking to the person in front of you.
