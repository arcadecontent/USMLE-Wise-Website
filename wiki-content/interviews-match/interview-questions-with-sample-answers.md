Residency interview questions fall into a small number of groups, and the strongest answers are short, specific and true. This page gives 15 common residency interview questions with what the interviewer is really asking, an illustrative sample answer and a mistake to avoid, with extra attention to the questions IMGs get.

It owns the sample answers. The overview of question types is in [common interview questions](/wiki/interviews-match/common-interview-questions), the STAR method and story bank are in [behavioral interview answers](/wiki/interviews-match/behavioral-interview-answers), and the opening question has its own page: [tell me about yourself](/wiki/interviews-match/tell-me-about-yourself).

> [!NOTE] Every sample answer below is an illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team. None is a real applicant's answer, and none is a script. Placeholders in square brackets are for you to fill with your own true facts. Do not copy these answers. Interviewers ask follow-up questions that expose a story that is not yours.

## Residency interview advice: how to use these answers

AAMC groups interview questions into three types: general questions (describe yourself, why this program), behavioral questions (describe a past experience) and situational questions (what would you do if). The 15 below cover all three.

1. **Prepare bullet points, not scripts.** AAMC advises preparing without memorizing so the conversation stays natural and you still answer what was asked.
2. **Build a story bank.** Six to eight true events can serve most behavioral questions. See [behavioral interview answers](/wiki/interviews-match/behavioral-interview-answers).
3. **Say what you did.** Use "I" for your own part, and credit others for theirs.
4. **Be accurate, always.** NRMP's Match agreement makes you responsible for the accuracy of what you tell programs. Every date and detail must match your ERAS application.
5. **Keep it short.** Opinion: about 60 to 90 seconds for most answers and up to two minutes for a behavioral story. Then stop and let the interviewer follow up.
6. **Practice out loud,** ideally with someone who interrupts with follow-up questions.

## What good example interview responses have in common

Whatever the question, the sample answers below share the same shape. Use it to build your own.

- **They answer the question in the first sentence,** then support it.
- **They contain one specific, true detail** that only you could give, such as a patient, a rotation or a decision.
- **They show your own role** and credit others for theirs.
- **They end with what changed,** a lesson or a result, and then stop.
- **They are honest about difficulty.** Opinion: confident answers that admit a hard part usually land better than polished ones that sound perfect.

## Opening questions

### 1. Tell me about yourself

**What they are really asking:** Who are you professionally, and what should we know before we start?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "I am a physician from [country], and I graduated from [school] in [year]. Since then I have worked in [role], and I completed [a US clinical experience] last year. What drew me to [specialty] is [one specific reason]. This year I have been [one recent, relevant activity]. I am looking for a program where I can [one thing you want from training], and your program stood out because of [one real feature]."

**Mistake to avoid:** reciting your CV from medical school onward. Three full examples, plus a framework and timing, are on [tell me about yourself](/wiki/interviews-match/tell-me-about-yourself).

### 2. Why this specialty?

**What they are really asking:** Do you understand the daily work of this specialty, and will you stay with it?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite, internal medicine):**

> "During my internship in my home country, I followed a patient with poorly controlled diabetes who was readmitted several times with the same complications. What interested me was not only the acute treatment but working out why the plan kept failing: medication cost, health literacy, follow-up. I enjoyed that detective work, and I enjoyed the long relationship with the patient. I have seen the same pattern in [my US clinical experience], and that is why internal medicine fits me better than the other fields I rotated through."

**Mistake to avoid:** "I like helping people" or "it is a broad field." Give one real experience. If you are applying to more than one specialty, prepare an honest answer for each. See [applying to multiple specialties](/wiki/application/applying-to-multiple-specialties).

### 3. Why this program?

**What they are really asking:** Did you research us, and is your interest real?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "Three things. First, [a real feature from the program's website, for example how residents follow their own clinic panel]: that is the kind of long-term care I want to learn. Second, the teaching: when I spoke with one of your residents at the virtual social, they described [one specific feature], and it matched what I read. Third, your patient population. I have worked with [a similar population], so I would be useful early on."

**Mistake to avoid:** praising the program's reputation or city only, or naming a feature that is not true of the program. Check the website and resident pages before every interview.

### 4. Why the United States, and why not practice at home?

**What they are really asking:** Are you committed and realistic, and will you speak respectfully about your own system?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "My training at home gave me a strong clinical foundation, and I value it. I want to train in the US because of [one specific reason: the structure of graded responsibility, access to a subspecialty, research training]. I have spent [time] in US clinical settings, so I understand how teams and documentation differ. After training, my plan is to [honest plan], and I expect to stay connected with colleagues at home."

**Mistake to avoid:** criticizing your home system, giving only money or lifestyle as the reason, or describing a long-term plan you do not hold.

## Behavioral and ethical questions

### 5. What is your biggest weakness?

**What they are really asking:** Do you know yourself, and can you change?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "I tend to carry a problem alone for too long before asking for help. On my [rotation], I spent most of a shift on a task I should have raised with my senior. She told me so, kindly. Since then I use a rule: if I am stuck, I ask within a short, set time. On my last rotation, I asked early about [a specific problem], and it saved time for the whole team."

**Mistake to avoid:** a fake weakness ("I work too hard"), a weakness that would be a patient-safety problem, or no sign of improvement.

### 6. Tell me about a time you worked effectively on a team

**What they are really asking:** What is your role on a team, and do you give others credit?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "On a busy ward during a staffing gap, discharges were being delayed because summaries were written late in the day. I suggested to our team that we divide the discharge tasks at morning rounds. I wrote the summaries for patients leaving that day, a nurse arranged follow-up appointments and the intern handled medication reconciliation. Our patients left earlier that week, and the senior resident asked us to keep the system. What I learned is that a small, clear division of work often solves a problem that effort alone does not."

**Mistake to avoid:** saying "we" for everything. US interviewers want your own role. See the pitfalls in [behavioral interview answers](/wiki/interviews-match/behavioral-interview-answers).

### 7. Tell me about a conflict with a colleague or a supervisor

**What they are really asking:** Can you disagree respectfully, including across a hierarchy?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "I once disagreed with a senior about discharging a patient. I had noticed that the patient was breathless again when walking. I asked to speak privately, told her exactly what I had seen, and asked what she was seeing. We agreed to repeat the assessment before deciding, and the patient stayed another day. I learned to bring observations, not conclusions, and to ask the person I disagree with what they see."

**Mistake to avoid:** a story where you were simply right and everyone else wrong, or a story in which you stayed silent. Show respectful escalation.

### 8. Tell me about a mistake you made

**What they are really asking:** Will you report errors, and do you learn from them?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "At the end of a shift I did not hand over a pending result for one of my patients. The next team found it the following morning. No harm resulted. I told my supervisor as soon as I realized, we reviewed the patient together, and I now keep a written handover list that includes every pending test. I also ask the person taking over to read back the list."

**Mistake to avoid:** choosing a trivial "mistake" or blaming others. Name the error, the effect, how you reported it and what you changed.

### 9. An ethical scenario: a family asks you not to tell the patient the diagnosis

**What they are really asking:** Can you reason through an ethical problem, including a cultural one, without freezing or acting alone?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "I would first listen to the family's concern, because it usually comes from wanting to protect the patient. Then I would ask the patient privately how much they want to know, because the patient's own wishes come first. If they want to know, I would tell them honestly and gently, with the family present if the patient agrees. I would involve my senior, and an ethics or palliative care service if it helped, follow hospital policy and document the conversation. I would respect the family's culture without letting it override the patient's right to information."

**Mistake to avoid:** an absolute ("I would always tell" or "I would never tell"), or deciding alone. It is fine to say you would ask a senior. This scenario matters for IMGs because in some health systems families are told first, and interviewers want to see that you understand the US approach to disclosure and consent.

## The questions IMGs get

### 10. Can you explain the gap since graduation?

**What they are really asking:** What did you do with the time, and are you clinically current?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "After I graduated in [year], I spent [period] preparing for my USMLE exams while working part-time at [a clinic or research group]. In [period], I completed [an observership or clinical position]. I understand the gap is something programs look at. In that time I passed [exams] and [one concrete outcome]. For the past [period] I have been in a clinical role at [place], and I can tell you more about that if useful."

**Mistake to avoid:** a vague answer, a long apology, or claiming activities you cannot document. Your dates must match ERAS. See [gap years and how to explain them](/wiki/application/gap-years-and-how-to-explain) and [years since graduation](/wiki/application/years-since-graduation).

### 11. Tell me about a failed exam attempt

**What they are really asking:** Are you honest, and what changed afterwards?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "I did not pass [exam] on my first attempt. I underestimated how much timed practice I needed and relied on reading instead. I changed my plan: I used timed question blocks, measured my readiness with self-assessments, and fixed the weak areas. I passed on my next attempt, and my [Step 2 CK result or clinical evaluations] since then reflect that. I am happy to answer questions about it."

**Mistake to avoid:** blaming the exam, the test center or your health without evidence, minimizing it, or spending more than a minute on it. NRMP's Match agreement requires accurate disclosure of matters relevant to a program's decision. See [failed attempts on your application](/wiki/application/failed-attempts-on-application).

### 12. How will you adapt to working in the US healthcare system?

**What they are really asking:** Can you be useful on day one, and do you know what you do not know?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "During my [observership or elective], I saw that US teams give residents graded responsibility inside a structure: daily rounds, electronic documentation and a team that includes nurses, pharmacists and case managers. What transfers directly from my training is history taking, examination and long hospital shifts. What I know I still have to learn is [insurance and discharge planning]. I plan to ask case managers to walk me through a real discharge, and to ask my seniors for feedback early."

**Mistake to avoid:** claiming you already understand the US system after a short exposure, or comparing it unfavorably with your own.

### 13. Will you need visa sponsorship?

**What they are really asking:** Can we hire you, and do you understand what that requires?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "I will need [visa type] sponsorship. I have read your program's visa policy on your website, and it says [what it says]. If your GME office needs any documents from me, I can send them promptly. If I have misunderstood something, I would like to hear it now."

If you are unsure what a program can sponsor, say so plainly: "I am not sure which visa categories your institution sponsors for residents, and I will confirm that with your GME office."

**Mistake to avoid:** guessing, promising you can obtain any visa, giving a legal opinion, or being evasive. NRMP's Match agreement makes accurate information about your status your responsibility. Programs publish their own visa policies, and the rules change, so confirm them with the program, with the official government sources and, where you need legal advice, with a qualified immigration attorney. We do not give visa or legal advice on this page. For the main categories, see [J-1 vs H-1B](/wiki/visas/j1-vs-h1b).

## Closing questions

### 14. Why should we rank you?

**What they are really asking:** What do you bring, and can you say it without bragging?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "Three things I can show you. I have [clinical experience that fits your population]. Colleagues describe me as reliable: [one example]. And I use feedback: [one example]. I want to train where I can contribute from the first week and keep growing, and [program] is such a place."

**Mistake to avoid:** vague praise of yourself, comparing yourself with other applicants, or asking the interviewer to commit. NRMP says you may express interest, but you may not solicit a statement from a program that implies a commitment to rank you.

### 15. Do you have any questions for us?

**What they are really asking:** Are you curious, prepared and serious about fit?

**Sample answer (illustrative composite):**

> "Yes. How do residents get feedback during a rotation? How do you support residents who are new to the US system? And what do recent graduates say they wish they had known in their first month?"

**Mistake to avoid:** asking something the website already answers, leading with vacation or salary, or asking where you rank. See [questions to ask programs](/wiki/interviews-match/questions-to-ask-programs).

## Questions you may decline

NRMP's Match agreement says programs may not ask you to reveal where else you applied or interviewed, your preference signals, or how you will rank programs, and may not suggest that your place on a list depends on a statement of preference. If you are asked, you can say politely that you are keeping that private and return to why you are interested in the program. See [post-interview communication](/wiki/interviews-match/post-interview-communication) and the [interview season overview](/wiki/interviews-match/interview-season-overview) for the rules and what to do if a program presses.

## Specialty-specific questions

Many programs add questions about the specialty itself, such as why internal medicine or why anesthesiology, or ask you to talk through a patient you cared for. A reliable preparation method (opinion): choose one patient story from your experience in the specialty, and be ready to describe the patient, your role, the reasoning behind the plan and what you learned. For how the field works, see the guides to [internal medicine](/wiki/specialties/internal-medicine) and [anesthesiology](/wiki/specialties/anesthesiology).

## A practice routine

1. Write bullet points for each of the 15 questions using your own experiences.
2. Say each answer aloud and time it. Cut anything over two minutes.
3. Record yourself, and listen for rambling, filler words and unclear endings.
4. Have a mentor or advisor run a mock interview with follow-up questions such as "Why?" and "What would you do differently?"
5. For virtual interviews, test your camera, audio and connection. See [virtual interview setup](/wiki/interviews-match/virtual-interview-setup).
6. Review your ERAS application the night before so dates and details match your answers.

> [!IMG TIP] If you can improve only one thing, make your answers shorter and more specific. A concrete story with your own role clearly stated beats a long general speech.
