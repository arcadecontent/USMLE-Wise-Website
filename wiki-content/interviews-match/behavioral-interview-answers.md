A behavioral question asks you to describe something you actually did: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague." Interviewers use your answer to predict how you will behave on a team and with patients. This page gives a structure, a bank of stories to prepare, and illustrative outlines. It builds on [common interview questions](/wiki/interviews-match/common-interview-questions), which lists the usual topics.

## Why programs ask these questions

In NRMP's 2026 program director survey, interpersonal skills (89 percent of respondents) and interview interactions (84 percent) were among the leading ranking factors. Behavioral questions test those directly. They also check for honesty and self-awareness, which are hard to read from scores.

## The structure: situation, action, result, lesson

Many people teach STAR (situation, task, action, result). A compact version for a one-to-two-minute answer:

1. **Situation and task (15 to 25 percent of your time):** who, where, what was at stake. Two or three sentences.
2. **Action (50 percent):** what you personally did and why. Use "I", not "we", for your part.
3. **Result (15 to 20 percent):** what happened, in concrete terms where possible.
4. **Lesson (one sentence):** what you do differently now.

> [!KEY] Spend most of your time on your actions and your reasoning. Long set-ups and vague endings are the usual failures.

## Build a story bank

Prepare six to eight real events and map them to common themes. One story can serve several themes.

| Theme | Source of stories |
|---|---|
| Conflict with a colleague or supervisor | Ward, research team, student group |
| A mistake or near miss | Clinical, exams, administrative |
| A difficult or upset patient or family | Clinical rotation, home-country training |
| Leadership or taking initiative | Project, teaching, quality improvement |
| Receiving critical feedback | Evaluations, supervisor comments |
| Working with limited resources | Training in a low-resource setting |
| Ethical concern or uncomfortable situation | Consent, confidentiality, honesty |
| Adapting to a new system | Moving countries, new language, new hospital |

Check each story against your ERAS application so that dates and details match. See [ERAS experiences section](/wiki/application/eras-experiences-section).

## Illustrative sample outlines

These outlines are invented to show the shape of an answer. They are not real people or real events. Do not copy them. Your answer must come from what actually happened to you.

### Example 1: A disagreement with a team member

*Question: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague.*

- **Situation:** A fictional medical student and a fellow student disagreed about who would present on rounds when the team was short-staffed.
- **Action:** The student asked to talk privately after rounds, listened to the colleague's constraints, proposed a rotating schedule and checked it with the senior resident.
- **Result:** The schedule was adopted and both presented equally for the rest of the rotation.
- **Lesson:** Raise concerns early and privately, and propose a fair process instead of arguing about one case.

### Example 2: A mistake

*Question: Tell me about a mistake you made.*

- **Situation:** A fictional trainee transcribed a medication dose in a hand-written note from a verbal order.
- **Action:** A nurse's question caught it before administration. The trainee thanked the nurse, corrected the note, told the supervising physician at once and now reads back every verbal order.
- **Result:** No harm reached the patient. The unit later added a read-back step to its checklist.
- **Lesson:** Report errors immediately and build checks into your habits.

### Example 3: A difficult patient interaction

*Question: Describe a time a patient or family was upset.*

- **Situation:** A fictional family member was angry about waiting for results.
- **Action:** The trainee acknowledged the frustration, explained what was pending and why, gave an honest time frame, and asked the nurse to update the family.
- **Result:** The family calmed and later thanked the team for the update.
- **Lesson:** Anger often reflects uncertainty, and regular honest updates reduce it.

Notice what these share: a specific event, a clear personal action, honest ownership and a practical lesson.

## Pitfalls that affect IMGs

- **"We" answers.** Many training cultures reward team credit. US interviewers want to know what *you* did, so name your role clearly while still crediting others.
- **Overly modest or deferential answers.** Being direct about your contribution is expected. Speak about your decisions with confidence and without exaggeration.
- **Criticizing your home system.** Describe constraints factually and focus on what you learned and did. Avoid sounding as if you are comparing the two systems.
- **Hierarchy stories.** If a story involves disagreeing with a senior colleague, show respectful escalation, not silence or confrontation.
- **Hypothetical answers.** "I would always..." is weaker than "Here is what I did." If you lack an example, say so and offer the closest real one.
- **Sensitive details.** Do not name patients or share identifiable information, even in a past-tense story.

## Questions you must handle carefully

Some questions touch exam failures, gaps and visa status. NRMP's applicant materials require accurate disclosure of matters relevant to ranking, including USMLE failures, delays and visa needs, and say that omissions can be treated as violations. A behavioral framing of a failure ("Tell me about a setback") is a chance to show insight, but your facts must match your application. Also remember that programs cannot ask where else you applied or how you will rank them. See [interview season overview](/wiki/interviews-match/interview-season-overview).

## Practice routine

1. Write one-paragraph outlines (not scripts) for each story.
2. Say each aloud and time it. Cut anything over two minutes.
3. Record yourself, or practice with a mentor who will interrupt with follow-up questions.
4. Prepare for "Why?" and "What would you do differently?" follow-ups on each story.
5. Review stories the night before, then stop. Over-rehearsed answers sound recited.

If you have your own mentors or advisors, ask them to run a mock interview. For virtual practice, see [virtual interview setup](/wiki/interviews-match/virtual-interview-setup).

> [!NOTE] ACGME expects residents to show professionalism, interpersonal and communication skills, and a commitment to improving through feedback. Behavioral answers that show those qualities honestly match what training programs are built to develop.
