Residency interviews are more predictable than they feel. Most questions fall into a dozen categories, and the strongest answers are specific, honest and short. This page lists the common questions and shows how to structure answers. It does not offer scripts, because interviewers notice scripts.

## What interviewers are actually assessing

In NRMP's 2026 Program Director Survey, the leading factors for ranking were interpersonal skills (89 percent of respondents), interactions during interviews (84 percent) and feedback from current residents (74 percent). So they are assessing three things: Can you communicate with patients and teams? Will you fit and stay? Are you honest and reflective?

## A simple structure for answers

For behavioral questions, use a short structure often called STAR:

- **Situation:** one sentence of context.
- **Task:** what you were responsible for.
- **Action:** what you did, in the first person.
- **Result:** what happened and what you learned.

Prepare six to eight real stories that can be reused: a difficult patient, a team conflict, a mistake, a time you led, a time you were wrong, a time you taught, an ethical concern. Do not invent. Interviewers ask follow-up questions that expose invented stories.

## The questions you should expect

### Tell me about yourself

Give a 60 to 90 second professional summary: where you trained, what drew you to the specialty, the most relevant experience and why this program. Do not recite your CV.

### Why this specialty?

Give one or two specific reasons grounded in experience (a patient, rotation or project), not generalities such as "I like helping people". If you are applying to more than one specialty, be honest and prepare a clear answer for each.

### Why this program?

Name two or three real features: the patient population, curriculum, teaching culture, a resident you spoke to. Check the program website and its resident pages before the interview.

### Why the United States, and why not your home country?

Be honest and positive. Describe what you want to learn and contribute, and your long-term plan. Avoid criticizing your home system.

### What are your strengths and weaknesses?

Choose strengths backed by evidence. For a weakness, choose a real one, say what you do about it and give a recent example of improvement.

### Tell me about a time you made a mistake or had a conflict

Pick an example where you owned the problem, communicated and changed something. Do not choose a story where you were simply right and others wrong.

### How would you handle a difficult patient or an ethical dilemma?

Describe your reasoning: safety first, communicate, involve the team and follow policy. Avoid absolutes. It is acceptable to say you would ask a senior for help.

## The harder questions for IMGs

### Exam failure or delays

NRMP's applicant materials say applicants must disclose academic issues, USMLE failures, score delays and testing eligibility issues, and that omissions relevant to a program's decision can be treated as violations. So disclose, briefly and factually.

A good answer has four parts: what happened, what you learned, what you changed, and the evidence since (for example, an improved Step 2 CK). Do not spend more than a minute on it.

### Gaps since graduation

Account for the time honestly: clinical work, research, exam preparation, family or health reasons. If you were studying only, say what you studied and what you produced. Programs can screen on years since graduation, so be ready.

### Visa status and sponsorship

NRMP lists visa requirements among the items applicants must report accurately. Know your visa type, what the program needs to sponsor, and your timeline. See [J-1 vs H-1B](/wiki/visas/j1-vs-h1b). Answer in one or two sentences.

### Your English and communication

Programs value clear communication. Speak at a measured pace, ask for clarification if needed and avoid memorized phrases. Practice with native English speakers if possible.

### Where else are you interviewing and how will you rank us?

NRMP says programs cannot ask where you applied or interviewed or ask you to disclose your ranking. If asked, you can politely say you prefer to keep that confidential and redirect to why you are interested in the program. You may express genuine interest, but you do not have to make promises. For rules, see [interview season overview](/wiki/interviews-match/interview-season-overview).

## Questions to avoid answering carelessly

- Questions about family plans, religion or other protected personal topics. Programs are generally expected to avoid these. Answer only what you are comfortable sharing and redirect to professional plans.
- Any request to commit to rank the program first. See [how the Match algorithm works](/wiki/interviews-match/how-the-match-algorithm-works).

## Do you have questions for us?

Always have two or three real questions: how residents are supervised, how feedback is given, what recent graduates do, how the program supports IMGs, wellness and call schedules. Avoid questions you can answer from the website.

## Practice plan

1. Write bullet points, not scripts, for each category above.
2. Record yourself answering and watch for rambling, filler words and unclear structure.
3. Do at least two mock interviews with someone who gives specific feedback.
4. For virtual interviews, test your camera, audio, lighting and connection before each one.

> [!IMG TIP] If you can only improve one thing, make your answers shorter and more specific. A concrete two-minute story beats a long general speech.

> [!NOTE] Opinion: confident, honest answers that acknowledge difficulty generally land better than polished perfection. Programs are looking for colleagues who learn.
