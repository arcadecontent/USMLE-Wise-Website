"Tell me about yourself" in a residency interview asks for a short professional summary, not your life story and not your CV. The most reliable way to answer it is a three-part structure: present, past, future. This page gives the framework, a timing guideline (labeled as opinion), three illustrative answers for different IMG backgrounds, the common mistakes and a practice routine.

It sits alongside the wider lists in [common interview questions](/wiki/interviews-match/common-interview-questions) and [interview questions with sample answers](/wiki/interviews-match/interview-questions-with-sample-answers), where this is question number one.

> [!NOTE] Every example answer on this page is an illustrative composite written by the USMLE Wise team. None is a real applicant's answer, and none is a script. Do not copy them. Programs read your ERAS application, and an answer that is not yours is exposed by the first follow-up question.

## What interviewers listen for

The question often comes early, so it shapes the first impression. An interviewer listening to your answer is trying to learn four things:

- **Who you are professionally,** in a way they can remember.
- **Why this specialty,** in one or two real reasons.
- **Whether you communicate clearly,** with a sense of structure and a measured pace.
- **What to ask you next.** A good answer leaves a few interesting threads for follow-up.

AAMC lists "describe yourself" among the general questions you should expect, and advises preparing without memorizing scripts so the conversation stays natural. It also advises being the expert on your own application and adding depth rather than repeating what you wrote.

## How to answer "tell me about yourself" in a residency interview: present, past, future

| Part | What to include | How much of the answer (opinion) |
|---|---|---|
| **Present** | Who you are now: your training, current role or position, and one line that tells them what kind of doctor you are | The shortest part |
| **Past** | Two or three specific experiences that explain why this specialty, chosen for relevance and not completeness | The longest part |
| **Future** | What you want from training, one or two goals, and one real reason this program fits | A medium part |

You can reorder the parts. If your current position is the strongest part of your story, start with it. If you changed specialty, the past often comes first. The order matters less than the clarity of each part.

The test for any detail: does it help the interviewer understand why you want this specialty and why you would be a good resident? If it does not, cut it.

### Timing guideline (opinion)

Opinion, not a rule: about 60 to 90 seconds, which is roughly 150 to 220 words at a conversational pace. Shorter is fine. Longer risks the interviewer losing track.

Prepare three versions:

1. **A 30 second version** for informal moments, such as a first meeting with residents or a short virtual social.
2. **The 60 to 90 second version** for the interview itself.
3. **A version with one program-specific closing sentence** that you adapt for each interview.

## Three illustrative answers for different IMG backgrounds

These three composites show how the same structure fits different stories. The bracketed parts are placeholders for your own true details.

### Example 1: A recent graduate with US clinical experience (internal medicine)

*Background (composite): graduated last year, completed an inpatient observership in the US, one small quality project.*

> "I am a physician from [country]. I graduated from [school] last year, and this year I have been completing an inpatient observership in [city]. I chose internal medicine during my internship at a district hospital, where many of my patients had diabetes or heart failure and kept returning because their plans did not fit their lives. That made me want to learn how to manage complex patients over time. In my observership, I presented patients on rounds and followed discharge planning, and I joined a small project to improve follow-up calls after discharge. I am looking for a program with a strong continuity clinic where I can keep working on that kind of problem, and that is why I am excited about [program]."

**Why it works:**

- It opens with identity and current position (present) in two sentences.
- The past is one concrete experience, not a list.
- The future names a real interest and ends on the program.

### Example 2: Several years since graduation, with clinical work and research (family medicine)

*Background (composite): graduated several years ago, worked in a rural clinic, part of a research group, then passed exams and did US clinical experience.*

> "I am a family physician from [country], and I graduated in [year]. For the past [period], I have worked as a general practitioner in a rural clinic, seeing adults, children and pregnant women, and I have been part of a small research group studying [topic]. I came to family medicine because in a clinic with few specialists to refer to, I learned that a doctor who knows the patient and the family can solve most of what walks through the door. Over the last [period] I completed my USMLE exams and a clinical experience in [city] to prepare for the US system. I want to train in a program with strong outpatient and community training, and I am drawn to [program] because [one real feature]."

**Why it works:**

- It addresses the years since graduation by showing continuous clinical work, without apologizing.
- It places research in one clause, not a paragraph.
- It ends on a concrete interest in the program.

For how programs read time since graduation and how to explain it, see [years since graduation](/wiki/application/years-since-graduation) and [gap years and how to explain them](/wiki/application/gap-years-and-how-to-explain).

### Example 3: Changing specialty (radiology)

*Background (composite): began surgical training at home, now works in a radiology department and is applying to radiology.*

> "I am a physician from [country], and I currently work in a radiology department in [role], where I review [type of studies] with the reporting team and attend clinical meetings with referring teams. I began surgical training at home, and what I discovered there was that the part I liked best was the diagnostic reasoning: looking at the scan before the operation and deciding what it meant. After [period], I moved to radiology full time and have co-authored [one outcome]. I am applying for radiology because I want to be the physician that clinical teams trust to read the image. My surgical years help me understand what a surgeon needs from a report. I am looking for a department with strong teaching and exposure to [area of interest], and [program] offers both."

**Why it works:**

- It reorders the structure: present, then past, then future, with the change of specialty explained in one honest sentence.
- It turns the earlier training into a strength without criticizing it.
- It stays specific without claiming anything that cannot be documented.

### Adapting the answer to your specialty

The structure stays the same, and the emphasis changes with the specialty. Opinion: in internal medicine, past experiences that show interest in complex, long-term patients tend to fit. In family medicine, breadth and continuity. In radiology, an interest in image interpretation and in communicating findings to clinical teams. Describe only what you have actually done. If you are not sure how a specialty works, read its guide first, such as [internal medicine](/wiki/specialties/internal-medicine) or [radiology](/wiki/specialties/radiology).

## Common mistakes

- **Reciting your CV in order.** The interviewer has your CV. Choose the experiences that explain your choice.
- **Starting with childhood.** Include early life only if it directly explains your specialty.
- **Repeating your personal statement word for word.** Add a different detail. See the [personal statement guide](/wiki/application/personal-statement-guide).
- **Running too long.** If the interviewer reaches for the next question, you are over.
- **Memorizing a monologue.** It sounds recited. Memorize the three parts and one detail for each.
- **Ending without a future.** A good answer finishes with what you want from training and why this program.
- **Apologizing in the first minute** for being an IMG, for time since graduation, or for an exam result. Answer those questions honestly when they are asked. They do not belong in the opening.
- **Criticizing your home system or an earlier program.** Describe what you learned.
- **Saying "we" for everything.** Say what you did.
- **Saying anything that does not match ERAS.** Dates, roles and outcomes must agree with your application. NRMP's Match agreement makes you responsible for the accuracy of what you tell programs.
- **Oversharing protected personal topics.** You may share a hobby or a family detail in one sentence if you choose, but you are not required to, and programs are generally expected to avoid asking about protected topics.

## A practice routine

1. **Write three lists,** one each for present, past and future. Use bullet points, not a script.
2. **Choose two or three hooks:** things you hope the interviewer asks about. Keep a good detail ready for each.
3. **Say it aloud and time it.** Trim to about 60 to 90 seconds (opinion).
4. **Record yourself** and listen for filler words, a flat opening or an unclear ending.
5. **Make the three versions:** a 30 second version, the main version and a program-specific closing sentence.
6. **Practice with a person** who asks a follow-up on each hook, such as "Tell me more about that" and "Why did you decide that?"
7. **Test the real setup.** For virtual interviews, check the camera at eye level, the sound and the lighting. See [virtual interview setup](/wiki/interviews-match/virtual-interview-setup).
8. **Check it against ERAS** for dates and details. See the [ERAS experiences section](/wiki/application/eras-experiences-section).
9. **Review it the night before, then stop.** Over-rehearsed answers sound recited.

> [!IMG TIP] If you learned to introduce yourself formally by listing credentials and awards, shift toward one or two short stories. US interviewers usually remember a specific patient or decision better than a list. Say what you did, in the first person, and keep the credit for others where it belongs.
