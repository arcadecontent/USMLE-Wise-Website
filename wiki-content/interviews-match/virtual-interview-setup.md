A virtual residency interview is judged like an on-site one: on preparation, communication and professionalism. The technology should disappear so the interviewer sees you, not your connection. This guide covers the setup, the day itself and what to do when something fails. For what the interview is for and the NRMP rules around it, see [interview season overview](/wiki/interviews-match/interview-season-overview).

## Why this matters

AAMC has encouraged residency programs to use virtual interviews, mainly for cost and access reasons, and its applicant guidance says to treat a virtual interview as an official interview. Some specialties publish their own recommendations: the CORD and APIC best-practice document for the 2026-2027 season still favors virtual interviews for emergency medicine. Other specialties and individual programs differ, and some offer in-person or hybrid options, so read each invitation. This is a practice trend, not a rule, and it can change from one cycle to the next.

For IMGs, virtual interviews remove travel and visitor-visa costs, but they also remove the chance to make an impression in person. A calm, clear video presence is the equivalent.

## Set up your technology

Do this at least a week before your first interview, not the night before.

| Item | What to check |
|---|---|
| Internet | Use a wired connection if you can. Know where your backup is (a phone hotspot). |
| Device | A laptop or desktop with a built-in or external webcam. Avoid joining from a phone. Charge it and plug it in. |
| Platform | Programs use different platforms. Install the app or test the browser version in advance, and update it. |
| Camera | At eye level, so you are not looking up or down. Raise a laptop on books. |
| Audio | Wired earbuds or a headset often sound better than laptop speakers. Test for echo with a friend. |
| Lighting | Light should come from in front of you (a window or lamp behind the camera). A bright window behind you makes you a silhouette. |
| Notifications | Close other apps and silence email, chat and phone alerts. |

If a test link is offered, run a full test call on the exact platform. Record a practice session to see what the interviewer sees.

## Your setting and appearance

- **Background:** a tidy wall or bookshelf. Remove anything distracting. If you share a living space, tell others the interview times and put a note on the door.
- **Frame:** show your head and upper chest, with your face in the upper third of the screen.
- **Dress:** the same professional dress you would wear on-site, top to bottom. Plain colors usually look better on video than busy patterns.
- **Notes:** keep a short page of program facts and your questions near the camera. Do not read answers off the screen, because it shows in your eye movement.

> [!IMG TIP] Looking at the camera lens, not at the face on your screen, reads as eye contact to the interviewer. Put the video window close to the camera so the gap is small, and glance at the lens when you make your key points. This is practice advice, not a rule.

## On the day

1. **Confirm the time in both time zones.** Write the program's time zone and your own next to each interview. See [managing interview invitations](/wiki/interviews-match/managing-interview-invitations).
2. **Join early,** about five to ten minutes, unless the invitation says otherwise. Some programs use a waiting room and admit you at the start time.
3. **Greet everyone you meet.** Staff, residents and coordinators often give feedback to the selection committee. In NRMP's 2026 program director survey, interpersonal skills and resident feedback were among the leading ranking factors, as the overview page explains.
4. **Speak a little slower than normal.** Video adds a small delay. Pause briefly before answering to avoid talking over the interviewer.
5. **Keep answers short.** One to two minutes is usual. See [common interview questions](/wiki/interviews-match/common-interview-questions) and [behavioral interview answers](/wiki/interviews-match/behavioral-interview-answers).
6. **Do not record the session** unless the program says you may. Many programs prohibit recording or sharing interview content.

## If something goes wrong

- **Connection drops:** rejoin immediately. If you cannot, switch to your backup device or hotspot, then email or call the coordinator. Keep their number and email handy before the interview begins.
- **Audio problems:** say so early. A short, polite "I can't hear you clearly, can we check my audio?" is better than guessing.
- **Interruptions:** if something unavoidable happens, apologize briefly and continue.
- **Interviewer technical failure:** wait patiently. It is not a test.

Programs usually understand that failures happen. What they notice is whether you stay calm and communicate.

## Rules and customs

The NRMP rules on interviews apply to virtual interviews too: accept only interviews you intend to keep, give notice when cancelling, and programs cannot ask where else you applied or how you will rank them. Everything else here is custom, not rule:

- Keep water nearby, but do not eat during the interview.
- Keep your phone out of reach.
- Do not multitask on a second screen.
- Thank the interviewer at the end and ask a question or two. See [questions to ask programs](/wiki/interviews-match/questions-to-ask-programs).

## Virtual social events and resident sessions

Many programs hold pre-interview socials or resident question sessions. Whether they are evaluated varies by program, so ask if the invitation does not say. Either way, be courteous and curious. They are also your best source of honest information for your list, as covered in [rank order list strategy](/wiki/interviews-match/rank-order-list-strategy).

## IMG-specific tips

- Check that each interview time works in your time zone and sleep schedule. Programs generally set times in their own time zone, so plan to be alert.
- If your power or internet is unreliable, arrange a quieter location with a stable connection, such as a friend's home or a library room where allowed.
- Practice with someone outside medicine to check that your explanations are clear to a non-specialist, and to get used to speaking at video-call pace.
