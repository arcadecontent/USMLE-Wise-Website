The H-1B is an employment visa for "specialty occupation" workers, and some US residency programs use it for IMGs who have already passed all three USMLE Steps. Your institution, as employer, files the petition; you cannot file it yourself. Fewer programs sponsor H-1B than J-1, so treat it as something to confirm program by program. For a side-by-side comparison, see [J-1 vs H-1B](/wiki/visas/j1-vs-h1b).

> [!CHANGED] November 2026: A September 2025 presidential proclamation required a large payment with certain new H-1B petitions. On June 8, 2026 a federal district court in Massachusetts vacated the agency guidance implementing it (State of California v. Mullin), and on July 24, 2026 the First Circuit denied the government's motion to stay that order. USCIS says DHS will comply with the order while it considers next steps and that, if the order is lifted, it still plans to collect the payment. A proclamation signed on September 18, 2026 extends the restriction for another 12 months. Status can change quickly: see [USCIS](https://www.uscis.gov/newsroom/alerts/h-1b-faq) and the [September 2026 proclamation](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/09/restriction-on-entry-of-certain-nonimmigrant-workers-faad/), and ask your program's international office what it will do for your petition.

## Who sponsors and who decides

The petition is filed by the employer, usually the hospital or the sponsoring institution of the residency. Before it files, the employer also has to obtain the other approvals that go with an H-1B (including a labor condition filing with the Department of Labor). That workload, plus legal cost and institutional policy, is why many programs do not offer it. Programs that do sponsor often limit H-1B to certain candidates or specialties, so read each program's website and ask.

## Requirements in practice

| Requirement | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Medical degree and credentials | Your degree, transcripts and [[ecfmg-certificate]] documents are normally part of the petition. |
| USMLE | In practice, resident H-1Bs require passing Steps 1, 2 CK and 3. See [Step 3](/wiki/exams/step-3) and [Step 3 and H-1B timing](/wiki/visas/step-3-and-h1b-timing). |
| State training license | Many states require a license or permit before the H-1B can start, and states differ on what they require. |
| Employer | The employer must be willing and able to file, and its cap status decides the timeline. |
| English | Demonstrated through your ECFMG certification process. |

These are not fixed numbers on an official card: they combine federal rules, state licensing rules and each employer's own policy. A program can require more than the law does.

## Cap-exempt versus cap-subject employers

Most H-1B petitions are limited by an annual cap, with registration and selection each spring. Certain employers are exempt, including many universities, non-profit entities affiliated with universities and some non-profit research organizations. For residency, that is often the difference between filing at any time and being tied to a once-a-year process that does not match a July start. USCIS describes the categories on its [H-1B specialty occupations page](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/h-1b-specialty-occupations); for the current summary used on this wiki, see {{fact:h1b_cap_exempt_note}}.

> [!NOTE] A new weighted selection system, based on wage level, applied to cap-subject registrations beginning with the fiscal year 2027 cap season (USCIS [cap season page](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/temporary-workers/h-1b-specialty-occupations/h-1b-cap-season)). It concerns cap-subject employers; ask your program whether it is cap-exempt rather than assuming either way.

## The timeline you can plan around

This is a typical sequence, not a guarantee, and every step depends on the employer:

1. **Before applying**: check which programs sponsor H-1B and whether Step 3 is required before the rank list or before the start date.
2. **After the Match**: confirm in writing that the program will file and what documents it needs.
3. **Licensing**: apply for the state training license if required.
4. **Filing**: the employer submits the petition. Ask whether it will use premium processing, which USCIS offers for certain categories at a fee set by USCIS.
5. **If you are abroad**: after approval, you apply for the H-1B visa at a US consulate and enter the US. If you are already in the US in another status, the employer may request a change of status instead.
6. **After arrival**: keep the program informed of your I-94, work authorization and extensions.

Start this planning much earlier than a J-1, because step 1 can fail if you have not passed Step 3 by the time the program needs to file. See [Your visa timeline after the Match](/wiki/visas/visa-timeline-after-the-match).

## What the proclamation payment means in practice

The proclamation applies to certain new H-1B petitions filed for people outside the US who do not hold a valid H-1B visa, and USCIS has said it does not apply to previously issued visas, petitions filed before the effective date, or renewals. The proclamation allows exceptions in the national interest, but its text does not name physicians, and the standard is narrow. Medical organizations have asked DHS for a physician exemption. For an applicant, the realistic position is:

- A residency program may decide not to offer H-1B, or to limit it, while the rules are unsettled.
- The way a program treats an applicant abroad can differ from an applicant already in the US in another status. Ask the program rather than assuming.
- Expect programs to hold different positions at different times. Ask in writing and ask again closer to your start date.

Because this is actively litigated, we do not give an amount or a "safe" answer here. The payment amount and terms are in the proclamation and on USCIS's page.

## Other changes that touch H-1B applicants

- The State Department has extended its online presence review to H-1B and H-4 visa applicants, so expect the same attention to social media identifiers that J-1 applicants face (see the [announcement](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/News/visas-news/announcement-of-expanded-screening-and-vetting-for-h-1b-and-dependent-h-4-visa-applicants.html)).
- Nationals of countries covered by the entry restrictions effective January 1, 2026 should check whether H visas are affected for their nationality. See the [tracker](/wiki/visas/visa-policy-changes-tracker).

## Dual intent and the long game

The H-1B allows "dual intent", meaning you can pursue permanent residence while holding it, and it is not subject to the J-1 two-year home-country requirement. That is the main reason IMGs prefer it. Weigh it against the lower number of programs that offer it. If your plan is to stay in the US after residency, a long-term plan discussed with an attorney is worth more than a visa choice made alone.

## Questions to ask each program

- Do you sponsor H-1B for residents, and for which specialties and PGY levels?
- Is the employer cap-exempt?
- Do you require Step 3 before the petition is filed, and by what date?
- Who pays filing fees and attorney costs, and how are the new proclamation rules handled?
- If H-1B is not possible for me, do you offer a J-1?
