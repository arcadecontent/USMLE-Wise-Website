Conrad 30 is the most common route for J-1 physicians to remove the two-year home-country requirement and stay in the US. A state health department recommends you for a waiver, you work full time for at least three years at a qualifying facility, and you do it in H-1B status. This page explains how that works and what to check, including a status question that every applicant now has to ask. For the rule itself, see [the two-year home residency requirement](/wiki/visas/two-year-home-residency-rule).

That is our reading of the statutes, not an official USCIS statement, and we could not confirm what happened to people admitted between October 1, 2025 and the later extensions. A standalone reauthorization bill (H.R. 1585) was introduced in 2025, and we found no sign it has passed. Check the [USCIS page](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/students-and-exchange-visitors/conrad-30-waiver-program), your state's program page and an attorney, and re-check near December 11, 2026.

## How the program works

Conrad 30 comes from section 214(l) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. A foreign medical graduate on a J-1 who is subject to the two-year rule can get it waived if a state agency (or a federal agency) asks for the waiver to support H-1B employment in a federally designated shortage area. According to the USCIS Policy Manual, the main conditions are:

| Element | What the official sources say |
|---|---|
| Sponsor | A state department of health, or another state or federal agency. Each state may request up to 30 waivers per fiscal year. |
| Work | Full-time clinical work, which the State Department treats as about 40 hours a week on average. |
| Length | At least three years. |
| Site | A facility in or serving a Health Professional Shortage Area, Medically Underserved Area or Medically Underserved Population designated by HHS. |
| Status | H-1B, under a bona fide full-time employment contract. |
| Start | The physician agrees to begin work within a set period after the waiver is granted. |

The Policy Manual also describes "flex" slots: a state may use a limited number of its waivers for physicians who work outside a designated area if they serve patients who live in one. How a state uses flex slots, and which of them it allows, is its own decision.

## Step by step

1. **Finish or near the end of training on the J-1.** Waiver timing is tied to your DS-2019 dates and to each state's cycle, so start months ahead.
2. **Find an employer in a state that participates.** The employer must offer a full-time contract at an eligible facility. Many employers advertise Conrad positions.
3. **Apply to the state.** Each state publishes its own process. Some open an application window once a year and use a lottery or ranking when demand is high. For example, one state's public page lists a short application window in early October and a lottery date in November for its fiscal year, which shows how tight timing can be. That same page says it keeps accepting applications after the window if its slots are not filled, but do not assume every state does.
4. **State recommendation to the State Department.** The state sends its recommendation, and the State Department's Waiver Review Division reviews it.
5. **Employer files the H-1B.** The petitioner files Form I-129 with the favorable State Department recommendation to change you from J-1 to H-1B. Family members on J-2 file to change to H-4.
6. **Serve the contract.** After the three years you can move toward permanent residence. See [green card paths for physicians](/wiki/visas/green-card-for-physicians).

> [!NOTE] The H-1B in Conrad 30 is usually filed by a hospital or clinic that is often cap-exempt or that has to deal with the annual cap. Ask the employer which applies. See [H-1B cap-exempt employers](/wiki/visas/h1b-cap-exempt-employers).

## What varies by state

State programs differ more than most applicants expect:

- Which specialties are welcome. Many states favor primary care, psychiatry or other specialties in short supply, and some reserve a few slots for others.
- Whether the state limits facility types, requires the employer to show recruitment efforts or restricts how the contract is written.
- How and when applications are accepted, how the state ranks competing applications and what it charges, if anything.
- Whether employers must cover the cost, and what happens if a job falls through.

Do not use a ranking article or a forum post from another year. Open the health department page for each state you might work in.

## Risks to plan around

- **The sunset.** If the authority lapses at a date when you have not yet been recommended, you may lose the option or have to wait for Congress. Have a second plan, such as another waiver route in [J-1 waiver options](/wiki/visas/j1-waiver-options), or a different visa.
- **Leaving the contract.** USCIS says a physician who does not complete the three-year commitment can become subject to the home-country requirement again, with limited exceptions for extenuating circumstances. Read the contract for what happens if the facility closes, changes the job or lets you go.
- **Your own timing.** Most states have deadlines that sit within your final residency or fellowship year. Missing one can cost a year.
- **Policy changes.** H-1B rules, including the 2025 and 2026 payment proclamation, may affect the employer's filing. See the [visa policy changes tracker](/wiki/visas/visa-policy-changes-tracker).

> [!WARNING] A physician's waiver is a contract with legal consequences. Have an immigration attorney review the employment contract before you sign, and do not let an employer or recruiter be your only source of advice.

## Questions to ask in residency

- Does our program have a history of residents who got Conrad 30 waivers, and in which states?
- Which states in my target region still have unused slots by the time most residents apply?
- Is my specialty one the state prioritizes?
- If the program ends before the sunset date, do I still have a path?

> [!IMG TIP] Ask about Conrad 30 while you are still choosing programs, not in your final year. A program with graduates in shortage-area jobs can tell you which states and employers worked in practice. Treat that as experience, not a promise.
