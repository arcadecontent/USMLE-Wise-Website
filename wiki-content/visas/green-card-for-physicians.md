Physicians usually get a green card through an employment-based category: EB-1, EB-2 or EB-3. The category you can use depends on your job, your credentials and, if you were on a J-1, whether your two-year home-country requirement has been waived. How long it takes depends mostly on how many visas are available for your country of birth. This page is an overview of those routes, not a plan for your case, because green card strategy is where an immigration attorney is most useful.

> [!CHANGED] November 2026: Immigration processing rules have shifted repeatedly since 2025, and some of them affect immigrant visas for nationals of certain countries. See the [visa policy changes tracker](/wiki/visas/visa-policy-changes-tracker), and check the current Visa Bulletin and USCIS pages before relying on any timeline.

## The basic sequence

1. **A category.** You or your employer files a petition (Form I-140) in a category such as EB-1, EB-2 or EB-3.
2. **A priority date.** According to USCIS, for employment-based cases the date is when the Department of Labor accepts the labor certification, or, when none is required, when USCIS accepts the I-140.
3. **A visa number.** If visas are available for your category and country, you can move to the final step. If not, you wait.
4. **The final step.** Either adjustment of status inside the US (Form I-485) or consular processing abroad.

Family members can generally be included as derivatives when the principal's case is approved.

## The main categories for physicians

| Category | Who it fits | Job offer and labor certification |
|---|---|---|
| EB-1 | People with extraordinary ability, outstanding professors or researchers, and some multinational managers | Extraordinary ability can self-petition without a job offer. Outstanding researchers need an employer and no labor certification. |
| EB-2 | Members of the professions with an advanced degree, or people with exceptional ability | Usually requires a labor certification, unless a national interest waiver applies. |
| EB-3 | Professionals with a bachelor's degree, skilled workers and others | Usually requires a labor certification and a permanent full-time job offer. |

A physician has an advanced degree in the usual sense, so EB-2 is a common home. EB-3 is also possible, and which one is faster can differ by country because each category has its own backlog. These are general descriptions from USCIS pages. Which category you can use is a legal question.

### The labor certification step

For most EB-2 and EB-3 cases the employer first obtains a labor certification from the Department of Labor. As USCIS describes it, for filings from June 1, 2023 that is done through DOL's FLAG system. Schedule A occupations skip it, but USCIS lists nurses and physical therapists there, not physicians. That is why a physician usually follows either the labor certification route or a waiver of it.

## The physician national interest waiver

Under section 203(b)(2)(B)(ii) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, certain physicians can have the job offer, and so the labor certification, waived. The USCIS Policy Manual describes the main conditions:

- The physician agrees to work full time in a clinical practice, primary or specialty care, in an underserved area for an aggregate of five years.
- The area is one designated by the Secretary of Health and Human Services, such as a Health Professional Shortage Area, Medically Underserved Area or Medically Underserved Population.
- The five years exclude time in J-1 status.
- A federal agency or a state health department with jurisdiction must determine that the work is in the public interest.
- Service before the petition can count if the area was a designated shortage area when the work began, and the physician cannot receive permanent resident status until the five years are complete.

Specialists qualify, and physicians at VA facilities are not bound by the HHS area categories. The related national interest waiver for other advanced-degree professionals is a separate discretionary standard that USCIS describes in three parts: substantial merit and national importance, being well positioned to advance the endeavor, and the benefit of waiving the job offer.

## How a J-1 waiver fits in

If you were subject to the two-year rule, you must satisfy it or have it waived before you can get a green card. The Policy Manual says a physician who needs a waiver must still obtain it and meet its conditions, including three years of service, before USCIS can approve adjustment. In practice, a physician may do a Conrad 30 or other waiver job in H-1B status and then apply under the physician national interest waiver or another category. Whether service years under one route count toward another is a question for your attorney. See [J-1 waiver options](/wiki/visas/j1-waiver-options) and [Conrad 30](/wiki/visas/conrad-30-waiver).

An H-1B physician who is not subject to the two-year rule can also start a green card process through the employer while working.

## Why country of birth matters

The Department of State's Visa Bulletin publishes cut-off dates by category and by country of chargeability, which is usually your country of birth. Each country's share is limited, so an oversubscribed country can have its own cut-off date. USCIS says you wait when your priority date is later than the cut-off for your category and country, and that cut-off dates can move backward. Two physicians with identical credentials can have very different waits. The November 2026 Visa Bulletin is the current reference at the time of writing, but we do not copy its dates because they change monthly.

> [!NOTE] Many physicians are in categories and countries where waits are long. Anyone planning around a green card should have an attorney who can read the Visa Bulletin for their country, and should not rely on a friend's timeline from a different country or year.

## What depends on your country and what does not

- **Depends on your country:** the priority date cut-off, the length of the wait, and sometimes whether the Department of State is currently issuing visas to nationals of your country.
- **Does not depend on your country:** the categories themselves, the physician national interest waiver rules, and the two-year rule.

## Planning questions

- Am I subject to the two-year rule, and which waiver route am I using?
- Which category fits my credentials and employer?
- What is the current Visa Bulletin position for my country of birth in that category?
- Is my employer willing to sponsor, and when does it file?
- Does a five-year underserved-area commitment fit my career plan?

> [!WARNING] Do not assume that signing a waiver job or an employer promise guarantees a green card. Each step is separate, and rules and visa numbers change.
