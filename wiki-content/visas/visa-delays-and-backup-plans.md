If your visa will not be ready by your start date, the most important step is to tell your program and its graduate medical education (GME) office early and in writing. They control your position, your contract dates and the paperwork that depends on them. Whether a late start is possible depends on the program and, for a J-1, on rules set by the sponsor. This page explains what typically causes delays and what a realistic backup plan looks like. It is not legal advice, and we cannot promise any outcome.

> [!CHANGED] November 2026: Several rules affect timing, including narrower interview waivers, online presence review, entry restrictions for some nationalities and litigation over the H-1B payment and "duration of status." The [visa policy changes tracker](/wiki/visas/visa-policy-changes-tracker) logs what we verified. Do not assume last year's timeline applies to you.

## What commonly delays a start

The normal sequence is in [your visa timeline after the Match](/wiki/visas/visa-timeline-after-the-match). These are the points where it most often slips.

| Pathway | Typical delay points |
|---|---|
| J-1 | Statement of Need from your home ministry; errors or name mismatches on forms; DS-2019 delivery across borders; appointment availability; additional screening after the interview |
| H-1B | Step 3 result timing; state license processing; the employer's decision about whether and when to file; policy changes, including the payment requirement for some new petitions; visa appointment after approval |
| Both | Passport validity; document verification; entry restrictions for certain nationalities; program or GME office workload |

Two things are outside your control and worth knowing: your program may decide not to file an H-1B at all under current rules (see [H-1B for residents](/wiki/visas/h1b-for-residents)), and consular appointment availability varies by country and by season. In June 2025, NRMP noted that the State Department had paused and then resumed scheduling for some applicants, and that it was prioritizing J-1 physicians for interview scheduling.

## What the rules say about start dates

### J-1

ECFMG's materials state several rules that matter for planning:

- A newly sponsored J-1 physician must enter and begin the program within 30 days of acquiring J-1 visa status. If this does not happen, ECFMG's information sheet says the SEVIS record can be deactivated and you may have to reapply for sponsorship (confirm the current wording on ECFMG's site).
- ECFMG's pre-arrival guidance says a physician may enter no earlier than 30 days before the program start on the DS-2019, and may not participate in training during that period.
- Once ECFMG validates your arrival in SEVIS, the start date cannot be changed. If the start date was delayed and contract dates were amended, ECFMG's validation form asks for the updated contract or offer letter.
- The DS-2019 dates drive the rest. A start-date change generally means the program and ECFMG have to align the paperwork, so raise it before you travel.

### H-1B

The H-1B petition states the requested start date and the validity period. USCIS notes for cap-subject cases that the petition must request a start date of October 1 or later and that a petition cannot be filed more than six months before the requested start date. Cap-exempt employers can file on a different schedule, which is why [cap-exempt employers](/wiki/visas/h1b-cap-exempt-employers) matter for residency. If your start date moves, the employer must tell you what that does to the petition and your entry date.

### The Match commitment

The NRMP's published position is that applicants who cannot honor a Match commitment must request a waiver, and that applicants must meet ACGME and institutional requirements before training starts. For visa-affected applicants in June 2025, NRMP asked programs to consider a delayed start or a one-year deferral rather than a waiver, while saying that programs may request waivers if a matched applicant cannot begin on time. That statement was tied to the 2025 situation, so ask the program what it will do now. Do not rely on an old forum post.

## Who to tell and when

1. **Tell the program coordinator and the GME office as soon as you see a risk**, not when the delay is certain. Put dates in the message: what you submitted, when, and what you are waiting for.
2. **Ask about their written policy** on late arrival, deferral and leave. Ask who decides.
3. **Keep ECFMG or your sponsor informed** if you are a J-1 candidate. Dates, program and specialty changes must be reflected in the sponsored paperwork.
4. **Ask your international office** whether any step can be expedited and whether your employer can adjust the filing plan.
5. **Keep records**: copies of receipts, emails, appointment confirmations and policy pages you relied on.

> [!IMG TIP] Ask each program, before ranking or soon after matching, what happens if your visa is late. A program that has handled late starts before can often describe its options clearly. That answer is useful information, but it is not a guarantee.

## What a realistic contingency plan looks like

We cannot tell you which of these will be available. They are options that programs and sponsors sometimes use, listed from least to most disruptive.

- **A short delay.** The program holds your position and amends your start date, if your sponsor's paperwork allows.
- **A longer delay or a deferral.** The program keeps your position for a later start, or a full year, as NRMP's 2025 statement suggested. The program must agree, and it may affect your contract and your visa timing.
- **A waiver of the Match commitment.** NRMP says an applicant who cannot honor the commitment must request one. Breaking a commitment without a waiver has consequences under NRMP's rules, so read them and ask the program before you act. See [if you do not match](/wiki/interviews-match/if-you-do-not-match) for related options.
- **A different visa path.** For example, a program that offers both J-1 and H-1B may switch, if time and eligibility allow. This is a significant decision with long-term consequences, so discuss it with the international office and an attorney first.
- **Release from the position.** In the worst case, the program fills the position. Have a plan for the year, including how you will keep your exams, USCE and application current for the next cycle. See [reapplicant strategy](/wiki/application/reapplicant-strategy).

## Preparation that helps

- Start the Statement of Need and any ministry requests as early as the instructions allow.
- Check your passport validity and scan every document.
- Know your nationality's current restrictions on the tracker, and avoid non-refundable travel until you have a visa in hand.
- Plan finances for a delayed first paycheck. Rent, flights and fees often come before your first pay.
- Keep your family's travel separate from yours until your own timing is clear. See [J-2 and H-4 dependents](/wiki/visas/dependents-j2-and-h4).

## What not to do

- Do not enter the US to "wait" in a status that does not allow training or work, and do not start work without valid status.
- Do not stop communicating with the program. Silence is the most common way a recoverable delay turns into a lost position.
- Do not rely on advice from social media about what a program "has to" do. Ask the program and the official bodies.
