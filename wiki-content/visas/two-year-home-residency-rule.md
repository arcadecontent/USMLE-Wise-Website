Most IMG physicians on an ECFMG-sponsored J-1 are subject to the two-year home-country physical presence requirement in section 212(e) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. In plain terms, unless you obtain a waiver, you cannot move straight from the J-1 to certain other statuses. It does not affect your ability to train on the J-1. It decides what you can do afterward, so understand it before you accept a J-1 offer. For the visa decision itself, see [J-1 vs H-1B](/wiki/visas/j1-vs-h1b).

> [!CHANGED] October 2025: USCIS posted that, unless Congress extends the Conrad 30 provision, J-1 physicians admitted or acquiring J-1 status on or after October 1, 2025 would not be eligible for Conrad 30. We could not verify from official sources whether Congress has since extended it. Check the [USCIS Conrad 30 page](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/students-and-exchange-visitors/conrad-30-waiver-program), your state health department and an immigration attorney before relying on Conrad 30 for any plan.

## What the requirement is

Section 212(e) applies to certain J-1 exchange visitors, including those whose training was funded by their home government or the US government, those whose field appears on the Exchange Visitor Skills List, and all physicians who came to the US to receive graduate medical education or training. The State Department's waiver FAQs explain who is subject. The Skills List was updated in 2024, but a person can still be subject to the requirement because of graduate medical training, so do not assume a country or field exempts you.

Your DS-2019 and visa usually show whether you are subject. If you are unsure, ask [[ecfmg]] or the program's international office, and confirm with an attorney.

## What it blocks

Until you fulfil it or obtain a waiver, the requirement generally blocks:

- Changing status inside the US to most other categories (for example from J-1 to H-1B).
- Obtaining an H, L or K visa, or permanent residence.

It does not block you from visiting the US, and some other categories may remain open. Rules differ by category, so get advice before you plan around a specific route. See [H-1B for residents](/wiki/visas/h1b-for-residents) for the usual alternative.

## Fulfilling it by going home

You can satisfy the requirement by physically living in your home country (the country of nationality or last legal permanent residence) for two years in total, in the right periods, after your J-1 program. Brief visits abroad generally do not count as fulfilling it. Many physicians prefer a waiver because returning home can be costly or impractical, but it is the plain legal baseline.

## The five waiver bases

The USCIS Policy Manual lists these waiver bases. Which of them is open to you depends on your facts.

| Basis | In brief | Who handles it |
|---|---|---|
| Exceptional hardship | The requirement would cause exceptional hardship to a US citizen or permanent resident spouse or child | Form I-612 with USCIS, then the State Department's Waiver Review Division |
| Persecution | You fear persecution in your home country on specified grounds | Form I-612 with USCIS, then the State Department |
| No objection | Your home government states it has no objection | State Department; not available to physicians who came for graduate medical education |
| Interested government agency | A US federal agency asks for the waiver on public-interest grounds | The agency, with the State Department |
| State or federal physician programs | In exchange for service in an underserved area, such as Conrad 30 | A state health department or federal program, with the State Department |

The State Department's Waiver Review Division makes the recommendation on most waivers, and the I-612 instructions say not to file that form for interested agency, no objection or Conrad requests. Always use the current instructions on the State Department and USCIS pages.

## Conrad 30 in outline

Conrad 30 lets a state health department sponsor a limited number of J-1 physicians per year for a waiver. According to USCIS, the physician must agree to a full-time clinical contract in H-1B status at a facility in or serving a designated shortage area, for a minimum period set out on the USCIS page, and must obtain sponsorship from the state. Each state sets its own priorities and sponsors a limited number of physicians, so competition and rules differ by state and year.

Because the program has needed periodic renewal by Congress and its authorization status has been uncertain, treat it as one possible route and not a guaranteed one. See the [Conrad 30 guide](/wiki/visas/conrad-30-waiver) and [J-1 waiver options](/wiki/visas/j1-waiver-options) for the routes in detail, and use the USCIS page above and your state health department's own instructions for current rules.

> [!WARNING] Do not assume a waiver will be available when you need it. Eligibility, service commitments, location, specialty and timing all matter, and some J-1 physicians find the options narrower than they expected. Ask programs whether residents have successfully obtained waivers, and have a backup plan.

## Planning questions

- Does the program have a track record with waivers, and does it support them?
- Does the state where you want to practise have a Conrad 30 program, and what does it prioritise?
- Do you have family circumstances that might support a hardship basis? These are demanding standards, not a typical route.
- Would an H-1B-sponsoring program change your plan, given your exam timeline? See [Step 3 and H-1B timing](/wiki/visas/step-3-and-h1b-timing).
- Do you want to stay long term? If yes, talk to an immigration attorney before you sign for a J-1.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the requirement as optional, or assuming it can be waived at the last minute.
- Planning a status change to H-1B or a green card before checking whether you are subject.
- Relying on forum posts about a waiver that applied to someone in a different year, category or country.
- Missing that waiver programs have their own deadlines relative to your training end date.
