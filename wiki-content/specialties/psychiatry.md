Psychiatry is a specialty where IMGs match, but it is neither the largest nor the most open destination. In NRMP's 2026 Main Residency Match, non-U.S. citizen IMGs filled 8.8 percent of the 2,516 Psychiatry positions, compared with 30.8 percent of Internal Medicine categorical positions. The specialty also filled almost completely, which leaves little room for last-minute openings.

## The 2026 numbers

| Measure | 2026 figure (NRMP) |
|---|---|
| Psychiatry positions offered | 2,516 |
| Share filled by non-U.S. citizen IMGs | 8.8% |
| Share filled by U.S. citizen IMGs | 7.0% |
| Overall fill rate in the algorithm | 97.4% |
| Applicants who ranked Psychiatry first (all applicants, per NRMP) | 2,873 for 2,516 positions (about 1.1 per position) |

NRMP also reports that 566 U.S. DO seniors filled Psychiatry positions in 2026, 179 (46.3 percent) more than in 2022. More U.S. graduates entering the specialty means more competition for IMGs.

> [!KEY] Because Psychiatry filled 97.4 percent of positions in the algorithm, SOAP is a weak safety net. Do not plan to rely on it for Psychiatry.

## What programs commonly look for

- **Exams.** In NRMP's 2026 Program Director Survey, 92 percent of programs required IMGs to have taken Step 1 and 84 percent required Step 2 CK for interview consideration. Among programs requiring Step 2 CK from IMGs, 32 percent required a target score.
- **Psychiatry exposure.** Clinical rotations, observerships or externships in psychiatry, research, or relevant work in mental health. A letter from a psychiatrist is often valuable.
- **Communication and insight.** Interviews in psychiatry usually probe self-awareness, empathy, handling of difficult patient interactions and professional boundaries. NRMP's survey found interpersonal skills and interview interactions to be leading ranking factors across specialties.
- **Fluency and cultural competence.** Language and cultural background can be a strength, but spoken English and the ability to build rapport are central.
- **Commitment.** A clear, specific explanation of why psychiatry, not a vague interest in "helping people".

## How IMGs commonly strengthen a Psychiatry application

1. **Get psychiatry-specific US clinical experience** and a detailed letter. See [how to get US clinical experience](/wiki/clinical-experience/how-to-get-usce).
2. **Add psychiatry or neuroscience research** where possible, e.g. a poster or paper. See [how programs view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research).
3. **Prepare for conversation.** Practice answering questions about empathy, boundaries, stigma and your own resilience. See [common interview questions](/wiki/interviews-match/common-interview-questions).
4. **Apply broadly.** NRMP's 2026 report shows matched non-U.S. citizen IMGs had a median of 5 contiguous ranks in their preferred specialty versus 2 for unmatched.
5. **Score as high as you can.** Across specialties, matched non-U.S. citizen IMGs had a median Step 2 CK of 248 versus 242.

## Visa and exam realities

- Visa sponsorship differs by program. Read [J-1 vs H-1B](/wiki/visas/j1-vs-h1b) and confirm before applying.
- Licensure, state requirements and ECFMG certification matter for starting training ([ECFMG certification](https://www.ecfmg.org/certification/)).
- Exam attempts and years since graduation are common screening filters ([program screening filters](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters)).

## Red flags

- Multiple USMLE attempts, long gaps without clinical activity, or professionalism concerns.
- A personal statement that discloses sensitive patient information or presents psychiatry as a fallback.
- Weak English communication in the interview.
- Letters that do not speak to your clinical skills.

## How the interview and ranking stage tends to differ

Psychiatry interviews are often conversational and lean on narrative rather than quick-fire questions. Interviewers may ask about a patient who changed your thinking, how you handle emotional intensity, how you look after your own wellbeing, and how you would respond when a patient disagrees with you or declines treatment. Preparation means reflecting on real cases (without identifying patients) and practicing concise, warm, honest answers. The [common interview questions](/wiki/interviews-match/common-interview-questions) guide covers structure, and the [interview season overview](/wiki/interviews-match/interview-season-overview) covers NRMP conduct rules.

## A realistic way to decide

1. Check your profile against what programs screen for: Step 2 CK result, attempts, years since graduation and visa needs.
2. Count how much psychiatry-specific evidence you already have: rotations, letters, research, volunteering, language skills.
3. Estimate how many programs you can apply to that sponsor your visa type, then compare with the broad-application pattern in the NRMP data above.
4. If the gap between your profile and the specialty's expectations is large, decide whether to close it before applying (another cycle with psychiatry experience) or to widen your plan to a second specialty.

> [!NOTE] Opinion: psychiatry rewards applicants who can show sustained, specific interest. A single short observership rarely tells that story on its own; a pattern of experiences does.

## Backup planning

- **A second specialty.** [Internal Medicine](/wiki/specialties/internal-medicine) or [Family Medicine](/wiki/specialties/family-medicine) are common second choices, but you must present a believable story for each.
- **Preliminary or transitional year.** A one-year position can keep you in the US system but does not guarantee a psychiatry spot later.
- **Reapply with a stronger profile** ([reapplicant strategy](/wiki/application/reapplicant-strategy)).

> [!WARNING] Do not tailor your story so heavily that it sounds insincere. Programs read many applications, and mismatches between your letters, experiences and statement are easy to spot.
