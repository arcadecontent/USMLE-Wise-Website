The best way to choose a specialty is to answer three questions in order: do you want the work, can your profile compete for it, and can you actually train there on your visa. Most IMGs start with the second question and ignore the first, which is a mistake because you will spend at least three years doing the work. Use the framework below, and check every claim against the latest [NRMP data](/wiki/specialties/img-match-rates-by-specialty).

> [!NOTE] For the 2027 Match, NRMP says Emergency Medicine and Obstetrics and Gynecology programs use ResidencyCAS rather than ERAS, and all other specialties use ERAS. If you are applying to either of those two specialties, the ERAS steps, fees and signaling described on this page may not apply to them. Read the [Emergency Medicine](/wiki/specialties/emergency-medicine) and [OB-GYN](/wiki/specialties/obstetrics-and-gynecology) guides, and confirm the current system with NRMP.

## Step 1: Fit, before competitiveness

Write down what you actually enjoy day to day, not what sounds prestigious.

- Do you prefer long continuity relationships (Family Medicine, Internal Medicine primary care, Psychiatry) or short episodes (acute care, procedures)?
- Do you like diagnostic puzzles, procedures, children, talking with people about mental health, or population-level care?
- How do you feel about nights, call, and the pace of inpatient work?
- What kind of practice do you want after training (hospital, clinic, subspecialty fellowship, academia)?

> [!NOTE] Opinion: a specialty you tolerate is hard to sustain through residency and the exams that follow. Fit protects you in the long run in a way a slightly higher match chance does not.

## Step 2: Honest competitiveness

For IMGs, competitiveness has two parts: how many seats exist for people like you, and how your profile compares with what programs screen for.

### Seats and applicants

NRMP's 2026 data shows that IMG positions are concentrated. In the 2026 Main Residency Match, 52.8 percent of matched non-U.S. citizen IMGs obtained positions in Internal Medicine, and 64.1 percent of matched U.S. citizen IMGs obtained positions in Internal Medicine, Family Medicine or Pediatrics. Specialties with very few IMG matches (for example Orthopaedic Surgery and Otolaryngology, where NRMP reports very few non-U.S. citizen IMGs per position) are not closed to IMGs in principle, but the historical numbers are small, so the practical risk is high.

### What programs screen for

In NRMP's 2026 Program Director Survey, 92 percent of responding programs required IMG applicants to have taken USMLE Step 1 and 84 percent required Step 2 CK for interview consideration, regardless of result. Among programs that required Step 2 CK for IMGs, 32 percent required a target score. Program directors named Step 2 CK score (89 percent), Step 1 pass (87 percent) and specialty-specific letters of recommendation (86 percent) among the key considerations for choosing interviewees.

Run your own numbers: your Step 2 CK score, attempts, how long ago you graduated, US clinical experience, letters and visa needs. The [program screening filters](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters) guide shows how programs apply these.

### Reading the match data without over-reading it

- A high share of IMGs in a specialty means programs there have a history of ranking IMGs, not that your chance is high.
- NRMP's 2026 Charting Outcomes report notes that, across specialties, non-U.S. citizen IMGs who matched had higher median Step 2 CK scores (248 vs 242) and more contiguous ranks (median 5 vs 2). Both of these are more achievable in some specialties than others.

## Step 3: Visa and logistics

Many programs do not sponsor visas, and those that do may sponsor only a J-1 or only an H-1B. This changes your list more than any other factor if you need sponsorship. Before you commit to a specialty, check how many programs in it accept your visa type, and read [J-1 vs H-1B](/wiki/visas/j1-vs-h1b). Specialty also affects the likelihood that a program wants Step 3 early, which matters for H-1B timelines.

## Step 4: Build the backup, honestly

A backup plan is not a promise of a match. It is a deliberate decision about what you will do if the primary plan fails.

- **A second specialty.** Applying to two specialties is allowed but means separate letters and personal statements. Only do it if you can build a credible application for both.
- **Preliminary or transitional year.** These are one-year positions and are not a pathway to a categorical position by default. Read the [[categorical]] and [[preliminary-year]] definitions, and check each program's own page for what happens after the first year.
- **A reapplication year.** Use the time to add US clinical experience, research and a stronger Step 2 CK if possible (see [reapplicant strategy](/wiki/application/reapplicant-strategy)).

## A decision table

| Question | If the answer is yes | If the answer is no |
|---|---|---|
| Do I like the day-to-day work? | Continue to the next question | Drop it, even if it looks easier |
| Does my profile fit what programs screen for? | Apply broadly in this specialty | Fix the gap first, or reconsider |
| Do enough programs sponsor my visa type? | Build your list | Reconsider specialty or visa plan |
| Do I have a documented backup? | Proceed | Write one before applications open |

## Common mistakes

- Choosing by rumor ("everyone says X is easy for IMGs") instead of data.
- Choosing in the last few months, after letters and experiences are already set.
- Ignoring visa realities until after submitting applications.
- Applying to too few programs in a specialty, which the contiguous-rank data suggests can reduce odds.
- Treating a one-year preliminary position as a guaranteed way into another specialty.
