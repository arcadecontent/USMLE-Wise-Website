A good backup specialty is one you would accept, can support with real evidence, and can apply to without starving your main application of time and letters. Plan it before you collect letters, not in the week before ERAS opens. This page covers how to choose it, how to rank it, and the situations where a backup does more harm than good.

## Why a backup is part of planning

NRMP's 2026 figures show how much risk IMGs carry. The PGY-1 match rate among active applicants was 56.4 percent for non-U.S. citizen IMGs and 70.0 percent for U.S. citizen IMGs, against 93.5 percent for U.S. MD seniors ([IMG match rates by specialty](/wiki/specialties/img-match-rates-by-specialty)). Specialties differ widely. For example, non-U.S. citizen IMGs filled 30.8 percent of categorical Internal Medicine positions but 2.7 percent of OB-GYN positions and 2.9 percent of Emergency Medicine positions in 2026. If your first choice is one of the narrower specialties, a backup is a normal part of the plan.

CORD's 2026 guide for IMGs applying to Emergency Medicine tells realistic applicants to apply broadly in EM and also to other specialties, and not to count on scrambling into the specialty later through SOAP ([Emergency Medicine for IMGs](/wiki/specialties/emergency-medicine)).

## The rule that protects your main plan

Choose the backup using the same three questions as the main specialty (see [choosing a specialty](/wiki/specialties/choosing-a-specialty)): would you accept the work, can your profile support it, and can you train there on your visa.

1. **You would accept the work.** NRMP's rules make a match binding: its IMG newsletter notes you must obtain a waiver to decline a matched position and should only rank programs you are willing to attend. A backup you would resent is not a backup.
2. **Your profile is credible in it.** You need rotations, letters, research or at least coherent experience pointing to that specialty. A backup with no evidence does not deliver the benefit.
3. **Your visa is workable.** Check sponsorship for the backup specialty separately, because policy varies by program ([J-1 vs H-1B](/wiki/visas/j1-vs-h1b)).

> [!NOTE] Opinion: Internal Medicine is the most common backup because of its size and IMG share, but it is also where the most IMGs apply. A backup in a smaller specialty where you have real evidence can be better than a generic Internal Medicine application.

## How a backup can hurt the main plan

- **Split attention.** A second application means a second personal statement, second set of letters and second program list. Weak execution of both is worse than a strong single application.
- **Mixed messages.** Programs see your experiences. A pathology application whose entire experience section points to surgery looks unfocused.
- **Letters.** Letters should come from physicians in the specialty. If you must split your few letter writers, check that each specialty gets a specific one. See [letters of recommendation](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation).
- **Signals.** AAMC sets the number of signals by specialty, so a dual applicant has separate signal allotments to plan ([program signaling explained](/wiki/application/program-signaling-explained)).
- **Two systems.** EM and OB-GYN use ResidencyCAS while other specialties use ERAS, and the NRMP newsletter says you register with both the NRMP and the application service your programs use. A dual application between them means separate fees and deadlines ([ERAS and NRMP costs](/wiki/application/eras-and-match-costs)).
- **Interview schedule.** Interviews for two specialties can collide, and you cannot be everywhere.

## How to rank a backup

The rank order list is yours to order. NRMP calls its algorithm applicant proposing, so the algorithm tries your first-ranked program first and moves down only if you are not matched there. Ranking a backup program below all your first-choice programs therefore does not reduce your chance of matching to your first choice. See [how the match algorithm works](/wiki/interviews-match/how-the-match-algorithm-works) and [rank order list strategy](/wiki/interviews-match/rank-order-list-strategy).

The Charting Outcomes report measures contiguous ranks: the number of programs ranked in your first-choice specialty before a program in another specialty appears. In 2026 the median for matched non-U.S. citizen IMGs was 5, against 2 for unmatched (consenting applicants, all specialties). Matched and unmatched applicants both had a median of one distinct specialty ranked. So the data supports depth in a primary specialty more than breadth across many. Put the backup after your serious first-choice programs, not interleaved, unless you truly prefer a backup program to a first-choice one.

## When not to apply to a backup

- You have no realistic way to build credible evidence in the backup.
- You would not accept the backup if matched.
- Adding it would cut the number of programs you can apply to or interview with in your first-choice specialty.
- The backup depends on a visa that its programs rarely sponsor.

## Other kinds of backup

- **Preliminary or transitional year.** It can keep you in the US system and may be a bridge, but it does not secure the specialty you wanted ([categorical vs preliminary](/wiki/specialties/categorical-vs-preliminary)).
- **SOAP.** It is not a plan. See [SOAP explained](/wiki/interviews-match/soap-explained).
- **Reapplying with a stronger profile.** See [reapplicant strategy](/wiki/application/reapplicant-strategy) and [if you do not match](/wiki/interviews-match/if-you-do-not-match).

> [!IMG TIP] Decide your backup and its trigger before the season: for example, "if I have fewer than a certain number of first-choice interviews by a set date, I will widen my list." Deciding in advance prevents panic choices in January.
