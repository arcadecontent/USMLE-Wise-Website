Fellowship is a path to subspecialty training after residency, and the application mechanics depend on the specialty: some use ERAS, some use their own portals, and the match may run through NRMP's Specialties Matching Service, SF Match or another service. For an IMG, the extra questions are about visa continuity and how long you can stay in training. This page explains the system and the visa issues. It is general information, not legal advice.

## How fellowship applications work

### The application service

AAMC runs ERAS for fellowships, and its participating specialties and programs list shows which fellowships use it. The list covers many subspecialties of internal medicine, pediatrics, surgery, neurology, obstetrics and gynecology, pathology and radiology, among others, and it changes by season. Some fellowships point applicants to their own society's application. NRMP's fellowship page says the same: some programs use their own application, others use ERAS or FellowshipsCAS, and applicants must register separately with NRMP and with any application service a program requires.

### The match

- **NRMP Specialties Matching Service.** NRMP says its Specialties Matching Service runs multiple fellowship matches covering "almost 90 subspecialties," where applicants rank programs and results are released on a Match Day. In its 2026 results release, NRMP reported 81 subspecialties in 23 separate matches, 15,846 active applicants, and 12,963 matched applicants, including 2,451 non-US citizen graduates of international medical schools. NRMP gave matched counts by group but not group match rates, so these numbers do not tell you your odds.
- **SF Match.** SF Match runs fellowship matches for other specialties, including ophthalmology and several orthopaedic and surgical subspecialties. Its match calendar is the source for the current list.
- **No match at all.** Some fellowships fill through direct offers or program-specific rules.

Use the NRMP participating fellowships page and the SF Match calendar to confirm how your target fellowship works. Rules and participation change each year.

### Timing

AAMC's published timeline for fellowship applicants shows two ERAS cycles:

| Cycle | What AAMC's timeline says (2026 season, for the next appointment year) |
|---|---|
| July cycle | Applicants may begin submitting on July 1, 2026, and programs may begin reviewing applications on July 15, 2026 |
| December cycle | Applicants may begin submitting on November 12, 2026, and programs may begin reviewing on December 2, 2026 |

These are the dates AAMC published for the 2026 season, and they change annually, so check the timeline before you plan. Specialties differ: most fellowships use the July cycle, and a few, such as epilepsy and maternal-fetal medicine on AAMC's list, use the December cycle. Applicants for the 2026 season can also use program signals in participating specialties, according to AAMC.

Because fellowship applications are often due during the second or third year of residency, plan letters, research and exams early. For ideas on strong files, see [how programs view research](/wiki/research/how-programs-view-research) and [letters of recommendation](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation).

## What is different for IMGs

### Eligibility

NRMP says applicants must complete all training required for the position before it starts and meet ACGME requirements and any extra requirements of the sponsoring institution. Fellowship eligibility is set by each specialty's ACGME requirements. The ACGME fellowship Common Program Requirements also contain an exception that a Review Committee may allow: a program may accept an "exceptionally qualified" international graduate who completed a core residency outside the US that was not ACGME-accredited, if the program director and selection committee, the GMEC and ECFMG certification all clear it. ACGME says applicants accepted this way must be informed that their training may not lead to ABMS board certification. Not every specialty allows this exception, so ask the program.

If you completed a US residency, you are in the same pool as other residency graduates. IMG status itself is not a separate eligibility rule, but some programs prefer or require certain visas or training histories. We did not find a published rule on this, so ask each program.

### Visa continuity

**If you are on a J-1.** ECFMG sponsorship is tied to a specific program, specialty and dates. ECFMG's reference guide says a new Statement of Need is required if you enter a new specialty or subspecialty, and the home country that issued your statement cannot be changed once you hold J-1 status. The regulation limits J-1 participation in GME to the time typically required to complete the program, and, under 22 CFR 62.27, to seven years unless the State Department finds the home country has an exceptional need for the additional qualification. Residency plus fellowship can approach that limit in longer pathways, so count your years.

**If you are on an H-1B.** An H-1B is employer-specific. A fellowship at a different institution means a new petition or a transfer, and cap status can matter. See [H-1B cap-exempt employers](/wiki/visas/h1b-cap-exempt-employers). The H-1B has a six-year limit in general, with exceptions under the law. Time spent on H-1B in residency generally counts, so ask an attorney how fellowship years fit.

**The waiver connection.** If your J-1 is subject to the [two-year home residency rule](/wiki/visas/two-year-home-residency-rule), you will need a waiver before changing to H-1B or applying for permanent residence, and the common waiver programs involve service commitments. See [Conrad 30](/wiki/visas/conrad-30-waiver) and [J-1 waiver options](/wiki/visas/j1-waiver-options). Fellowship often comes before that service. Ask whether your target program, your state or a federal agency will support your waiver after training, and whether fellowship changes your eligibility.

> [!WARNING] Do not assume you can add a fellowship later without checking the visa. A fellowship on a J-1 can run into the seven-year limit, a new Statement of Need and a sponsor decision. Ask ECFMG and your international office before you apply, not after you match.

### Practical points

- **Ask each program** which visas it sponsors for fellows, and whether it supports waivers.
- **Check board eligibility.** Fellowships accredited by ACGME generally lead to board eligibility in the subspecialty, subject to each board's rules. See [board certification after residency](/wiki/licensure-career/board-certification-for-imgs).
- **Plan your exams.** If your visa plan includes H-1B at fellowship, Step 3 and licensing timing apply again. See [Step 3 and H-1B timing](/wiki/visas/step-3-and-h1b-timing).
- **Think about contracts.** Your later job and waiver plans interact with your fellowship end date. See [your first attending contract](/wiki/licensure-career/first-attending-contract).

## A simple planning sequence

1. In your PGY-1 or PGY-2 year, learn which service your target fellowship uses and its timeline.
2. Ask your program director about their fellowship placement and visa experience.
3. Check your J-1 or H-1B timeline for fellowship dates with the international office.
4. Register with ERAS, NRMP or SF Match as required, and keep records.
5. Keep a fallback: a job, a waiver plan or another program.

Fellowship rules, visa rules and cycle dates change. Verify each step on the official pages listed in the sources before acting.
