Whether US training is right for you is a personal decision with no single answer. It can offer excellent training, strong earning potential and access to research and subspecialty careers, but it also demands years of exams, money and uncertainty before any guarantee of a position. This page lays out both sides so you can decide with open eyes. It is written to inform, not to push you toward or away from the path.

## What the path asks of you

Before you weigh benefits, be clear about the entry price. An IMG usually needs to:

- Pass the required USMLE Steps and meet [ECFMG certification](/wiki/ecfmg/ecfmg-certification-overview) requirements.
- Build a competitive application, often including US clinical experience and letters.
- Apply through ERAS, interview, and be ranked in the Match by programs that choose to rank you.
- Secure a visa if you are not a US citizen or permanent resident.

For realistic timing, see [how long it takes](/wiki/start-here/how-long-does-it-take). For the money, see the [full budget](/wiki/start-here/total-cost-of-matching).

## Honest pros

These are common reasons people choose US training. Whether they apply to you depends on your field and goals.

- **Structured, accredited training.** US residency programs are accredited by [[acgme|ACGME]] against published requirements, including supervision, education and a cap on work hours.
- **Breadth of specialties and subspecialty paths.** Many fellowships are open to graduates of US residency programs.
- **Research and teaching infrastructure.** Academic centers offer research resources that may not exist at home.
- **Professional mobility.** Completing US training can make it easier to practice in some other countries, though recognition rules differ by country and change over time. Verify with the regulator in the country you care about.

> [!NOTE] Salary and lifestyle comparisons depend heavily on specialty, location, exchange rates and your family situation. We do not quote figures here because they change and mislead more than they help.

## Honest cons

- **Uncertainty is built in.** In the {{fact:nrmp_match_year}} Main Residency Match, NRMP reported PGY-1 match rates of {{fact:us_img_match_rate}} percent for U.S. citizen IMGs and {{fact:nonus_img_match_rate}} percent for non-U.S. citizen IMGs among applicants who submitted a rank order list (see [IMG match statistics](/wiki/start-here/img-match-statistics)). Those are averages, but they show the risk is real.
- **A rank-and-match system means you do not choose.** You apply, programs interview and rank you, and you may end up in a specialty or city you would not have chosen.
- **Long hours.** ACGME requires that clinical and educational work be limited to 80 hours per week, averaged over four weeks. That is a ceiling, not a typical week, and it applies to residents in all specialties.
- **Years of delayed income and savings.** Exam fees, travel for clinical experience, application and interview costs add up before your first paycheck.
- **Visa constraints.** J-1 and H-1B each carry rules that shape your options during and after training. Some J-1 holders must return home for a period or obtain a waiver (see the [two-year home residency rule](/wiki/visas/two-year-home-residency-rule)).
- **Distance from family.** Residency often means relocating without choice of city, sometimes alone, for three or more years.
- **Your seniority can reset.** A consultant or experienced specialist at home often starts again as a first-year resident.

## Questions to ask yourself

1. **What is the specific thing I want that I cannot get at home?** If you cannot name it, explore further before you spend money.
2. **What is my plan if I do not match in the first cycle?** Decide this in advance. See [if you do not match](/wiki/interviews-match/if-you-do-not-match).
3. **Can I afford the whole path, including a possible second cycle?** Set a budget cap.
4. **Does my family support the move, and what would they need?**
5. **Do I want to stay in the US long term, or return?** The answer affects visa choice and where you train.
6. **What are my realistic alternatives?** Training in another country, practicing at home, research or non-clinical paths are all legitimate.

## A simple way to test the decision

Instead of deciding all at once, make the first stage low-cost and reversible:

1. Read the official requirements (ECFMG, USMLE) and confirm your school and graduation year are eligible.
2. Take a diagnostic practice exam to see how your preparation stands (see [NBME self-assessments](/wiki/exams/nbme-self-assessments)).
3. Talk to several IMGs at different stages, including ones who did not match, and ask what they would do differently. Treat each story as one data point, not a forecast.
4. Set decision points: for example, a date by which you need a passing result, or a number of cycles after which you will reassess.

> [!IMG TIP] Write down your stopping rules before you start. It is far easier to make a clear-headed decision about a second or third cycle when you set the criteria in advance than after Match Day.

## Staying versus leaving afterwards

Finishing US training does not automatically mean staying in the US. Some physicians return home with US credentials; others pursue fellowships or positions in the US. Visa pathways shape this choice: see [J-1 versus H-1B](/wiki/visas/j1-vs-h1b) before you decide.

## What to do next

If you decide to proceed, read [the IMG roadmap](/wiki/start-here/imgs-roadmap) and [the common mistakes IMGs make](/wiki/start-here/common-img-mistakes). If you are undecided, that is a reasonable place to be: collect the evidence in this page's list of questions, then revisit.
