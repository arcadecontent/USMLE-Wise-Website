For most IMGs, getting from the first eligibility check to a first day of US residency takes several years, with the exact figure depending heavily on your starting point. No official body publishes an expected duration, so this page explains what drives the timeline and how to plan around it rather than quoting a single number.

## The components of the timeline

| Component | Typical drivers | Can it overlap other work? |
|---|---|---|
| Eligibility and credential verification | School response times, document availability | Yes, start early |
| Step 1 | Your starting knowledge and study time | Partly |
| Step 2 CK | Clinical knowledge, time since clerkships | Partly |
| US clinical experience | Visa, funds, availability of sites | Yes |
| Research | Project length, publication timelines | Yes |
| Application season | Fixed by AAMC and NRMP calendars | No |
| Visa after Match | Embassy and sponsor processing | After the Match |

Read the [year-by-year roadmap](/wiki/start-here/imgs-roadmap) for how these fit together.

## Fixed dates versus flexible work

Some parts of the timeline you control: when to study, when to test (within the rules), and when to do clinical experience. Other parts are set externally.

- The application and Match calendars are set by [ERAS](/wiki/application/eras-timeline) and the [[nrmp]] each year. You cannot speed them up.
- Test appointments are limited by scheduling windows and seat availability. See [scheduling your exam](/wiki/exams/scheduling-with-prometric).
- ECFMG certification has its own requirements and deadlines relative to the Match and the start of training. See the [ECFMG certification overview](/wiki/ecfmg/ecfmg-certification-overview) and confirm current dates with ECFMG.
- Visa processing happens after the Match and has its own queues: [your visa timeline after the Match](/wiki/visas/visa-timeline-after-the-match).

> [!WARNING] The most common cause of lost years is not slow studying but missing a fixed date: an unverified credential, an exam result that arrives after the application, or a visa that is not ready by the start date.

## Three illustrative scenarios

These are illustrative, not predictions.

### Recent graduate, no major delays

Eligibility and verification run while you study. Step 1 and Step 2 CK happen in sequence, you gather clinical experience and letters, then you apply. This is a multi-year plan even when everything goes well, because exams and clinical experience are sequential in practice.

### Several years since graduation

You may need more time for refreshing basic science, recent US experience and a clearer explanation for gaps. Programs differ in how they weigh years since graduation; see [how programs screen applications](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters).

### Not matching first time

A reapplication adds at least one more cycle. The strategy changes in the second round: see [reapplicant strategy](/wiki/application/reapplicant-strategy) and [if you do not match](/wiki/interviews-match/if-you-do-not-match).

## What lengthens the timeline

- **Failed or incomplete exam attempts.** Waiting periods and attempt limits apply: [attempt limits and retakes](/wiki/exams/usmle-attempt-limits-and-retakes).
- **Slow verification.** Documents from schools can take a long time.
- **Limited seats and scheduling windows** at test centers.
- **Funding gaps** that force breaks in preparation.
- **Visa delays** for observerships or interviews.
- **Changing specialty** after wasted experience.

## What shortens it

- Starting credential verification before you finish studying.
- Testing when self-assessments show you are ready, rather than at the first possible date. See [NBME self-assessments](/wiki/exams/nbme-self-assessments).
- Working backward from the application opening date.
- Combining USCE, letters and research toward the same specialty.

## Build a backward plan

1. Choose the application cycle you want to target.
2. Mark the date your exams must be done and results received.
3. Subtract study time and an eligibility period buffer for each Step.
4. Mark when documents and letters must be ready.
5. Add one spare quarter for surprises.

Use [when to take each Step](/wiki/exams/when-to-take-each-step) to choose the order and spacing.

> [!IMG TIP] If your plan only works when nothing goes wrong, it is too tight. A realistic plan survives one delay.

## Rule, practice and opinion

Official requirements (exams, certification, attempt limits) set a floor on the timeline. Common practice (observerships, research, several months of dedicated study) sets the typical length. Our opinion: treat the plan as a series of checkpoints rather than a deadline, and decide at each checkpoint whether to continue or adjust.

For the money side of the same plan, see [the total cost of matching](/wiki/start-here/total-cost-of-matching).

> [!NOTE] This page gives structure, not a promise. Your timeline depends on your own circumstances and on policies that change. Confirm requirements on the official sites listed above.
