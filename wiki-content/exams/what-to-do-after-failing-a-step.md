After a failed attempt, do four things in order: read your score report carefully, confirm exactly how many attempts and what timing rules apply to you, diagnose why it went wrong, and rebuild your plan around evidence rather than hope. Avoid rebooking quickly out of panic, because every attempt counts toward your limit.

## Step 1: Read the score report

For failing examinees, USMLE provides feedback on content areas. For Step 1 reported as pass/fail, a failing outcome comes with feedback on content areas requiring further study. For other exams, the report shows overall performance and whether each content area was higher, lower or about the same as your overall result.

USMLE advises that failing scores are best remediated through attention to **all** content areas, not only the lowest. Treat the report as a map, not a diagnosis.

## Step 2: Check your attempts and timing

Under the current rules you have {{fact:usmle_attempts_per_step}} attempts per Step in total, no more than three attempts in any 12-month period, and a fourth attempt must be at least 12 months after your first and at least six months after your most recent one. Four failures at any Step make you ineligible to apply for any Step. Read [attempt limits and retakes](/wiki/exams/usmle-attempt-limits-and-retakes) for the full rule set.

> [!WARNING] Many forum posts quote old limits or invented waiting periods. Use the Bulletin and your registering organization for your own situation.

## Step 3: Decide whether to request a recheck

The Bulletin describes a score recheck process, but USMLE reports that to date it has not resulted in a score change. A recheck costs money and time, so weigh that against putting the same resources into preparation. This is our view (opinion): unless you have a specific reason to suspect an error, focus on the retake.

## Step 4: Diagnose the cause

Common causes, with a different fix for each:

| Cause | Typical signs | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge gaps | Weak across content areas; low on question banks | Rebuild with a structured core resource and questions |
| Test-taking | Know the material but misread or rush | Practice timed mixed blocks; review how you read stems |
| Time and stamina | Fading late in the day | Full-day simulations |
| Anxiety or health | Performance drops under pressure | Support, rest and possibly accommodations |
| Poor timing | Booked too early or in a hurry | Use self-assessments to set the date |

If you suspect a health or learning issue, speak to a qualified professional. Accommodations have their own process.

## Step 5: Rebuild the plan

1. **Get a fresh baseline** with an official self-assessment. See [NBME self-assessments](/wiki/exams/nbme-self-assessments).
2. **Choose one core resource and one question bank.** Do not restart with five.
3. **Study by error pattern,** not by topic list.
4. **Set a rebooking rule** in advance, for example: only when the last several practice results are comfortably above your target.
5. **Allow time for the eligibility period and scheduling.** See [scheduling with Prometric](/wiki/exams/scheduling-with-prometric).

> [!IMG TIP] A retake date set by a self-assessment trend is usually safer than one set by calendar pressure. Moving the date costs a fee inside 45 days; a second failure costs an attempt.

## Step 6: Think about the application

A failed attempt changes how you present your application, but it is not the end of it. Read [how programs screen applications](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters) and consider the timeline impact in [how long it takes](/wiki/start-here/how-long-does-it-take). If it pushes you out of a cycle, see [reapplicant strategy](/wiki/application/reapplicant-strategy).

Keep your explanation factual and short: what happened, what you changed, and what the later result shows. Do not blame the exam or the test center.

## What not to do

- Retake without new evidence of readiness.
- Switch to a completely new set of resources.
- Skip the score report analysis.
- Hide the attempt. It is on your transcript.
- Let embarrassment stop you from asking mentors or advisors for help.

## Support

Talk to someone who has seen the pattern: a faculty advisor, a mentor who passed after a failure, or a coach who will look at your data. Choose whatever structure gives you honest feedback and accountability.

> [!NOTE] A failure does not predict the result of your next attempt. Rules, formats and fees change, so verify them on the official sources before acting.
