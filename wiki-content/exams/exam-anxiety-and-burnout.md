Dedicated study for USMLE is demanding, and many IMGs do it while far from family, working, or waiting on visas. The short answer: some stress is normal, a few habits make it more manageable, and persistent or severe symptoms deserve a professional, not more willpower. If you are studying with ADHD, there is a [section on study structure for ADHD](#studying-for-usmle-with-adhd) below, and a pointer to the official accommodations process.

> [!NOTE] This page is general information, not medical advice, and it cannot diagnose anything. If you are worried about your health, talk to a licensed clinician.

## If you are in crisis

If you are thinking about harming yourself or feel unsafe, do not wait for exam results to improve. In the United States, call or text **988** or use the chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org), and in an emergency call your local emergency number. If you are outside the US, contact local emergency services or a crisis line in your country. You can also tell a friend, family member or doctor today.

## Stress, anxiety and burnout are different things

| Experience | What it often looks like | Usual response |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary exam stress | Nervousness that rises near test dates and eases afterward | Routine, sleep, practice |
| Exam anxiety | Racing thoughts, physical tension, blanking, avoiding practice tests | Skills training; consider a professional |
| Burnout | Exhaustion, cynicism, feeling you are not progressing despite effort | Rest, workload change, support |
| Symptoms that may need assessment | Symptoms that persist, spread beyond studying, or impair daily life | Evaluation by a clinician |

Only a clinician can tell which of these applies to you. The table is a map, not a diagnosis.

## Practical habits that help

These are common, low-risk strategies. They are not a cure and may not suit everyone.

1. **Protect sleep.** Mood and concentration both suffer when sleep is cut. Studying until 3 a.m. rarely pays for itself.
2. **Use a fixed daily structure.** A start time, an end time and a rest day each week reduce decision fatigue.
3. **Move your body.** Regular exercise is widely recommended for mood and stress.
4. **Stop comparing.** Score screenshots on forums and social media are a curated sample. Compare yourself with your own baseline.
5. **Plan around data, not dread.** Track practice results and use them to adjust your plan. See [how long to study](/wiki/exams/how-long-to-study).
6. **Schedule worry.** Setting a short time each day to write worries down can keep them from taking over study blocks.
7. **Keep one non-medical connection.** A call, a meal with someone, a walk. Isolation makes everything feel heavier.

## Anxiety around practice tests and the real exam

Avoiding practice exams because they feel threatening is a pattern worth noticing. NIMH says avoiding feared situations may feel helpful briefly, but the anxiety is likely to remain without treatment. Gradual exposure, such as short timed blocks that build toward full-length simulations, follows the same idea as exposure-based therapy. Opinion: build up in small steps and keep the first ones easy.

Before test day, rehearse logistics so there are fewer unknowns: the route, ID, check-in and what to bring (see the [exam day checklist](/wiki/exams/exam-day-rules-and-checklist)). If you blank on a question, the habit that helps most people is the same as in practice: mark it, move on, and return if time allows.

## Studying for USMLE with ADHD

> [!NOTE] This section is general information about study structure. It is not medical advice, it cannot tell you whether you have ADHD, and it says nothing about treatment. Diagnosis, treatment and medication decisions belong with a licensed clinician.

NIMH describes ADHD as a neurodevelopmental condition marked by inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity, with symptoms that often continue into adulthood. It lists medication and psychosocial interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy as standard treatments. USMLE prep is hard in ways that can be especially hard with attention difficulties: long stretches of self-directed work, few outside deadlines and a large volume of repetitive review.

The ideas below are common study-structure strategies. They are our opinion, not proven treatments, and we have no outcome data for them. Use what helps and drop what does not.

### Study structure ideas

1. **Shrink the unit of work.** Study in timed blocks with a defined pause after each, rather than open-ended "study all day" sessions. Using the real block length ({{fact:usmle_block_minutes}} minutes) also rehearses the exam.
2. **Lower the cost of starting.** Same place, same start time, and the first task decided the night before. Begin with the easiest block of the day if starting is the hard part.
3. **Make questions the main method.** Question blocks give immediate feedback and a clear finish. Long passive reading or video can be harder to sustain, and you can check this against your own results.
4. **Change the format, not the resource.** Rotate by discipline or system, switch between timed and tutor mode, explain answers aloud, or change location. Keep one question bank and one review source, because adding resources is a common way to avoid starting.
5. **Add outside structure.** A study partner, mentor or coach who checks your plan each week. Studying alongside another person, in the room or on video, is a common way to hold focus.
6. **Make progress visible.** Use your [error log](/wiki/exams/step-1-study-schedule), a weekly checklist and the trend of your assessment results, so progress does not depend on how the day feels.
7. **Protect the basics.** Sleep, meals and movement come first. Put your hardest content in the part of the day when your energy is best.
8. **Rehearse the long day.** Step 2 CK is one {{fact:step2ck_session_hours}}-hour session with breaks you manage yourself. Practice full-length sessions with the real block and break structure so stamina and pacing are not a surprise. See the [exam day guide](/wiki/exams/exam-day-rules-and-checklist).

### Overcoming boredom when studying for Step 2 CK

Step 2 CK is broad and the question volume is high, so some boredom is predictable. Plan for it instead of trying to out-will it:

- **Theme your weeks.** Give each week a discipline and a finish line, so you can see an end.
- **Alternate heavy and light tasks.** Follow a demanding timed block with error-log review or a short re-quiz of earlier mistakes.
- **Use the phases.** A plan with a foundation, core, integration and final phase gives the work a shape. See the [Step 2 CK study plan](/wiki/exams/step-2-ck-study-schedule).
- **Plan the rewards and the rest.** A fixed stop time, planned breaks and a weekly rest day are part of the plan, not a bonus.
- **Notice avoidance.** If you cannot start at all, or boredom keeps turning into avoidance, that is worth discussing with a clinician. We cannot tell you what it means.

### Medication and exam day

Questions about medication, including timing around exam day, belong with your prescriber. The USMLE exam-day rules restrict what you may bring into the testing room, so read the [exam day checklist](/wiki/exams/exam-day-rules-and-checklist), check the current pre-approved items list, and ask the testing organization if you are unsure whether something is permitted.

### Official accommodations for ADHD

If a documented condition affects how you can test, you may be able to request a test accommodation. USMLE's request page includes impairment-specific guidance for ADHD, which asks for an evaluation report from a qualified professional and objective non-medical records of impaired functioning. Read the current guidance on usmle.org, because it controls what you must submit.

Plan for the timing and the limits:

- USMLE reviews each request individually, and decisions take about 60 business days.
- USMLE says accommodations are not a guarantee of improved performance, test completion or a particular outcome.
- We cannot tell you what a request would be approved for. Do not book travel or rely on an accommodation until you have a written decision.

See the [testing accommodations guide](/wiki/exams/testing-accommodations) for the process, the documentation and the timeline, including what is different for IMGs.

## When to get professional help

Consider speaking to a doctor, psychologist or counselor if:

- symptoms last most days for weeks, or are getting worse;
- you cannot sleep, eat or concentrate despite rest;
- you are avoiding studying, work or people;
- you are using alcohol or other substances to cope;
- you feel hopeless.

NIMH describes cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) as a well-established, effective treatment for anxiety disorders, and notes that medication is also an option for some people, to be decided with a clinician. Many universities, employers and insurers offer counseling, and telehealth may help if you are abroad.

> [!IMG TIP] As an IMG you may be dealing with migration, finances, or distance from home on top of exam pressure. Naming those pressures to a counselor is part of getting support, not a distraction from studying.

## Accommodations

If a documented condition affects how you can test, you may be able to request a test accommodation. USMLE reviews requests individually, approval is not guaranteed, and review takes time. See the [testing accommodations guide](/wiki/exams/testing-accommodations).

## After a bad practice score or a failed attempt

One poor practice result is information, not a verdict. A failed attempt is hard emotionally and practically, and the [what to do after failing guide](/wiki/exams/what-to-do-after-failing-a-step) covers the next steps. Because attempts are limited to {{fact:usmle_attempts_per_step}} per Step, give yourself time to recover and rework your plan before booking again.

## Burnout and decision-making

Burnout often makes people want to switch resources, change specialties or quit entirely. Opinion: delay big decisions until you have had sleep and a few lighter days. Then decide with a mentor or trusted colleague, and revisit your timeline using your actual results.
