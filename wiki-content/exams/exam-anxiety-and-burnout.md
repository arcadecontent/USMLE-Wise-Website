Dedicated study for USMLE is demanding, and many IMGs do it while far from family, working, or waiting on visas. The short answer: some stress is normal, a few habits make it more manageable, and persistent or severe symptoms deserve a professional, not more willpower.

> [!NOTE] This page is general information, not medical advice, and it cannot diagnose anything. If you are worried about your health, talk to a licensed clinician.

## If you are in crisis

If you are thinking about harming yourself or feel unsafe, do not wait for exam results to improve. In the United States, call or text **988** or use the chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org), and in an emergency call your local emergency number. If you are outside the US, contact local emergency services or a crisis line in your country. You can also tell a friend, family member or doctor today.

## Stress, anxiety and burnout are different things

| Experience | What it often looks like | Usual response |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary exam stress | Nervousness that rises near test dates and eases afterward | Routine, sleep, practice |
| Exam anxiety | Racing thoughts, physical tension, blanking, avoiding practice tests | Skills training; consider a professional |
| Burnout | Exhaustion, cynicism, feeling you are not progressing despite effort | Rest, workload change, support |
| Symptoms that may need assessment | Symptoms that persist, spread beyond studying, or impair daily life | Evaluation by a clinician |

Only a clinician can tell which of these applies to you. The table is a map, not a diagnosis.

## Practical habits that help

These are common, low-risk strategies. They are not a cure and may not suit everyone.

1. **Protect sleep.** Mood and concentration both suffer when sleep is cut. Studying until 3 a.m. rarely pays for itself.
2. **Use a fixed daily structure.** A start time, an end time and a rest day each week reduce decision fatigue.
3. **Move your body.** Regular exercise is widely recommended for mood and stress.
4. **Stop comparing.** Score screenshots on forums and social media are a curated sample. Compare yourself with your own baseline.
5. **Plan around data, not dread.** Track practice results and use them to adjust your plan. See [how long to study](/wiki/exams/how-long-to-study).
6. **Schedule worry.** Setting a short time each day to write worries down can keep them from taking over study blocks.
7. **Keep one non-medical connection.** A call, a meal with someone, a walk. Isolation makes everything feel heavier.

## Anxiety around practice tests and the real exam

Avoiding practice exams because they feel threatening is a pattern worth noticing. NIMH says avoiding feared situations may feel helpful briefly, but the anxiety is likely to remain without treatment. Gradual exposure, such as short timed blocks that build toward full-length simulations, follows the same idea as exposure-based therapy. Opinion: build up in small steps and keep the first ones easy.

Before test day, rehearse logistics so there are fewer unknowns: the route, ID, check-in and what to bring (see the [exam day checklist](/wiki/exams/exam-day-rules-and-checklist)). If you blank on a question, the habit that helps most people is the same as in practice: mark it, move on, and return if time allows.

## When to get professional help

Consider speaking to a doctor, psychologist or counselor if:

- symptoms last most days for weeks, or are getting worse;
- you cannot sleep, eat or concentrate despite rest;
- you are avoiding studying, work or people;
- you are using alcohol or other substances to cope;
- you feel hopeless.

NIMH describes cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) as a well-established, effective treatment for anxiety disorders, and notes that medication is also an option for some people, to be decided with a clinician. Many universities, employers and insurers offer counseling, and telehealth may help if you are abroad.

> [!IMG TIP] As an IMG you may be dealing with migration, finances, or distance from home on top of exam pressure. Naming those pressures to a counselor is part of getting support, not a distraction from studying.

## Accommodations

If a documented condition affects how you can test, you may be able to request a test accommodation. USMLE reviews requests individually, approval is not guaranteed, and review takes time. See the [testing accommodations guide](/wiki/exams/testing-accommodations).

## After a bad practice score or a failed attempt

One poor practice result is information, not a verdict. A failed attempt is hard emotionally and practically, and the [what to do after failing guide](/wiki/exams/what-to-do-after-failing-a-step) covers the next steps. Because attempts are limited to {{fact:usmle_attempts_per_step}} per Step, give yourself time to recover and rework your plan before booking again.

## Burnout and decision-making

Burnout often makes people want to switch resources, change specialties or quit entirely. Opinion: delay big decisions until you have had sleep and a few lighter days. Then decide with a mentor or trusted colleague, and revisit your timeline using your actual results.
