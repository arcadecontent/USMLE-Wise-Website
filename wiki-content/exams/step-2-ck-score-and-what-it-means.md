Your Step 2 CK score is reported on a three-digit scale, and the minimum passing score is {{fact:step2ck_passing_standard}} for exams administered on or after July 1, 2025. What counts as a good score has no single answer, because it depends on your specialty, your programs and the rest of your application. USMLE does publish a percentile table, but only for first-time test takers from US medical schools, so it is not an IMG percentile.

This page explains how Step 2 CK is graded, what the score range and percentiles mean, how to read them as an [[img|IMG]], and how to set a realistic target without relying on rumours.

## How is Step 2 CK graded?

USMLE grades Step 2 CK against a set standard of proficiency, not against other test takers. The Bulletin of Information says recommended performance standards are based on a specified level of proficiency, so no predetermined percentage of examinees pass or fail. The minimum passing score is reviewed periodically and can change, with notices posted on the USMLE website.

One exam produces two results:

1. **A pass or fail result.** You pass if you reach the minimum passing standard, currently {{fact:step2ck_passing_standard}} for exams on or after July 1, 2025.
2. **A three-digit score.** This is a scaled score, not a percent correct.

The number of questions you must answer correctly is not fixed. USMLE says the percentage needed to pass varies by Step and from form to form, and it uses statistical procedures to adjust for small differences in difficulty across forms and years. That is why a score is not a simple count of right answers.

> [!IMG TIP] Treat online "percent correct to score" converters as guesses. USMLE says the percentage required varies from form to form, and the Bulletin gives only a rough typical figure for passing, not a conversion for higher scores. Judge your readiness from the trend in your [NBME self-assessments](/wiki/exams/nbme-self-assessments), not from a conversion chart.

## Step 2 CK score range and how scores are reported

Step 2 CK is reported on a three-digit scale, as is Step 3, while Step 1 is reported as pass/fail only. The passing standard is one point on that scale. It tells you whether you passed, not where you rank. USMLE prints the exact lowest and highest reportable scores in its Score Interpretation Guidelines, so [check the current table](https://www.usmle.org/scores-transcripts) rather than trusting a number from a video or forum.

| Exam | What is reported |
|---|---|
| Step 1 | Pass/fail only, since {{fact:step1_pass_fail_since}} |
| Step 2 CK | A pass/fail result and a three-digit score |
| Step 3 | A pass/fail result and a three-digit score |

USMLE gives three rules for reading scores:

- **Across forms and years.** Scores for a given Step are adjusted so they are comparable across forms and across years.
- **Over time.** USMLE says exams change in content and format, so individual scores separated by more than three to four years should not be compared. If you are a reapplicant or graduated some time ago, expect an older score to be read with more caution. See [years since graduation](/wiki/application/years-since-graduation).
- **Across Steps.** The same number on Step 2 CK and on Step 3 does not mean the same level of performance. USMLE says a comparison of scores across Steps is not appropriate.

## Does USMLE publish Step 2 CK percentiles?

Yes, with an important limit. USMLE's Score Interpretation Guidelines include a norm table that turns a Step 2 CK score into a percentile rank, but the comparison group is first-time test takers from medical schools accredited by the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME), which accredits MD programs in the United States. We found no USMLE percentile table specific to IMGs.

What USMLE publishes:

- **A norm table.** For each score, it shows the share of the comparison group who scored lower. The dates covered by the group are printed above the table.
- **A mean and standard deviation.** These describe the same group of first-time takers, for the most recent years, in a separate table.
- **An annual update.** USMLE says norm tables are updated every year by dropping earlier examinees and adding newer ones, and tells readers to use the most recent table available. A percentile you saw last year, or in an old forum post, may no longer match.

We do not copy the figures onto this page because they change every year. To read them, [check the current table](https://www.usmle.org/scores-transcripts):

1. Open the Score Interpretation Guidelines from USMLE's scores and transcripts page.
2. Find the most recent norm table and note the date range and the group it describes.
3. Find your score in the score column and read across to the Step 2 CK percentile column.
4. Read the mean and standard deviation table for the same group to see how far your score sits from the average.

A percentile rank answers one question: what share of the comparison group scored lower than you. It describes your standing in that group. It does not predict whether you will pass, interview or match.

> [!WARNING] A US norm table is not an IMG percentile. The people in the table are first-time takers from US schools, so it shows where your score would sit among them, not among IMGs.

### How precise is a score?

Every test has measurement error. USMLE publishes a standard error of measurement for Step 2 CK of several points: the same person tested on a different but equivalent set of questions could score somewhat higher or lower. The current error figures are in the "Precision of Scores" section of the guidelines.

So a gap of a few points between two scores, or between your score and a friend's, rarely means a real difference in knowledge. USMLE's own guidance says it directly: "Small differences in Step examination scores alone should not be used as the basis for selection decisions about examinees." Programs set their own screening rules, so this guidance does not stop a program from using a cutoff.

## How to read a Step 2 CK percentile as an IMG

This section is our opinion, not a USMLE rule.

- **Use it as a rough yardstick, not a verdict.** The table describes US first-time takers. It does not describe the IMG applicants in your specialty, who are reported separately by NRMP, so it tells you little about where you stand among them.
- **Compare with your own target, not with the table.** Look at what matched applicants in your specialty and applicant group tend to have. NRMP reports outcomes separately for different groups, including non-U.S. citizen IMGs. See [IMG match rates by specialty](/wiki/specialties/img-match-rates-by-specialty) and the [NRMP](https://www.nrmp.org/match-data-analytics/) data pages, and check the year.
- **Do not set a goal in percentiles.** In our experience programs and their screening filters work with the score itself, or with other criteria, not with a percentile. A percentile also shifts when USMLE updates the table.
- **Ignore tiny differences.** A few points either way is within normal measurement noise. Spend that energy on the parts of your application you can still change.
- **Be careful with third-party charts.** A percentile chart on a blog or in a video may be out of date or may mix groups. The official table is the one to use, and it carries its own date.

## What is a good Step 2 CK score?

There is no single good score. The passing standard is the only official line, and it is a minimum, not a competitive target. Whether a score is good depends on what you are trying to achieve.

| If "good" means | Who decides | Where to check |
|---|---|---|
| Passing | USMLE | The [USMLE scoring page](https://www.usmle.org/scores-transcripts/examination-results-and-scoring) |
| Competitive for a specialty | Programs, through their own screens | Program websites, official match data and the specialty pages |
| Strong compared with US students | Nobody officially for IMGs | USMLE's norm table (US first-takers only) |
| Good enough for your list | You, with a mentor | The programs you plan to apply to |

Several things move the answer:

- **Specialty.** Competitive specialties and less competitive ones draw very different score ranges. See [choosing a specialty](/wiki/specialties/choosing-a-specialty).
- **Program type.** Programs differ in how much weight they give to a score, and some publish no cutoff at all.
- **The rest of the application.** US clinical experience, letters, research and recency of graduation all count. See [how programs screen applications](/wiki/application/program-screening-filters).
- **Your applicant group.** Published match data generally show that higher scores are associated with better odds, but a high score alone does not offset other gaps, and a lower score does not close every door. See [IMG match rates by specialty](/wiki/specialties/img-match-rates-by-specialty) for how to read the data.

If your score is lower than you hoped, read [application strategy when your scores are not ideal](/wiki/application/strategy-with-lower-scores).

## Why the score matters more now

Step 1 has been pass/fail since {{fact:step1_pass_fail_since}}, so Step 2 CK is the main scored USMLE exam that programs can compare. This does not mean every program uses it the same way:

- Some programs use filters at the screening stage.
- Others read the whole application and use the score as one input.
- Some weigh US clinical experience and letters as heavily.

> [!IMG TIP] Do not rely on a forum screenshot of "average scores" for your specialty. Use official data from NRMP and the programs' own websites, and check the year.

## How to find out what your target programs expect

1. **Read program websites** for stated requirements, such as minimum exam results or years since graduation.
2. **Use NRMP data** on matched applicants, including the program director survey, where available. Check the year and the specialty. Do not generalize from US MD data to IMGs without checking. See the [NRMP data pages](https://www.nrmp.org/match-data-analytics/).
3. **Look at specialty pages** such as [choosing a specialty](/wiki/specialties/choosing-a-specialty) and [IMG match rates by specialty](/wiki/specialties/img-match-rates-by-specialty).
4. **Ask mentors** who have recently matched, and treat the answers as anecdote, not rules.

## Set a target, not a hope

A useful target comes from the programs you plan to apply to, not from the passing standard or a percentile. Our opinion (not an official rule):

| Step | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Before studying | Which specialties and programs am I aiming at, and what do they say about scores? |
| During studying | Are my self-assessment trends on track for that goal? See [NBME self-assessments](/wiki/exams/nbme-self-assessments) |
| After the exam | Does my score fit the list I planned? If not, what else can strengthen my application? |

## What if your score is lower than hoped

A lower score limits some options but not all. Programs also look at US clinical experience, letters, research, recency of graduation and fit. Consider:

- Widening your list and diversifying specialties within what is realistic. See [how many programs to apply to](/wiki/application/how-many-programs-to-apply-to).
- Strengthening other parts of the application: [USCE](/wiki/clinical-experience/usce-explained), [letters](/wiki/application/letters-of-recommendation) and [research](/wiki/research/why-research-matters-for-imgs).
- Being honest and specific about growth in your explanation of any weaker results. If you also have a failed attempt, see [failed attempts on your application](/wiki/application/failed-attempts-on-application).

> [!WARNING] You cannot retake Step 2 CK to raise a passing score. The Bulletin allows retaking a passed Step only to meet a time limit set by a US medical licensing authority or another authority USMLE recognizes. If you are weighing a delay to improve your preparation, decide before you take the exam, not after. See [attempt limits and retakes](/wiki/exams/usmle-attempt-limits-and-retakes).

## When Step 2 CK results arrive

USMLE publishes a typical release window for results and advises building in extra time beyond it, and the report stays online for a limited period afterward. If your application timeline is tight, plan around the longer estimate. See [score reports and transcripts](/wiki/exams/score-reports-and-transcripts) for the current timing and [when to take each Step](/wiki/exams/when-to-take-each-step).

## Mistakes to avoid

- Using the passing standard, or a percentile, as your goal.
- Quoting a US percentile as if it were an IMG percentile.
- Using an old norm table or a forum chart instead of the current official table.
- Treating a gap of a few points as a real difference.
- Trusting old averages from before the pass/fail change.
- Comparing yourself with US MD applicants without checking the IMG data.
- Treating a single self-assessment as your final score.
- Assuming a high score alone offsets other gaps.

## Putting it together

Treat Step 2 CK as one part of a profile. Read the full [Step 2 CK guide](/wiki/exams/step-2-ck) for format and registration, use the official norm table only as a rough yardstick, and use your results to plan the rest of the application.

> [!NOTE] Passing standards, score reporting and the percentile table are set by USMLE and can change. Check the official [scores and transcripts page](https://www.usmle.org/scores-transcripts) before you use any number from this wiki or anywhere else.
