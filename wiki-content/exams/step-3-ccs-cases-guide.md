Step 3 CCS cases (computer-based case simulations) are interactive patient-management cases on Day 2 of Step 3. You type orders for a simulated patient, advance a simulated clock to see results, and keep managing the case until it ends. USMLE says CCS performance affects your Step 3 score and could affect whether you pass, so CCS deserves its own preparation, separate from multiple-choice practice.

This guide covers what USMLE says CCS is, how the software works, how scoring is described, a preparation approach, common mistakes and a day-before checklist. If your main question is how much practice is enough, read [how many CCS cases to practice](/wiki/exams/how-many-ccs-cases-to-practice) next. For the exam as a whole, see the [Step 3 guide](/wiki/exams/step-3).

> [!NOTE] This page is independent and not affiliated with USMLE, NBME, FSMB or ECFMG. Case counts, time limits and software details are set by USMLE and can change, so wherever a number matters we say "check the current figure on usmle.org" rather than printing one.

## What Step 3 CCS cases are

USMLE describes CCS as a dynamic, interactive simulation of a patient-care situation that tests your approach to clinical management: diagnosis, treatment and monitoring. You act as the physician responsible for one patient at a time, using the *Primum* software.

- **The patient can be acute or chronic.** A case can unfold over minutes of simulated time (an emergency) or over months (a chronic problem).
- **The setting changes.** USMLE lists the office, the emergency department, an inpatient unit, the ICU and the patient's home.
- **You do the work yourself.** Other team members do not write orders for you, so monitoring and treatment you do not order do not happen.
- **It is not a test of bedside manner.** USMLE says CCS does not measure skills that need human interaction, such as taking a history from a live person or performing a physical examination. It measures management decisions.

CCS sits on Day 2 (Advanced Clinical Medicine) with the multiple-choice items. The number of cases and the real-time limit per case are published on the [Step 3 exam content page](https://www.usmle.org/step-exams/step-3/step-3-exam-content): check the current figure there before you plan your pacing.

## How CCS differs from the multiple-choice part

| | Multiple-choice items | CCS cases |
|---|---|---|
| What you do | Choose the best answer from the options shown | Decide for yourself what to order, in what order, and when to look again |
| What it tests | Recognizing a presentation and reasoning to the best answer | Managing a patient over time: diagnosis, treatment, monitoring, follow-up |
| Recall demand | Options jog your memory | You must produce orders by name, with nothing to recognize |
| Time | A timer per block | A real-time limit per case, plus a simulated clock inside the case |
| Reversibility | Questions are self-contained | Once you advance the clock you cannot go back, and some orders cannot be cancelled after their results are seen |
| Scoring idea | One best answer | Credit for indicated actions, loss for unnecessary or harmful ones, with timing and sequence mattering |

The practical consequence is that CCS skill does not come automatically from multiple-choice skill. Many examinees who reason well on questions still lose time and credit in CCS because they are unfamiliar with the software or have no routine for a case.

## How the CCS interface works

The details below come from USMLE's CCS pages and its Step 3 CCS common questions. Confirm them against the current official material, because the software and wording are updated from time to time.

### Order entry

- You type orders in free text on the **order sheet**. USMLE says you can place orders only in the order sheet section of the patient chart.
- The software recognizes orders from names or acronyms as you type. You do not need verbs such as "administer".
- Medications must be requested by a specific generic or trade name. A drug class is not accepted.
- You do not need to specify doses or rates. Medication duration is assumed to be appropriate.
- Medications cannot be ordered "as needed". You order them when indicated and cancel them when no longer needed.
- Physical examination components are ordered separately, and you can cancel an order that has not yet been carried out if new information changes your plan.

### Advancing the clock

- Orders are only carried out, and results only appear, as simulated time moves forward. Each diagnostic order shows the simulated time at which its result will be available.
- You advance the clock yourself. If you advance it when nothing is pending, the case moves to the next patient update or ends.
- Requesting an interval history or a physical examination also uses simulated time.
- Advancing is a one-way action. You cannot rewind.

### Changing location

- You move the patient between the home, office, emergency department, inpatient unit and ICU as you judge appropriate.
- You cannot send a patient directly to the operating room, labor and delivery, or inpatient or outpatient laboratory or imaging. USMLE says CCS staff arrange those.

### Ending a case

- A case ends when the real-time limit runs out or when you have shown enough skill. USMLE warns that an early end does not necessarily mean you did something right or wrong.
- Once the **Case-end Instructions** screen appears you have a short period to finish orders and review the chart, but you can no longer order an examination, change location, schedule follow-up or receive pending results. You finish by selecting Exit Case.
- Real time you do not use on one case is not added to the next.

## How USMLE describes CCS scoring

This section reports what USMLE publishes. It is the rule, not our opinion.

- **CCS counts.** USMLE says that on Step 3 your performance on the case simulations affects your Step 3 score and could affect whether you pass or fail.
- **The weight is capped by time.** The proportional contribution of the CCS score is no greater than the proportional contribution of the time allotted for CCS.
- **What is scored.** Scoring considers diagnosis (including examination and tests), therapy, monitoring, timing, sequencing and location.
- **Credit and loss.** Indicated actions earn credit. Actions that are not indicated, or are potentially harmful, reduce the score. Unnecessary or excessive orders can also lower it.
- **Sequence and timing matter.** USMLE says seemingly correct decisions made in an incorrect sequence may earn no credit.
- **Cost is indirect.** USMLE says cost is accounted for indirectly, through how inappropriate an action is.
- **Counseling and diet orders** usually carry little weight unless they are specifically relevant to the case.
- **Scoring is rubric-based.** USMLE says CCS cases are scored using algorithms defined by expert clinicians, not by a simple count of orders.

> [!WARNING] USMLE does not publish a per-order scoring key. Anyone who quotes exact point values for CCS actions, or promises that a certain checklist guarantees credit, is guessing. Treat unofficial scoring claims as opinion.

## How to prepare for CCS cases

Rule (official): USMLE tells examinees to work through the Step 3 interactive testing experience before test day and says that practicing CCS there helps, with examinees who skip it possibly at a disadvantage. The official site also provides sample cases with written feedback and an instructional video on running a case. At the test center, USMLE offers an optional CCS tutorial but no practice cases.

Practice and opinion (ours): the approach below is what we suggest, not an official method.

1. **Do the official material first.** Run the interactive testing experience, the sample cases and the video before any other resource. They use the real interface, which is the one thing a third-party tool can only approximate.
2. **Learn the mechanics before the medicine.** Practice typing orders, naming drugs, advancing time, and ending a case until the software is boring. Then your attention is free for clinical decisions.
3. **Build one repeatable workflow** and use it on every case (see below).
4. **Add more cases from other sources, with care.** Several question-bank providers sell CCS practice, and they are not USMLE products, so their cases and scoring may differ from the real exam. See [question banks compared](/wiki/exams/question-banks-compared) for how to judge a resource.
5. **Review every case against what was needed.** After each case, write down what was missed, what was unnecessary and what was out of sequence. Keep this CCS log separate from your multiple-choice error log.
6. **Practice under the real time limit.** Once the mechanics are comfortable, run cases against the clock so that pacing is not a surprise.
7. **Fit it into a plan.** Interleave CCS with multiple-choice blocks and a self-assessment near the end. The [Step 3 study plan](/wiki/exams/step-3-study-schedule) and the [NBME self-assessment guide](/wiki/exams/nbme-self-assessments) show how.

### A repeatable workflow (opinion)

| Stage | Questions to ask yourself |
|---|---|
| 1. Stabilize and place | Is the patient unstable? What needs to happen right now, and is this the right location (home, office, emergency department, inpatient unit, ICU)? |
| 2. Gather focused data | Which history and examination components and which first tests will change management? |
| 3. Treat what is indicated | What does this patient need now, ordered by exact drug name and route? |
| 4. Advance deliberately | Which result am I waiting for, and how far should I move the clock to see it? |
| 5. Reassess | What do the results change? Do I stop, add or change an order, or move the patient? |
| 6. Close the loop | Before the case-end screen: is follow-up scheduled, is counseling or education ordered where relevant, and is the location right for what comes next? |

This is a thinking aid, not an algorithm. If a stage does not apply to a case, skip it, and do not add orders just because the table has a row.

## Common CCS mistakes

| Mistake | Why it costs you | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Ordering a long list "just in case" | USMLE says unnecessary or excessive orders can lower the score | Order what changes management for this patient |
| Right orders in the wrong order | USMLE says correct decisions in an incorrect sequence may earn no credit | Treat the urgent problem first, then work up |
| Placing orders but not advancing the clock | Orders are carried out and results appear only as time advances | Advance deliberately, then read the results |
| Advancing too far, too fast | You cannot go back, and the patient may change before you act | Move the clock to the next result you need |
| Forgetting follow-up or location before the case ends | You cannot schedule follow-up or change location after the case-end screen | Close the loop before you finish |
| Using a drug class or loose phrasing | Drugs must be requested by name | Practice typing generic names |
| Expecting someone else to order monitoring | Other team members do not write your orders | Order monitoring yourself |
| Reading an early case end as failure | USMLE says an early end does not mean you did something right or wrong | Finish the case calmly and move on |
| Never practicing the interface | USMLE warns examinees who skip practice may be at a disadvantage | Do the official practice early |

> [!IMG TIP] If you trained somewhere that routinely orders broad panels of tests, practice restraint on purpose: in CCS, an order that does not help the patient can cost you. If your training followed different guidelines from US practice, check the US approach to common presentations while you practice. This is our opinion, not a USMLE statement.

## Day-before checklist

Opinion and practice, built around the official exam-day rules in the [exam day guide](/wiki/exams/exam-day-rules-and-checklist).

- Permit and ID are packed, the name on the permit matches the ID, and you know the route and arrival time for Day 2.
- You have done your last CCS practice and are not starting new resources. A day or two before the exam, a light review of your CCS log beats cramming new cases.
- Your workflow is memorized. You cannot bring notes into the room, and the test center provides the writing materials, so check the current rules before you plan to write anything down.
- You have decided in advance whether to use the optional CCS tutorial. Time you do not use on it can be added to your break time under USMLE's rules, as described in the exam day guide.
- You have planned your breaks, food and water for the longest day of the exam.
- You have a plan for sleep and a calm evening. A tired mind misses the follow-up order.
- You know Day 1 and Day 2 are separate sessions, and you will not judge how Day 2 is going from how Day 1 felt.

> [!WARNING] USMLE exam content is confidential. Do not copy, record or post cases from your exam, and do not ask others for them. Using real exam cases is a violation of the Rules of Conduct, which can lead to cancelled scores and a permanent annotation on your record.
