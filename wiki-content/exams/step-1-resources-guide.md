The best Step 1 resource is the one that does a specific job for you and that you actually finish. Rather than ranking brands, this page gives you a way to evaluate any resource by its purpose, then an order for using them. We do not name a "best" product and we do not list prices.

## Why pass/fail changes resource strategy

Step 1 has been reported as pass/fail since {{fact:step1_pass_fail_since}}. You still need a margin of safety, because you cannot retake a passed Step and a fail counts toward your attempt limit of {{fact:usmle_attempts_per_step}}. But there is no reward for squeezing out extra points on Step 1.

What that means in practice (opinion, based on how the exam is structured):

- Spend more time on weak systems than on polishing strong ones.
- Prioritize timed accuracy and stamina over memorizing rare facts.
- Stop adding resources once your practice results are comfortably stable.
- Save energy for Step 2 CK, which still reports a three-digit score. See the [Step 1 guide](/wiki/exams/step-1) for why.

## Start with the official materials

USMLE publishes the content outline, a description of question formats, and an interactive test that shows the software. A new interactive test matching the format from {{fact:usmle_step1_new_format_date}} is available on the USMLE site. Use the official outline as the scope of your studying, not a third-party summary, and use the interactive test to learn the interface so test day has fewer surprises.

> [!CHANGED] Step 1 uses shorter {{fact:usmle_block_minutes}}-minute blocks for exams on or after {{fact:usmle_step1_new_format_date}}, according to USMLE. Check that any pacing advice or practice tool you use matches the current format. See the [policy tracker](/wiki/exams/usmle-policy-changes-tracker).

## Resource types by purpose

| Purpose | What it is for | How to evaluate it | Common trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question bank | Active recall, exam-style reasoning, finding gaps | Realistic vignettes, clear explanations, performance tracking, current content, timed mode | Doing blocks without reviewing explanations |
| Reference text or notes | Looking things up and consolidating | Accuracy, alignment with the official outline, readability | Reading it cover to cover as the main method |
| Video lectures | Teaching a topic you do not understand | Clear explanations at the depth you need, speed control, time cost | Watching passively for weeks |
| Flashcards | Memorizing facts and pharmacology | Fits your weak areas, scheduling of repetition, effort to maintain | Over-making cards instead of learning |
| Self-assessment | Measuring readiness | Official or well-validated, scores you can compare over time | Using it too often or reading one score as destiny |

## A sensible order

1. **Scope and baseline.** Read the official outline and take one self-assessment early to see where you stand. NBME offers a Comprehensive Basic Science Self-Assessment for Step 1; see the [self-assessments guide](/wiki/exams/nbme-self-assessments).
2. **Fix understanding gaps.** Use video or a reference for topics you cannot yet reason through. Keep this phase short and targeted.
3. **Make questions your main study method.** Choose one question bank and work through it in mixed, timed blocks. The [question bank comparison](/wiki/exams/question-banks-compared) explains how to choose.
4. **Add recall tools where they help.** Flashcards are best for stable facts, such as drugs and microbiology, that questions keep exposing.
5. **Reassess on a schedule.** Repeat self-assessments every few weeks, not daily, and use trends to decide whether to book or move your exam date.
6. **Run full-length simulations near the end.** Practice the day structure and pacing for the {{fact:usmle_block_minutes}}-minute block format.

For an actual calendar, see the [Step 1 study schedule](/wiki/exams/step-1-study-schedule) and [how long to study](/wiki/exams/how-long-to-study).

## How to judge a resource quickly

Ask five questions before you commit:

1. Does it map to the current official content outline?
2. Is it updated for the current exam format?
3. Does it explain wrong answers, not just give the right one?
4. Can I see my performance by topic?
5. Will I realistically finish it before my exam date?

If the answer to the last question is no, choose something smaller. Opinion: finishing one question bank with careful review usually serves you better than starting three.

## IMG-specific considerations

- **Different curricula.** If your school's basic sciences were taught differently or some time ago, spend more of your early time on foundation topics, and use a baseline self-assessment to decide how much.
- **Language and pace.** If English is not your first language, practice reading long clinical vignettes under time pressure, because speed in reading is a skill separate from medical knowledge.
- **Access and cost.** Resources vary in price and region availability. Compare current offers on each provider's site and check what free or institutional access you have before you buy.
- **Test center distance.** If your center is far away, plan a rehearsal visit or practice session. USMLE notes you can register at Prometric for a practice session to get familiar with a test center.

## What to avoid

> [!WARNING] Do not use any service that claims to provide actual USMLE questions or recalls. USMLE says using prep services that distribute actual exam content can lead to canceled registration or scores and further sanctions.

Beyond that, avoid "secret" resources promoted only through social media, unrealistic score claims, and resource-hopping when a plateau appears. Plateaus usually call for reviewing errors, not a new subscription.

## Putting it together

A lean setup for most candidates is: the official outline, one baseline and periodic self-assessments, one question bank used as the main method, one reference or video source for gaps, and optional flashcards. If your practice results are comfortably above the level you need and stable over several assessments, extra resources are unlikely to add much. If they are not, change how you study before you change what you buy.
