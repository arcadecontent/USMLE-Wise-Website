USMLE Step 1 is the first of the three exams you must pass to become eligible for ECFMG certification and US residency. It tests whether you can apply the basic sciences to medicine. Since {{fact:step1_pass_fail_since}} it has been reported as **pass or fail** only, with no three-digit score.

## Why Step 1 still matters when it is pass/fail

Pass/fail does not make the exam easier or less important. It changes what the result tells programs.

- **It is a gate.** No pass, no Step 3, no ECFMG certification, and programs expect to see a pass on your application.
- **A fail is visible.** A failed attempt appears on your transcript, and program directors read it as a risk signal.
- **Less differentiation, more weight on everything else.** With no score to compare, programs use Step 2 CK, US clinical experience, letters, research and the rest of your application to separate candidates.

> [!IMG TIP] Plan for Step 1 as a "must pass comfortably" exam, then put your extra effort into Step 2 CK, where a strong score can still help you.

## What the exam covers

Step 1 assesses principles of basic science that are important in medicine, with emphasis on concepts and mechanisms behind health, disease and treatment. The official content outline lists the disciplines and organ systems tested and their approximate weighting, so use it as your checklist rather than a third-party summary.

Questions are clinical vignettes. You are rarely asked for an isolated fact; you are asked to recognize a presentation and reason about the underlying mechanism, the test or the treatment.

## Exam format

Step 1 is a single-day, computer-based exam made up of multiple-choice questions divided into timed blocks, with scheduled breaks. The number of blocks, questions per block and total testing time are set by USMLE and can change, so confirm them in the current Bulletin and the official sample items before you plan your stamina.

## Step 1 eligibility for IMGs

> [!CHANGED] In January 2026, USMLE services for IMGs moved from ECFMG's MyIntealth portal to FSMB. Older guides and videos may describe the previous process, so follow the current instructions on usmle.org.

IMGs do not apply to NBME. As of the 2026 transition, the general sequence is:

1. Confirm your medical school and graduation year are eligible under ECFMG requirements. ECFMG still decides eligibility for ECFMG certification.
2. Apply for Step 1 through the FSMB website, as directed by the current USMLE Bulletin.
3. Your eligibility is confirmed and you receive a permit for a limited eligibility period.
4. You schedule your test date with Prometric within that period.

> [!WARNING] The eligibility period is limited and has a fixed window. Do not schedule your study plan around a date you have not confirmed is inside your window, and do not assume you can extend it freely.

## Registering and scheduling

- **Fee:** the examination fee changes over time and may include extra charges depending on your test region: {{fact:step_exam_fee}}.
- **Where:** Prometric test centers operate in the United States and in many other countries. Seats can be scarce in popular locations and months, so check availability before you apply.
- **When:** schedule as soon as your permit is issued if the dates you want are limited.
- **Travel:** if you must travel to test, read the [B-1/B-2 visitor visa guide](/wiki/visas/b1-b2-for-interviews-and-exams) early, because a visitor visa application can take longer than the exam registration.

## Scoring, results and attempts

Step 1 is reported pass/fail. Passing requires meeting a minimum performance standard that USMLE sets and reviews: {{fact:step1_passing_standard}}.

You are limited in how many times you can attempt each Step. The current limit is {{fact:usmle_attempts_per_step}} attempts per Step, and waiting periods apply between attempts. Read the Bulletin's rules on what counts as an attempt before you register.

> [!WARNING] Every attempt counts toward the limit and appears on your record. If you are unsure you are ready, moving the date is usually less costly than a failed attempt.

## How to build a study plan

There is no single correct plan, but strong plans share the same structure:

1. **Baseline first.** Take a reputable self-assessment early so you know where you stand instead of guessing.
2. **One core resource per subject, plus a question bank.** Switching resources constantly is the most common time sink. Most candidates anchor on one question bank and one reference for review.
3. **Questions as the main study method.** Do timed, mixed blocks, then review every explanation, including the ones you got right.
4. **Track error patterns.** Group wrong answers by cause (knowledge gap, misread question, time pressure) and fix the cause.
5. **Reassess on a schedule.** Use self-assessments every few weeks to decide whether to continue, adjust or move the exam date.
6. **Protect the last two weeks.** Finish new content early, then simulate full exam days.

### A realistic timeline

| Phase | Focus |
|---|---|
| Foundation | Rebuild weak basic sciences and learn how to do questions |
| Core | Mixed question blocks, systematic review of errors |
| Integration | Timed full-length practice, weak-area targeting |
| Final stretch | Full exam simulations, rest, logistics |

Adjust the length of each phase to your baseline. If you graduated some time ago or your school's curriculum differed, give the foundation phase more room.

## Common mistakes IMGs make

- Booking the exam before verifying the eligibility window and test-center availability.
- Treating pass/fail as permission to coast, then discovering the margin was thin.
- Using too many resources and finishing none.
- Skipping timed, full-length practice until the last week.
- Ignoring stamina: a full exam day is a long sitting.

## What comes next

After Step 1 you move on to Step 2 CK, then Step 3, alongside ECFMG certification requirements (see [when to take each Step](/wiki/exams/when-to-take-each-step)). If you do not pass, read [what to do after failing a Step](/wiki/exams/what-to-do-after-failing-a-step). Your Step 2 CK performance and your US clinical experience will do most of the work of setting you apart. See the [ERAS timeline](/wiki/application/eras-timeline) to understand how your exam dates fit into an application season.
